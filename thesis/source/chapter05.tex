\chapter{Discussion}\label{ch:discussion}%

This chapter interprets the results of Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments}. Section~\ref{sec:disc-ablations} answers the three research questions of Section~\ref{sec:research-questions} as far as the available results allow. Section~\ref{sec:disc-assembly} returns to the assembly motivation and asks how far the demonstrated skill is from industrial threading. Section~\ref{sec:disc-limitations} states the limitations of the work.

\section{Interpretation per Research Question}\label{sec:disc-ablations}

All three research questions presuppose a teacher that produces complete multi-cycle demonstrations, so the teacher result comes first. \ac{PPO} learns the threading turn once the thread is constrained kinematically and the learned task is one turn from an engaged nut. The kinematic constraint ties axial progress to rotation, so the policy earns the progress reward only by turning. With the hand restricted to three commanded pinch degrees of freedom (Section~\ref{sec:teacher}), the teacher acquires the pinch grasp and keeps it under the turning load. Losing the grasp after it has latched ends the episode, so grasp retention is a hard constraint (Section~\ref{sec:teacher}). Trained under initial-yaw, axial-height, and controller randomization, the retained teacher, chained into the hybrid teacher, produces the successful ten-cycle demonstrations of the student dataset (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}). The \ac{RL} teacher solves one turn, and the hybrid rollout extends it to ten consecutive cycles. The actor of the teacher and the student observe the two fingertip forces and the seven arm joint torques, which have counterparts on the physical system (Section~\ref{sec:teacher}). No experiment of this thesis isolates their contribution.

RQ1 asks whether one student distilled from multi-cycle demonstrations sustains consecutive cycles, and it tests the deployment side of the long-horizon difficulty of Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-horizon}. The full method completes all ten cycles in every simulated trial and lags the target by less than two degrees per cycle. The single-cycle ablation (a), which a state machine invokes once per cycle, completes $5.5\pm0.8$ cycles in simulation and $5.2\pm1.2$ cycles on hardware, and none of its trials reaches ten cycles (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). On hardware, its fingers typically slip off the edge of the nut after a few cycles. The result thus favors training one policy on the complete cyclic behavior over invoking a per-cycle policy repeatedly.

RQ2 asks which student design makes the unification work. It has two parts, the visual representation and the action head, and each of the ablations (b) to (d) changes one of them. On the representation side, sub-millimeter localization of the nut is unnecessary, because the engagement of the nut on the bolt and the compliance of the impedance controller absorb lateral error. The student needs the orientation of the nut faces for the re-grasp and the height of the nut for the approach. A representation that encodes position but not orientation would let the fingers close on the right spot with the wrong face alignment. The point-cloud encoder consumes the metric geometry of nut, bolt, and hand directly, with about 60\,000 parameters and no pretrained image backbone. The pose probe of Section~\ref{sec:ablations} tests whether the feature of each encoder carries the face orientation. The image-space encoder of ablation (b) also receives depth and per-pixel coordinates, so (b) tests whether the geometric structure of the representation, rather than the depth input alone, decides the closed-loop result. Random point sampling adds an augmentation that the image encoder does not receive. The closed-loop ablation (b) and the pose probe quantify the difference. In closed loop, the ResNet student completes $2.3\pm0.9$ cycles in simulation and $1.1\pm0.9$ cycles on hardware, and it overshoots the target by $+20.17\degree$ and $+24.09\degree$ per cycle (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The two features locate the nut with similar position errors of 8\,mm and 8.6\,mm, but the orientation error of the ResNet feature, $31.20\degree$, is about twice that of the point-cloud feature, $15.16\degree$. The image-space representation thus keeps the nut position and loses much of the face orientation that the re-grasp needs.

On the head side, the chunked Transformer regression head (d) shares the backbone, the chunk length, and the temporal ensembling of the flow student, so the difference between (d) and (e) isolates the generative objective. Two mechanisms could favor the flow head, the multimodality of the labels and the structure of the prediction. For near-identical observations, the dataset contains the teacher's nominal action in one demonstration and its recovery after an impulse in another. A squared-error head converges to their average, an action that neither demonstration contains, whereas the flow head samples one of them. The flow head also predicts a velocity from a noisy chunk toward the data, a denoising problem that may be easier to fit than a direct regression from observation to chunk. Through the sixteen integration steps it refines all eight steps of the chunk jointly, with full attention among them. The recurrent regression head (c) additionally removes chunking. The \ac{LSTM} student (c) diverges before it completes the first cycle, and the Transformer regression student (d) completes $1.4\pm0.8$ cycles with an overshoot of $+124.21\pm18.50\degree$ per cycle (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Because (d) differs from (e) only in the objective, the generative objective accounts for the difference between this failure and the full method. The experiments do not show which of the two mechanisms dominates.

RQ3 asks whether a student trained on simulated data only transfers zero-shot to the physical system. It tests the transfer difficulty of Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-transfer} together with the contact difficulty of Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-contact}, because the simplified thread model differs from the physical thread. The transfer rests on four properties of the pipeline. The calibrated camera model is meant to make the simulated and real point clouds geometrically comparable. The crop box removes the background, which is where rendered and real images differ most. Lighting, texture, and background randomization are meant to cover the remaining appearance variation of the points that enter the encoder. The shared Cartesian action path executes the student's commands through a controller that reproduces the simulated control law, and the teacher was trained under randomized gains and noisy pose inputs. Inside the crop box, the point geometry is identical in both domains up to depth noise and calibration error, whereas an image-space encoder faces the appearance gap of the full image. The per-point colors still carry the appearance gap of nut, bolt, and hand. The approach requires no real-world data collection and no training on the robot. On hardware, the full method completes all ten cycles in every trial on the training nut, with a cyclic error of $-4.21\pm1.20\degree$ against $-1.77\pm1.02\degree$ in simulation (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The transfer thus preserves task success and the spread of the error, and it increases the lag by about $2.4\degree$ per cycle. The student also completes all ten cycles in every trial on the M30 and M36 hexagonal nuts, whose sizes the demonstrations do not contain, with cyclic errors of $-3.70\pm0.77\degree$ and $-5.52\pm1.34\degree$ (Table~\ref{tab:geometry}). The hardware ablations agree with the geometric argument: the gap between the ResNet student and the point-cloud student widens from simulation to hardware.
\section{What This Means for Robotic Assembly}\label{sec:disc-assembly}

The motivation of this thesis was assembly with threaded joints (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}). Measured against an industrial fastening step, the demonstrated skill covers the run-down of an engaged nut and leaves gaps at the start of the task, at its end, in the grasp, and in cycle time.

Every trial starts with the nut already engaged on the bolt (Section~\ref{sec:thread-model}). In assembly the nut arrives loose and has to be seated on the thread first, which is a separate skill with a narrow entry configuration.

A trial ends after ten turns of $60\degree$, which advance the nut by one and two thirds of a pitch, 5.0\,mm on the M24 nut. The nut is never seated against a part, and it is never tightened to a preload.

The simulated thread resists the turn only with a smoothed friction torque whose Coulomb term is $2\times10^{-3}$\,N\,m, which models a free-running nut, and the reward penalizes a large net pad force and the wrench that the thread transmits to the nut. A tightening phase would demand that the pinch transmit a much larger torque and that the policy sense when the joint is tight, which the thread model does not represent.

The student executes one grasp strategy, a thumb--index pinch with three commanded hand joints, learned on the face topology of a hexagon. A tightening phase, in which the resistance grows, would likely require a change of grasp. The held-out octagonal, rectangular, and round profiles of Section~\ref{sec:geometry} test how far the single strategy generalizes. On the octagonal and round profiles, the student completes all ten cycles in every trial at all three sizes, and the round nut shows the smallest lag at every size. On the rectangular profile, it completes $3.0\pm1.2$ cycles on the M24 nut and none on the M30 and M36 nuts (Table~\ref{tab:geometry}). In the typical failure, the fingers turn the nut by $60\degree$ but do not establish contact with the next pair of faces in the following cycle.

In the demonstrations, release and return take a nearly fixed time in every cycle, since the four scripted waypoints and the final return are interpolated over fixed durations of 0.53 to 1.07\,s each, 3.9\,s in total (Table~\ref{tab:hybrid-params}). Because each cycle turns the nut by $60\degree$, a full revolution takes six cycles, which is far slower than the continuous rotation of a powered spindle.

Within these limits, a learned policy controlled a dexterous hand in simulation through ten cycles of grasp, turn, release, and return, and it re-grasped the nut at the angle and height at which the previous cycle left it. It perceived the nut from one fixed camera, and it was trained without any real data. Whether this versatility outweighs the speed of a dedicated tool depends on the application. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} turns the remaining gaps into future work.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:disc-limitations}

Beyond the task-level gaps of Section~\ref{sec:disc-assembly}, the method and the evidence have the following limitations. The demonstrated behavior is a pinch-grasp re-grasp strategy, and the student has no mechanism to discover a different grasp for a profile that requires one. The demonstrations start at the nominal nut height and cover only the 5.0\,mm of axial travel of ten cycles, and the global progress clock is defined over the nominal trial length. The teacher's thread model is a kinematic simplification. In simulation the nut therefore cannot jam or cross-thread, and the thread resists only through a small friction torque on the revolute joint. The randomization ranges that are meant to cover the resulting sim-to-real gap have not yet been fitted to measured statistics of the physical sensor. The camera calibration has a translation fit \acs{RMSE} of 9.7\,mm and a rotation fit \acs{RMSE} of $0.98\degree$ (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). The dependence of the student on calibration error has not been quantified. The evaluation uses ten trials per condition and reports means and standard deviations without confidence intervals. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} summarizes what the thesis achieved and turns these gaps and limitations into future work.
