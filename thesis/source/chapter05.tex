\chapter{Experiments on Real Hardware}\label{ch:real}%

Chapter~\ref{ch:sim} showed that the distilled student sustains consecutive threading cycles in simulation. This chapter asks whether that behavior survives the gap between the simplified simulation and the physical workcell, which is RQ3 of Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}: can a student trained on simulated demonstrations only transfer zero-shot to the physical system? No real-world data enters training, and nothing is trained on the robot. Section~\ref{sec:real-setup} introduces the physical system, including the robot, the camera and its calibration, the controller, the measurement of the nut angle, and the test objects. Section~\ref{sec:real-transfer} describes the zero-shot transfer protocol. Section~\ref{sec:real-results} reports the closed-loop results on the M24 training nut, Section~\ref{sec:real-geometry} on unseen nut geometries, and Section~\ref{sec:real-stress} under long-horizon stress.

\section{Hardware Setup}\label{sec:real-setup}

The physical workcell, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:real-setup}, realizes the generic platform of Section~\ref{sec:platform} with specific products. The arm is a seven-\acs{DoF} \ac{FR3}. An Inspire RH56DFX right hand is mounted on its flange through a 10\,mm adapter. The kinematic structure of the hand, the three commanded pinch degrees of freedom, and the bench geometry are those of Section~\ref{sec:platform}.

The simulated system is a model of this workcell. The transform of the adapter flange was measured and reproduced in the simulated asset. The hand asset was regenerated from the manufacturer's URDF, so that joint limits, link inertias, and the mechanical coupling of the finger joints match the physical hand. The \ac{FR3} is the arm that replaced the earlier humanoid-arm platform of Section~\ref{sec:platform}.

A fixed Intel RealSense D415 on a stand provides the \acs{RGBD} observation at $640\times480$. As in simulation, the stream is center-cropped and downscaled to the $320\times180$ policy input (Figure~\ref{fig:real-rgbd}). The pose of the camera relative to the robot base was measured by an eye-to-hand calibration, with a translation fit \acs{RMSE} of 27\,mm and a rotation fit \acs{RMSE} of $3.5\degree$ over the calibration poses. The calibrated optical pose and the intrinsics are the ones used to render the simulated camera and to back-project the point cloud, so the student sees the same geometry in both domains.
% TODO(unsure): the calibration artifact is labeled "measured", not "deployment validated"; confirm whether a refined calibration was used for the reported hardware trials.

Robot commands use the same Cartesian impedance law as in simulation, so the student's bounded action reaches the arm through the shared action path of Section~\ref{sec:sim2real}. The deployment controller is derived from the libfranka Cartesian-impedance example controller and modified to reproduce \eqref{eq:osc} exactly, including the axis-angle orientation error and the dynamically consistent nullspace term; the modifications are described below. Gravity is compensated by libfranka, which matches the simulation, where gravity is disabled for the arm. The controller runs at the 1\,kHz rate of the Franka control interface, while the student commands targets at 15\,Hz. The hand is position-controlled through its serial interface. The task-space gains equal those of the simulation, 565\,N/m and 28\,N\,m/rad with critical damping, and the nullspace stiffness is 10. The mapping of the student's bounded action to the controller target is \tbd.

\paragraph{Controller families.}
The unmodified libfranka example differs from the Factory controller of \eqref{eq:osc} in three places. It uses a damped kinematic nullspace projector built from a pseudoinverse of the Jacobian transpose with regularization 0.2, it feeds the rotational spring the vector part of the error quaternion instead of the rotation vector, and it adds the robot's Coriolis term. Near the threading posture, the damped projector lets the posture spring push 12.5\,N on the tool at a 1\,rad wrist excursion, comparable to the 11.3\,N that the policy commands for a 20\,mm position step, whereas the exact projector reduces this to 0.4\,N. The quaternion vector halves the effective rotational stiffness from 28 to 14\,N\,m/rad. The deployment controller therefore replaces all three: it uses the dynamically consistent projector with the arm mass matrix from the robot model, with the hand configured as end-effector load, the rotation-vector error, and no Coriolis term, because the simulated arm is not compensated either. Both backends are implemented in the simulation with selectable gains, so a teacher can be trained under either. The reported teachers use the Factory controller, and the physical controller reproduces it rather than the other way around. The student's commands therefore meet the stiffness under which the teacher acquired its contact behavior.

\paragraph{Trajectory export.}
For open-loop validation of the action path, every simulated demonstration can be exported as a command-only deployment artifact. The simulator recording is authoritative for the geometric joint path. Some scripted phases, however, change the joint position kinematically while writing zero articulation velocity, so the export does not use recorded velocities. It fits one twice continuously differentiable quintic curve through the recorded positions and derives position, velocity, and acceleration from it, with deployment limits on joint positions and velocities applied explicitly. Rows with a valid controller command also retain the recorded reverse-engineered 17-D impedance command of Section~\ref{sec:action-contract}, so a joint-space replay is possible on controllers that do not expose a Cartesian interface. On the \acs{FR3} the Cartesian targets are executed directly through the libfranka-style controller, which keeps open-loop playback and student execution on the same path.

\paragraph{Angle measurement and test objects.}
The metrics of Section~\ref{sec:metrics} require the nut angle. It is read from an angular scale printed on a sheet under the bolt pedestal (Figure~\ref{fig:real-phases}), and the unwrapped angle is recorded at the end of every goal-reaching phase. The test-object family, shown on the right of Figure~\ref{fig:real-setup}, consists of M24, M30, and M36 nuts in four profiles each, hexagonal, octagonal, rectangular, and round, with matching bolts and holders. Teacher and student training use the hexagonal profiles only. The other three profiles are held out for the generalization study of Section~\ref{sec:real-geometry}.

\section{Zero-Shot Transfer Protocol}\label{sec:real-transfer}

The protocol tests RQ3 directly: the student is trained on simulated data only and deployed unchanged. The student of the full method is trained for 300 epochs on the simulated dataset $\Dset=\Dsim$ of Section~\ref{sec:dataset} with eight-step chunks. It is deployed on the physical robot with a prediction horizon of eight and sixteen \ac{ODE} integration steps. No real-world images, trajectories, or corrections enter its training, and it is not fine-tuned on the robot. The transfer therefore rests entirely on the four properties of Section~\ref{sec:sim2real}. The calibrated camera of Section~\ref{sec:real-setup} is the one that rendered the demonstrations. The point-cloud crop box removes the physical background before encoding. The demonstrations were rendered under lighting, texture, and background randomization. The Cartesian targets are executed by the impedance controller described above. The student receives the progress scalar from the elapsed trial time and the process phase from the physical events of the cycle, as in simulation (Section~\ref{sec:student}).

\section{Long-Horizon Stress}\label{sec:real-stress}

Beyond the ten-cycle protocol, the student is run on hardware for as many cycles as it sustains, to measure how far its horizon extends beyond the ten cycles of the demonstrations. Every revolution raises the nut by one pitch. After enough cycles, the nut therefore leaves the axial range that the demonstrations cover, since the teacher that generated them was trained with up to 20\,mm of initial-height randomization. The stress trials thus measure how far the learned behavior reaches along the bolt. The cycle counts per nut are \tbd.
