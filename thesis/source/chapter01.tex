\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}%

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

Threaded fasteners such as a nut on a bolt join parts so that the joint can be released again, which makes them the standard choice wherever two or more parts must be connected and later maintained, adjusted, or taken apart~\cite{Budynas2020,Niemann2019}. They appear in almost every industry. For example, the halves of a cast engine housing are bolted together, airplane seats are mounted with threaded bolts, and the injector of a rocket engine is bolted onto the combustion chamber. Valves and pipes are screwed together with a seal, in building plumbing as well as in the cooling loop of a computer.
% TODO(cite): threaded joints in engine housings, aircraft seats, rocket engine injectors, and valve and pipe connections.
In all of these applications, the threaded joint lets the product be built from separately manufactured parts and opened again without damage for inspection, maintenance, and repair.

In industrial production, dedicated machines drive the fasteners. A nutrunner drives one part of the fastener, the nut or the screw, with a powered spindle, a multi-spindle station drives several fasteners of one part at once, and a robot with a screwdriving end effector feeds and drives them automatically.
% TODO(cite): industrial threading automation (nutrunners, multi-spindle stations, robotic screwdriving end effectors).
These machines do not need to locate the hole, because a fixture clamps the part in a known pose. The screw or nut arrives from a feeder in a known orientation, and the tool is matched to one fastener type and size.
% TODO(cite): dependence of automated fastening on fixtures, fixed part poses, and fastener feeding.
This setup is economical in high-volume production. It is not available where parts vary, where the cell is not built around one product, or where batches are small, and it is not available in maintenance and disassembly, where the pose of the part is not fixed in advance.
% TODO(cite): limits of dedicated fastening automation for product variants, small batches, maintenance, and disassembly.

People handle the cases that dedicated machines cannot. Without a fixture or a feeder, a machine must locate the bolt, align the nut, and engage the thread. A person does this without deliberate planning, picking up the nut, placing it on the bolt, and turning it with the fingers. If the thread does not catch, the person backs off, straightens the nut, and turns again. This is an example of Moravec's paradox, which describes that it is comparatively easy to make computers perform well on tasks that people find intellectually demanding, and difficult to give them the perception and mobility skills of a small child~\cite{Moravec1988}.

A robot with a dexterous, multi-fingered hand could in principle do the same. It could work with the parts and tools that were designed for people, in workcells that were designed for people, and without a dedicated tool for every fastener~\cite{BicchiKumar2000,Weinberg2024}. Learning-based control has made in-hand manipulation with such hands practical in simulation and on hardware~\cite{Chen2021,Handa2024,Weinberg2024}. The motivation of this thesis is a dexterous robot that assists in manufacturing and assembly in environments built for humans, and threading a nut by hand is one of the basic skills such a robot needs.

The same hand also makes the skill challenging to learn. Together with the arm, it has many actuated joints, and the solution of a task is therefore not unique, because a human-like hand can achieve the same task in many different ways. A hand can hold a small object such as a nut with many grasp types, from a pinch between thumb and index finger to grasps that involve several fingers, as the taxonomy of human grasps shows~\cite{Feix2016}. A policy that may choose among all of these grasps must explore a correspondingly larger space of finger motions, and its reward must steer it toward one grasp that turns the nut reliably. Beyond the control of the hand, learning the skill in simulation and executing it on hardware poses three further difficulties, which the next section describes.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem-setup}

Learning to thread a nut in simulation and executing the skill on hardware combines three difficulties. Engaged threads form a contact-rich interaction that is expensive to simulate, limiting parallel \ac{RL} training. The task repeats over many cycles, so that errors compound over a long horizon. Finally, the learned skill must cross the gap between simulation and hardware. The following subsections state each difficulty, the reason it arises, and the requirement it places on the method.

\subsection{Simulating Thread Contact at Scale}\label{sec:difficulty-contact}

Learning a contact-rich skill directly on hardware is slow and unsafe. \Ac{RL} requires many trials, every trial costs robot time and wear, and exploration produces collisions and excessive forces~\cite{Kober2013}. Simulation removes these limits. GPU-parallel simulators run thousands of environments at once and have made \ac{RL} feasible for dexterous manipulation and for contact-rich assembly~\cite{Handa2024,Petrenko2023,Narang2022}.

Faithful simulation of thread contact is expensive. A geometry-faithful simulation represents the nut and the bolt by high-resolution meshes with many vertices, and the collision pipeline must compute the contact pairs between them at every physics step~\cite{Narang2022}. Engaged threads produce thousands of simultaneous contacts, and their number grows with the number of engaged thread turns. This cost is paid again in every parallel environment, at every physics step, and for each of the millions of samples that on-policy \ac{RL} consumes.

Besides its cost, mesh-based thread contact admits a shortcut that a policy can exploit. PhysX, the physics engine of the simulator used in this thesis, resolves contact between mesh colliders by letting bodies overlap slightly and then pushing them apart again (Section~\ref{sec:thread-model}). This overlap is harmless for large bodies but not for threads, whose pitch is only 3 to 4\,mm for the nuts used in this thesis. A nut that is pressed along the bolt can therefore slip through the threads into the next groove instead of following the helix. An \ac{RL} policy can exploit this. If the reward pays for axial progress of the nut, the policy collects it by pushing the nut through the threads instead of turning it. The resulting behavior solves the simulated task and fails on hardware, where the threads are rigid and the nut advances only by rotating. A thread model for learning this skill therefore must be cheap enough for massively parallel \ac{RL}, and it must make the axial progress of the nut follow from its rotation rather than from the resolution of mesh contact.

\subsection{Compounding Error in Long-Horizon Cyclic Tasks}\label{sec:difficulty-horizon}

A long-horizon cyclic task repeats a fixed sequence of phases many times while the task state advances from one repetition to the next. Threading a nut with a dexterous hand is such a task. Because the range of the wrist and the fingers limits each turn, the hand must approach the nut, grasp it, turn it, release it, and return for the next grasp, cycle after cycle.

Three properties make long-horizon cyclic tasks challenging. First, errors compound across cycles. A grasp that places the fingertips slightly away from their intended contact points on the nut, a release that disturbs the nut, or a return that brings the fingers back at the wrong height carries over into the next cycle and accumulates. A learned policy amplifies this effect, because small deviations lead it to states it was not trained on, where its errors grow further~\cite{Ross2011}. Second, failures concentrate at the transitions between phases, where the fingers establish or break contact with the nut. Third, a single \ac{RL} policy for the whole task requires a reward that covers every phase and every transition, and it must assign the outcome of a late cycle to decisions made many steps earlier. Both requirements grow with the horizon. Every shaped phase adds reward terms that the policy may exploit (Section~\ref{sec:rl-background}), and the discounted return weights an outcome less the further it lies after the decision that caused it~\cite{Sutton2018,Kober2013}. For dexterous hands, this compositional structure adds to the difficulty of the high-dimensional action space~\cite{Chen2023}. A sparse binary task reward has trained a single policy on long assembly sequences in simulation~\cite{Zhang2026}, at the cost of \ac{RL} at large scale (Section~\ref{sec:related-chaining}).

The common answer is to decompose the task into skills, train one policy per skill, and chain the policies at deployment~\cite{Chen2023}. In a chain, the final state of one policy must lie in the region where the next policy succeeds. This requires additional mechanisms that fine-tune the sub-policies for their neighbors~\cite{Chen2023,Lee2022}, and deployment requires several policies together with a high-level controller that decides when to hand over from one policy to the next. A second answer is to imitate complete long-horizon demonstrations with one generative policy~\cite{Chi2024,Black2026}. The policy is generative because demonstrations of a long task are multimodal. Near-identical observations can be followed by different actions, and a deterministic regression of the actions averages them into an action that none of the demonstrations took~\cite{Chi2024}. Imitation avoids the reward problem, but it requires demonstrations of the whole task, and teleoperated demonstrations of forceful dexterous manipulation are difficult to collect~\cite{Kedia2026}. The method should therefore combine the strengths of both answers. Its training signal should be as easy to specify phase by phase as in a decomposition, and it should deploy one policy that sustains many cycles without switching between separate policies. The action head of this policy must represent multimodal demonstrations.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer for Visuomotor Policies}\label{sec:difficulty-transfer}

A skill learned in simulation must transfer to hardware. The policy acts on the physical system with the same commands that it learned in simulation. The simulation must therefore reproduce the physical system wherever the outcome of a command depends on it.

This begins with the robot asset. The inertial parameters, joint limits, and joint dynamics of the arm decide how the arm responds to a commanded torque. In the hand, the finger couplings, the joint and effort limits, and the pad friction decide where the fingertips land, how strongly they press, and whether a grasp holds under the turning load. The fingertip forces that the policy observes must also match the forces that the physical sensors report. A mismatch in any of these elements changes the outcome of an action, and a policy that relies on the simulated outcome fails on hardware.
The thread is the part of the system that is not measured on hardware. Its simulation must be fast enough for massively parallel \ac{RL} and accurate enough in kinematics and dynamics that the simulated nut responds to the fingers like the physical one, advancing along the helix as it turns and resisting the turn. Because the thread dynamics of the physical nut are unknown, the thread interaction must instead be randomized widely, from the friction that resists the turn to the friction between the nut, the bolt, and the finger pads.

Even the measured parts of the system are only approximated, because every simulator simplifies contact, friction, and actuator dynamics, and the physical parameters themselves carry uncertainty. No simulation reproduces the physical system exactly. Zero-shot transfer therefore requires a simulation that approximates the physical system closely enough, together with a policy that is robust to the error that remains. Domain randomization provides this robustness. It varies the parameters of the simulation across training episodes, so that the policy learns to be robust to a whole distribution of plants~\cite{Tobin2017}. If the physical system lies within this distribution, the policy transfers without further training, as Figure~\ref{fig:domain-randomization} illustrates. Calibration and randomization complement each other. Calibration moves the simulation toward the physical system, and randomization covers the error that remains.

Perception must be matched in the same way, because rendered images differ from real ones in lighting, texture, and depth noise~\cite{Tobin2017,Handa2024}. The visual encoder of the policy decides how much of this gap the policy faces. An image-space encoder reads the scene from its appearance, which differs most between the two domains. A point cloud back-projected from the depth image instead carries the metric geometry of hand, nut, and bolt explicitly~\cite{Ze2024}. The policy of this thesis should transfer zero-shot, so that it runs on the robot as it was trained in simulation. For this, the perception, the action path, and the randomization of the simulation must be matched to the physical system.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:research-questions}

The difficulties of dexterous threading are addressed separately in prior work. Simulation-based assembly learning makes thread contact tractable and transfers insertion and threading to hardware, but with a parallel-jaw gripper and a part that is already held when contact begins~\cite{Narang2022,Noseworthy2025,Tang2023industreal}. Learned dexterous screwing covers a spinning gait for an installed nut~\cite{Hsieh2026} and goal-conditioned screwing of a furniture leg~\cite{Lum2026}. Policy chaining reaches long horizons at the cost of transitions between separately trained policies~\cite{Lee2022,Chen2023}, and teacher--student distillation yields deployable visuomotor policies for single skills~\cite{Lum2024,Singh2025}. No prior work threads a nut over many grasp-and-release cycles with a dexterous hand. Chapter~\ref{ch:sota} reviews this work in detail. Closing this gap requires a policy that sustains cyclic threading with a dexterous hand from sensor data, learned in simulation and deployed on hardware. Three research questions follow from it.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (long horizon).] \RQone
  \item[RQ2 (policy design).] \RQtwo
  \item[RQ3 (sim-to-real).] \RQthree
\end{description}

RQ1 addresses the compounding error of the cyclic task (Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-horizon}). RQ2 addresses the design of this policy, the visual encoder that faces the perception gap (Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-transfer}) and the action head that must represent multimodal demonstrations (Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-horizon}). RQ3 addresses the transfer difficulty (Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-transfer}) and, through the simplified thread model, the contact difficulty (Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-contact}). Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} answers RQ1 and RQ2 with ablations in simulation, two of which are repeated on hardware, and RQ3 on hardware.

\section{Approach and Contributions}\label{sec:approach-contributions}

This thesis proposes NUTS, a framework that decomposes the task during data generation and unifies it at deployment, as Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes. In the teacher training phase, a privileged \ac{RL} teacher is trained with \ac{PPO} on a simplified thread model. To narrow the solution space of the hand (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}), the teacher learns one grasp strategy, the pinch grasp, which it must acquire, hold under load, and turn. Scripted controllers release the nut and return the hand, and chained with the teacher they form the hybrid teacher, which generates complete multi-cycle demonstrations. In the student training phase, a single visuomotor student is distilled from these demonstrations by \ac{CFM}~\cite{Lipman2023}, following earlier work that distills privileged policies into sensor-based students~\cite{Singh2025,Lum2024}. The student executes every phase of every cycle on hardware, from a point cloud and proprioception and without real-world data.

A conditional flow-matching student is distilled from them and acts from RGB and depth. The thesis makes three contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{A training recipe for long-horizon cyclic dexterous threading.} The recipe covers the three stages of the pipeline. A privileged \ac{RL} teacher is trained on a simplified thread model that couples axial travel to rotation. The teacher is chained with scripted release and return into a hybrid teacher that collects complete multi-cycle demonstrations. A single visuomotor student is distilled from these demonstrations by conditional flow matching.
  \item \emph{A simulation setup for zero-shot transfer.} The setup comprises the identified arm and hand assets, the calibrated camera model, the Cartesian action space shared by teacher, student, and robot, and the domain randomization of physics, thread, and appearance. Appendices~\ref{ch:appendix} and~\ref{ch:app-controller} list the settings needed to reproduce it.
  \item \emph{An empirical study of the design choices.} Ablations in simulation and on hardware compare the full method with students that replace one design choice each, namely the complete multi-cycle demonstrations, the point-cloud encoder, and the flow-matching action head. An evaluation across nut sizes and profiles shows where the learned skill generalizes and where it fails.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Outline}\label{sec:outline}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows.

Chapter~\ref{ch:sota} provides the theoretical background that the method builds on, reviews related work, and states the research gap.

Chapter~\ref{ch:method} presents the method: the problem formulation, an overview of the distillation pipeline, the privileged teacher with its thread model and action contract, the construction of the demonstration dataset with the hybrid teacher, and the distillation of the student by conditional flow matching.

Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} defines the evaluation metrics, the experimental setup, and the ablations, and it reports the results in simulation and on hardware, and across nut sizes and geometries.

Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets the results with respect to the research questions, discusses what they mean for robotic assembly, and states the limitations of the work.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} returns to the assembly motivation of this chapter, summarizes what a dexterous robot can now do in threading and what is still missing, and outlines future work.
