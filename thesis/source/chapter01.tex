\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}%

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

Threaded fasteners such as a nut on a bolt join parts so that the joint can be released again, which makes them the standard choice wherever products are assembled in the field, maintained, adjusted, or taken apart~\cite{Budynas2020,Niemann2019}. They appear in almost every industry. The halves of a cast engine housing are bolted together, airplane seats are mounted with threaded bolts, and the injector of a rocket engine is bolted onto the combustion chamber. Many valve and pipe connections consist of two threaded halves that are screwed together with a sealing material so that the fluid stays inside, in building plumbing as well as in the cooling loop of a computer.

In all of these applications, the product must be manufacturable in modules, assembled in a defined process, and opened again without damage for inspection, maintenance, repair, and development.

In industrial production, dedicated equipment turns the fastener along the thread. Handheld and stationary nutrunners drive the fastener with a powered spindle, multi-spindle stations tighten several fasteners of one part at once, and robots carry screwdriving end effectors that feed and drive fasteners.
% TODO(cite): industrial threading automation (nutrunners, multi-spindle stations, robotic screwdriving end effectors).
These machines do not need to locate the hole, because a fixture clamps the part in a known pose. The screw or nut arrives from a feeder in a known orientation, and the tool is matched to that one fastener type.
% TODO(cite): dependence of automated fastening on fixtures, fixed part poses, and fastener feeding.
This setup is economical in high-volume production. It is not available where parts vary, where the cell is not built around one product, or where batches are small, and it is not available in maintenance and disassembly, where the pose of the part is not fixed in advance.
% TODO(cite): limits of dedicated fastening automation for product variants, small batches, maintenance, and disassembly.

Threaded fasteners are also familiar from everyday life, for example from flat-pack furniture and household appliances. A person fastens a screw without deliberate planning. The person picks up the screw, finds the hole, seats the screwdriver, and turns. If the screw does not bite, the person backs off, straightens it in the hole, and turns again. People handle the cases that dedicated equipment cannot. They pick up a nut, place it on a bolt, and turn it with their fingers, and they use the same hands to operate a wrench or a screwdriver. A robot with a dexterous, multi-fingered hand could in principle do the same. It could work with the parts and tools that were designed for people, in workcells that were designed for people, without a dedicated tool for every fastener~\cite{BicchiKumar2000,Weinberg2024}. Learning-based control has made in-hand manipulation with such hands practical in simulation and on hardware~\cite{Chen2021,Handa2024,Weinberg2024}. Robots have fastened screws with manual screwdrivers~\cite{Tang2023,Tang2024screwdriving}, and a single learned policy has manipulated a range of everyday tools with a dexterous hand~\cite{Kedia2026}. The motivation of this thesis is a dexterous robot that assists in manufacturing and assembly in environments built for humans.

Threading a nut by hand is one of the basic skills such a robot needs, and it is effortless for humans but challenging for robots. Moravec's paradox describes this contrast. It is comparatively easy to make computers perform well on tasks that people find intellectually demanding, and difficult to give them the perception and mobility skills of a small child~\cite{Moravec1988}. Industrial fastening shows both sides of this paradox. A nutrunner on a fixture drives a fastener without difficulty, because the cell design has already solved the difficult part: the part is held in a known pose and the fastener is presented in a known orientation. Without this structure, a robot must locate the hole, align the fastener, and engage the thread, which a person does without thinking. A dexterous robot must therefore learn the skill.

The hand that makes such a robot versatile also makes the skill challenging to learn. A multi-fingered hand on an arm has far more actuated joints than a parallel-jaw gripper, and its action space is correspondingly high-dimensional~\cite{Chen2021,Chen2023}. The volume of this space grows exponentially with the number of joints. Random exploration therefore covers an ever smaller fraction of it, and a reward that guides the policy through it is difficult to design~\cite{Kober2013}. The task also has a large solution space. A hand can hold a small object such as a nut with many grasp types, from a pinch between thumb and index finger to grasps that involve several fingers, as the taxonomy of human grasps shows~\cite{Feix2016}. A policy that may choose among all of these grasps must explore a correspondingly larger space of finger motions, and its reward must steer it toward one grasp that turns the nut reliably.

A dexterous hand must also acquire the grasp, keep it under load while the nut turns, and release it again. Learned assembly with parallel-jaw grippers does not face this part of the problem. There, the threading or insertion policy starts every episode with the part already held. It therefore learns only to move a grasped part, and grasping, holding, and re-grasping are not part of the learned skill~\cite{Narang2022,Noseworthy2025,Tang2023industreal}. Learned dexterous threading has so far addressed the spinning of an already engaged fastener~\cite{Hsieh2026} and goal-conditioned screwing of furniture parts~\cite{Lum2026}. This thesis therefore trains one policy that controls the hand and the arm together, and it narrows the solution space to one grasp strategy, the pinch grasp, which the policy must acquire, hold, and release. Even with this restriction, learning the skill in simulation and executing it on hardware poses three further difficulties, which the next section describes.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem-setup}

Beyond the control of the hand (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}), learning to thread a nut in simulation and executing the skill on hardware combines three difficulties. Engaged threads form a contact-rich interaction that is expensive to simulate, limiting parallel \ac{RL} training. The task repeats over many cycles, so that errors compound over a long horizon. Finally, the learned skill must cross the gap between simulation and hardware. The following subsections state each difficulty, the reason it arises, and the requirement it places on the method.

\subsection{Simulating Thread Contact at Scale}\label{sec:difficulty-contact}

Learning a contact-rich skill directly on hardware is slow and unsafe. \Ac{RL} requires many trials, every trial costs robot time and wear, and exploration produces collisions and excessive forces~\cite{Kober2013}. Simulation removes these limits. GPU-parallel simulators run thousands of environments at once and have made \ac{RL} feasible for dexterous manipulation and for contact-rich assembly~\cite{Handa2024,Petrenko2023,Narang2022}.

Faithful simulation of thread contact is expensive. Engaged threads produce thousands of simultaneous contacts, and Factory requires dedicated signed-distance-field contacts to simulate 1000 nut-and-bolt assemblies in real time on one GPU~\cite{Narang2022}. The number of contacts grows with the number of engaged thread turns. Their cost is paid again in every parallel environment, at every physics step, and for each of the millions of samples that on-policy \ac{RL} consumes.

Cheaper contact handling introduces a second problem. PhysX, the physics engine of the simulator used in this thesis, resolves contact by letting bodies overlap slightly and then pushing them apart again (Section~\ref{sec:thread-model}). This overlap is harmless for large bodies but not for threads, whose pitch is only 3 to 4\,mm for the nuts used in this thesis. A nut that is pressed along the bolt can therefore slip through the threads into the next groove instead of following the helix. An \ac{RL} policy can exploit this. If the reward pays for axial progress of the nut, the policy collects it by pushing the nut through the threads instead of turning it. The resulting behavior solves the simulated task and fails on hardware, where the threads are rigid and the nut advances only by rotating. A thread model for learning this skill therefore must be cheap enough for massively parallel \ac{RL}, and it must not let the policy advance the nut by interpenetration.

\subsection{Compounding Error in Long-Horizon Cyclic Tasks}\label{sec:difficulty-horizon}

A long-horizon cyclic task repeats a fixed sequence of phases many times while the task state advances from one repetition to the next. Threading with a pinch grasp is such a task. Because the range of the wrist and the fingers limits each turn, the hand must approach the nut, grasp it, turn it, release it, and return for the next grasp, cycle after cycle. Because the nut advances along the thread by a fraction of a pitch in every cycle, no two cycles start from the same state.

Three properties make long-horizon cyclic tasks challenging. First, errors compound across cycles. A grasp that lands slightly off the intended faces, a release that disturbs the nut, or a return that brings the fingers back at the wrong height carries over into the next cycle and accumulates. A learned policy amplifies this effect, because small deviations lead it to states it was not trained on, where its errors grow further~\cite{Ross2011}. Second, failures concentrate at the transitions between phases, where the contact between hand and nut is made or broken. Third, a single \ac{RL} policy for the whole task requires a reward that covers every phase and every transition, and it must assign the outcome of a late cycle to decisions made many steps earlier. Both requirements grow with the horizon. Every shaped phase adds reward terms that the policy may exploit (Section~\ref{sec:rl-background}), and the discounted return weights an outcome less the further it lies after the decision that caused it~\cite{Sutton2018,Kober2013}. For dexterous hands, this compositional structure adds to the difficulty of the high-dimensional action space~\cite{Chen2023}. A sparse binary task reward has trained a single policy on long assembly sequences in simulation~\cite{Zhang2026}, at the cost of \ac{RL} at large scale (Section~\ref{sec:related-chaining}).

The common answer is to decompose the task into skills, train one policy per skill, and chain the policies at deployment~\cite{Chen2023}. In a chain, the final state of one policy must lie in the region where the next policy succeeds. This requires additional mechanisms that fine-tune the sub-policies for their neighbors~\cite{Chen2023,Lee2022}, and several policies together with a switching logic must be deployed. A second answer is to imitate complete long-horizon demonstrations with one generative policy~\cite{Chi2024,Black2026}. This avoids the reward problem, but it requires demonstrations of the whole task, and teleoperated demonstrations of forceful dexterous manipulation are difficult to collect~\cite{Kedia2026}. The method should therefore combine the strengths of both answers. Its training signal should be as easy to specify phase by phase as in a decomposition, and it should deploy one policy that sustains many cycles without switching between separate policies.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer for Visuomotor Policies}\label{sec:difficulty-transfer}

A skill learned in simulation must transfer to hardware. The policy acts on the physical system with the same commands that it learned in simulation. The simulation must therefore reproduce the physical system wherever the outcome of a command depends on it.

This begins with the robot asset. The inertial parameters, joint limits, and joint dynamics of the arm decide how the arm responds to a commanded torque. In the hand, the finger couplings, the joint and effort limits, and the pad friction decide where the fingertips land, how strongly they press, and whether a grasp holds under the turning load. The fingertip forces that the policy observes must also match the forces that the physical sensors report. A mismatch in any of these elements changes the outcome of an action, and a policy that relies on the simulated outcome fails on hardware.
The thread is the part of the system that is not measured on hardware. Its simulation must be fast enough for massively parallel \ac{RL} (Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-contact}) and accurate enough in kinematics and dynamics that the simulated nut responds to the fingers like the physical one, advancing along the helix as it turns and resisting the turn. Because the thread dynamics of the physical nut are unknown, the thread interaction must instead be randomized widely, from the friction that resists the turn to the friction between the nut, the bolt, and the finger pads.

No simulation reproduces the physical system exactly. Domain randomization therefore varies the parameters of the simulation across training episodes, so that the policy learns to be robust to a whole distribution of plants~\cite{Tobin2017}. If the physical system lies within this distribution, the policy transfers without further training, as Figure~\ref{fig:domain-randomization} illustrates. Calibration and randomization complement each other. Calibration moves the simulation toward the physical system, and randomization covers the error that remains.

A single calibrated simulation (red circle) may not match reality (green triangle), but a sufficiently wide distribution of randomized simulations (dashed ellipses) can encompass the real-world conditions and enable zero-shot transfer. Perception must be matched in the same way, because rendered images differ from real ones in lighting, texture, and depth noise~\cite{Tobin2017,Handa2024}. The policy of this thesis should transfer zero-shot, so that it runs on the robot as it was trained in simulation. For this, the perception, the action path, and the randomization of the simulation must be matched to the physical system.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:research-questions}

The difficulties of dexterous threading are addressed separately in prior work. Simulation-based assembly learning makes thread contact tractable and transfers insertion and threading to hardware, but with a parallel-jaw gripper and a part that is already held when contact begins~\cite{Narang2022,Noseworthy2025,Tang2023industreal}. Learned dexterous screwing covers a spinning gait for an installed nut~\cite{Hsieh2026} and goal-conditioned screwing of a furniture leg~\cite{Lum2026}. Policy chaining reaches long horizons at the cost of transitions between separately trained policies~\cite{Lee2022,Chen2023}, and teacher--student distillation yields deployable visuomotor policies for single skills~\cite{Lum2024,Singh2025}. No prior work threads a nut over many grasp-and-release cycles with a dexterous hand, and none does so with a policy trained on simulated data alone. Chapter~\ref{ch:sota} reviews this work in detail. Closing this gap requires a policy that sustains cyclic threading with a dexterous hand from sensor data, learned in simulation and deployed on hardware. Three research questions follow from it.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (long horizon).] \RQone
  \item[RQ2 (policy design).] \RQtwo
  \item[RQ3 (sim-to-real).] \RQthree
\end{description}

RQ1 addresses the compounding error of the cyclic task (Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-horizon}). RQ2 addresses the dimensionality of the hand (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}), because the deployed policy must control hand and arm from perception alone over the whole horizon. RQ3 addresses the transfer difficulty (Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-transfer}) and, through the simplified thread model, the contact difficulty (Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-contact}). Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} answers RQ1 and RQ2 with ablations in simulation, two of which are repeated on hardware, and RQ3 on hardware.

\section{Approach and Contributions}\label{sec:approach-contributions}

This thesis proposes NUTS, a framework that decomposes the task during data generation and unifies it at deployment, as Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes. In the teacher training phase, a privileged \ac{RL} teacher is trained with \ac{PPO} on a simplified thread model and learns the pinch-grasp turn. Scripted controllers release the nut and return the hand, and chained with the teacher they form the hybrid teacher, which generates complete multi-cycle demonstrations. In the student training phase, a single visuomotor student is distilled from these demonstrations by \ac{CFM}~\cite{Lipman2023}, following earlier work that distills privileged policies into sensor-based students~\cite{Singh2025,Lum2024}. The student executes every phase of every cycle on hardware, from a point cloud and proprioception and without real-world data.

    \centering%
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,trim=0bp 45bp 6bp 0bp,clip]{figures/teaser.png}%
    \caption{Overview of the approach: decompose during data generation, unify at deployment. Top: a privileged \acs{RL} teacher is trained in parallel simulated workcells. During data collection its rollouts are chained with scripted release-and-return controllers into long-horizon demonstrations $\Dsim$. A conditional flow-matching student is distilled from them and acts from RGB and depth. Bottom: the phases of one cycle on the physical robot, approach, grasp, rotation, goal reaching, and release and return, after which the next cycle begins.}%
    \label{fig:overview}%
\end{figure}%

The thesis makes three contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{A hybrid teacher for cyclic nut threading.} Cyclic threading is formulated as an ordered sequence of phases with cyclic and episodic tracking errors. A privileged \ac{RL} policy, trained in a simplified thread simulation that couples axial travel to rotation, is chained with scripted release and return into a teacher that generates multi-cycle demonstrations.
  \item \emph{A unified visuomotor student distilled by conditional flow matching.} One student predicts action chunks from a point cloud and proprioception for every phase of every cycle. In simulation, it completes all ten cycles in every trial, and every ablated student fails earlier.
  \item \emph{Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.} A calibrated camera model, visual randomization, and a Cartesian action path shared with the robot transfer the student to a physical dexterous hand--arm system without real-world data. On hardware, it completes all ten cycles in every trial on hexagonal nuts of three sizes and on unseen octagonal and round profiles, and it fails on a rectangular profile.
\end{enumerate}

This work was carried out together with the advisor, who developed substantial parts of the hybrid teacher, the distillation stack, and the hardware experiments. The acknowledgments state this attribution.

\section{Outline}\label{sec:outline}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows.

Chapter~\ref{ch:sota} provides the theoretical background that the method builds on, reviews related work, and states the research gap.

Chapter~\ref{ch:method} presents the method: the problem formulation, an overview of the distillation pipeline, the privileged teacher with its thread model and action contract, the construction of the demonstration dataset with the hybrid teacher, and the distillation of the student by conditional flow matching.

Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} defines the evaluation metrics, the experimental setup, and the ablations, and it reports the results in simulation and on hardware, and across nut sizes and geometries.

Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets the results with respect to the research questions, discusses what they mean for robotic assembly, and states the limitations of the work.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} returns to the assembly motivation of this chapter, summarizes what a dexterous robot can now do in threading and what is still missing, and outlines future work.
