\chapter{Experiments}\label{ch:experiments}%

This chapter evaluates the method of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} in simulation, where every component can be varied under matched conditions, and on the physical system, where the student is deployed zero-shot. It answers the three research questions of Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} defines the evaluation ladder and the metrics. Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the experimental setup, including the hand--arm platform, the camera, the test objects, the deployment controller, and the zero-shot transfer protocol. Section~\ref{sec:ablations} defines the ablation studies, the question each of them answers, and how they relate to the research questions. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, starting with the teacher as the simulation baseline, followed by the ablations in simulation and on hardware, the generalization across nut sizes and geometries, and the long-horizon stress trials, and Section~\ref{sec:exp-summary} summarizes the chapter.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:evaluation}

A failed closed-loop rollout can originate in the supervised fit, in the command conversion and controller, or in the distribution shift between teacher and student states. The three evaluation modes in Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} separate these causes, and each mode makes a stronger claim than the previous one.

\begin{figure}[htb]%
    \centering%
    \input{figures/ladder.tikz}%
    \caption{Evaluation ladder. Each mode closes one more part of the control loop. A failure in a mode points to the causes listed below it.}%
    \label{fig:ladder}%
\end{figure}%

In the offline teacher-fit mode, every recorded teacher observation of a demonstration is fed to the student, and the sampled chunk is compared with the recorded command chunk. The reported quantities are the \acs{RMSE} of the first action and of the full chunk and the per-joint errors over time. Passing shows that the checkpoint can represent the observation-to-action mapping under the chosen alignment. Controller correctness and closed-loop success are tested by the next two modes. In the teacher-forced clock mode, the student's predicted commands are executed in live physics, but the student receives the recorded teacher observations selected on the recorded playback timeline, and for progress-conditioned checkpoints the same timeline supplies $p_t$. The realized joints, tool center point, and nut pose are compared with the recording. Passing shows that the learned action map, the command conversion, the controller, and the physics reproduce the nominal behavior once closed-loop observation drift is removed. Only the normal closed-loop mode, in which the student consumes live observations and its errors feed back into later observations, measures task success and compounding error. It is the mode of all results that claim success. The two diagnostic modes are used in that order to localize a failure before expensive retraining. A bad offline fit points to capacity, normalization, or alignment. A good fit with bad teacher-forced execution points to the command contract. Good teacher-forced execution with a bad closed loop points to distribution shift and recovery, which online labels or disturbance augmentation address.

Each condition is evaluated over $N_{\mathrm{tr}}=10$ trials with up to ten cycles per trial, where each cycle consists of a nominal $60\degree$ clockwise turn followed by release and return. A cycle counts as completed when the release has been triggered and the return has been validated against the reset pose within its 16\,s timeout (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}). A trial ends at the first transition failure, early termination, or time limit. Three quantities are reported. The mean completed cycle count $\bar{C}=N_{\mathrm{tr}}^{-1}\sum_i C_i$ counts, for trial $i$, the cycles $C_i\in\{0,\dots,10\}$ completed before failure or timeout. Incomplete cycles are excluded, and higher is better. The cyclic tracking error is computed for every completed cycle $k$ of trial $i$ as $e_{i,k}=\Delta\varphi_{i,k}-60\degree$, where $\Delta\varphi_{i,k}=\varphi_{i,k}-\varphi_{i,k-1}$ is the clockwise nut rotation. Over all $M=\sum_i C_i$ completed cycles the mean $\mu_{\mathrm{cyc}}=M^{-1}\sum_{i,k}e_{i,k}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{cyc}}$ are reported, where negative values indicate lag and positive values overshoot, and a mean closer to zero with a smaller deviation is better. If $M=0$ both statistics are undefined. The ten-cycle tracking error is computed over the set $I_{10}=\{i:C_i=10\}$ of trials that complete all cycles as $e_{10,i}=\sum_{k=1}^{10}\Delta\varphi_{i,k}-600\degree$, reported as mean $\mu_{10}$ and standard deviation over $I_{10}$. All errors are in degrees. In simulation the nut angle is read from the simulator. On hardware it is read from an angular scale mounted under the bolt (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

The robot is a seven-DoF torque-controlled \ac{FR3} arm with a five-fingered Inspire RH56DFX right hand mounted on its flange (Figure~\ref{fig:platform}). The hand has twelve finger joints driven by six motors. The non-thumb fingers each have one actuated flexion joint with a mechanically coupled distal joint, and the thumb has an abduction joint and a coupled flexion chain. Only three hand degrees of freedom are commanded by the policies, thumb abduction, thumb flexion, and index flexion, which together form the pinch used for threading, and the remaining fingers hold a curled posture (Section~\ref{sec:teacher}). The hand is position-controlled through its serial interface, and the arm is torque-controlled through the Franka control interface at 1\,kHz. Both teacher and student command targets at 15\,Hz.

Robot commands use the same Cartesian impedance law as in simulation, so the student's bounded action reaches the arm through the shared action path of Appendix~\ref{sec:action-contract}. The deployment controller is a direct implementation of the dynamically consistent operational-space controller \eqref{eq:osc}~\cite{RoboticSystemsLab2019}, the same controller that is used in training. It uses the rotation-vector orientation error, the dynamically consistent nullspace projector with the arm mass matrix from the robot model and the hand configured as end-effector load, and no Coriolis term. The student's commands are therefore executed with the stiffness under which the teacher learned its contact behavior. Gravity is compensated by libfranka, which matches the simulation, where gravity is disabled for the arm. The controller runs at the 1\,kHz rate of the Franka control interface, while the student commands targets at 15\,Hz. The hand receives its pinch targets once per policy step. The task-space gains equal those of the simulation, 565\,N/m and 28\,N\,m/rad with critical damping, and the nullspace stiffness is 10 with critical damping. The targets pass through the same action smoothing as in training and no further low-pass filter, and the controller applies the step limit of Table~\ref{tab:controller} against the measured grasp pose in every 1\,kHz cycle. Further details on the deployment controller are given in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-ctrl-additions}.

\section{Ablation Studies}\label{sec:ablations}

The method of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} rests on three design choices. The student is trained on complete multi-cycle demonstrations, it perceives the scene through a point-cloud encoder, and it predicts action chunks with a flow-matching head. Each choice has a simpler or more common alternative, and the ablation study asks whether the choice is needed. Some of these questions are research questions of Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}. Others are design questions that arise when such a student is built, for example whether the established regression recipe for distilling a privileged \ac{RL} teacher would suffice. Every ablated student changes exactly one component of the full method and keeps all others. All students are trained on the simulated dataset of Section~\ref{sec:hybrid} with matched training and evaluation budgets and are evaluated with the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. A difference to the full method can therefore be attributed to the changed component. Table~\ref{tab:ablation-design} lists the five students, the component each one changes, the question it answers, and the domains in which it is evaluated.

\begin{table}[htb]%
    \centering%
    \caption{Design of the ablation study. Each ablated student differs from the full method (e) in the listed component only. All students are trained on the same simulated demonstrations with matched budgets.}%
    \label{tab:ablation-design}%
    \small
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.6cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}Xcc}
        \toprule
        Student & Changed component & Question & RQ & Evaluated in \\
        \midrule
        (a) single-cycle & training data: one-cycle demonstrations, context reset per cycle & Is a policy trained on the complete multi-cycle behavior needed, or can a single-cycle policy be repeated? & RQ1 & sim, real \\
        (b) ResNet encoder & observation encoder: pretrained image-space encoder & Does the geometric structure of the representation matter, given the same depth input? & RQ2 & sim, real \\
        (c) \acs{LSTM} regression & backbone and objective: recurrent, no chunking, deterministic & Does the established recurrent regression recipe suffice for the long horizon? & RQ2 & sim \\
        (d) Transformer regression & objective: deterministic regression & Is the generative objective needed when backbone and chunking are kept? & RQ2 & sim \\
        (e) full method & none & Reference for all comparisons & RQ1--3 & sim, real \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}%

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] The first question is whether long-horizon distillation is needed at all, or whether a policy that masters one cycle can be invoked repeatedly. A single-cycle student is the cheaper alternative. Its demonstrations are shorter, it needs no context across cycles, and the teacher of Section~\ref{sec:teacher} already solves one turn. Repetition, however, assumes that every cycle starts from the same state, and this does not hold for threading. Each $60\degree$ turn advances the nut by one sixth of a pitch down the bolt, so the nut is grasped lower in every cycle. A single-cycle demonstration always shows the nut at its reset height, whereas a ten-cycle demonstration shows it at every height it reaches during the trial. The single-cycle student (a) is trained on demonstrations of one cycle and is invoked ten times in a trial. Its temporal context is reset after every cycle. It receives the same progress scalar as the full method, so the comparison isolates the coverage of the axial state in the demonstrations. If the axial advance matters, (a) should complete the first cycles and fail once the nut has descended below the heights that its demonstrations show. The completed cycle count locates this failure. The ablation answers RQ1.

  \item[(b)] The second question is whether the student needs an explicit three-dimensional representation of the scene. Image-space encoders with a pretrained backbone are a common choice for visuomotor policies, and depth can be supplied to them as an additional input channel. The ResNet student (b) replaces the point-cloud encoder with a one-branch image-space encoder. An ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 stage-2 trunk processes RGB, log depth, and a validity mask. An embedding of the calibrated camera-frame XYZ is added on the token grid, and attention pooling over the tokens yields the same 64-D feature as the point-cloud encoder. Policy, objective, data, and budget are unchanged. Because the image encoder also receives depth and per-pixel coordinates, (b) tests whether the geometric structure of the representation decides the closed-loop result, rather than the depth input alone. One further difference remains. Random point sampling acts as an augmentation that the image encoder does not receive.

A closed-loop failure of (b) would not show what its feature lacks, so a representation probe complements the ablation. One shared pose decoder is trained on the frozen 64-D feature of each encoder to predict the position and the orientation of the nut, and the two prediction errors are compared between the encoders. The probe separates the two quantities that the task demands from the representation. Sub-millimeter localization of the nut is unnecessary, because the engagement of the nut on the bolt and the compliance of the impedance controller absorb lateral error. The re-grasp, however, depends on the orientation of the nut faces relative to the fingers. A feature that encodes position but not orientation would let the fingers close on the right spot with the wrong face alignment. The ablation and the probe together answer the encoder part of RQ2.

  \item[(c)] The third question is whether the generative objective is needed, or whether deterministic regression of the teacher's actions suffices. Two regression students address it at different granularity. The \ac{LSTM} student (c) tests the established recipe. It keeps the point-cloud encoder but replaces the flow-matching Transformer with the recurrent backbone of DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025}, a layer-normalized 512-unit \ac{LSTM} followed by a 512--512--256 ELU \acs{MLP}, and it is trained by deterministic action regression on the same data. It changes the backbone, removes action chunking, and removes the generative objective at once. It therefore asks whether a recurrent regression student, as used to distill a privileged teacher into a vision-based student, carries over to the long-horizon task.
  \item[(d)] The Transformer regression student (d) isolates the objective. It keeps the point-cloud encoder and uses a deterministic variant of \ac{ACT}~\cite{Zhao2023} without the variational objective. It predicts the eight-step chunk from eight learned action queries, and its backbone has the same size as the flow Transformer. It shares the chunk length and the temporal ensembling of the full method, so the difference between (d) and (e) is the training objective alone.
% TODO(unsure): no implementation of the ACT-style regression baseline exists in forgeUltra (distillation/utils/action_heads/) or in ~/code/icra_2027 (LaTeX only); it is described as published in NUTS and must be implemented before ablation (d) can be retrained.

The comparison is motivated by the structure of the labels. For near-identical observations, the dataset contains the teacher's nominal action in one demonstration and its recovery after an impulse in another. A squared-error head converges to the average of the two, an action that neither demonstration contains, whereas the flow head samples one of them. The flow head also predicts a velocity from a noisy chunk toward the data, a denoising problem that may be easier to fit than a direct regression from observation to chunk. If (d) fails where (e) succeeds, the generative objective accounts for the difference. The design does not separate these two mechanisms, because both come with the objective. The two regression students answer the action-head part of RQ2.
\end{enumerate}

The simulated comparisons hold under matched conditions, but the physical system adds sensor noise, calibration error, and real contact. The full method (e), the single-cycle student (a), and the ResNet student (b) are therefore also deployed zero-shot on the M24 hexagonal nut, with the setup of Section~\ref{sec:setup} and the same protocol. The full method answers RQ3 directly. The two ablations ask whether the comparisons of RQ1 and RQ2 carry over to the physical system. For (a), the question is whether the advantage of multi-cycle distillation persists under real contact. For (b), it is whether the image-space representation transfers as well as the point cloud. The encoder is the component most exposed to the transfer gap. Inside the crop box, the point geometry agrees between the two domains up to depth noise and calibration error, whereas an image-space encoder faces the appearance gap of the full image. The regression students (c) and (d) are evaluated in simulation only.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Transfer to Hardware}\label{sec:results-real}

The real-world part of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the zero-shot deployment on the training nut. The full method (e) completes all ten cycles in every trial with a cyclic error of $-4.21\pm1.20\degree$ and a ten-cycle error of $-42.10\pm4.37\degree$. Its lag per cycle is larger than in simulation, while its spread stays similar. The single-cycle student (a) completes $5.2\pm1.2$ cycles. In its typical failure, the fingers slip off the edge of the nut after a few cycles, which is consistent with a student that has learned the turning skill but does not follow the axial advance of the nut. The ResNet student (b) completes only $1.1\pm0.9$ cycles with an overshoot of $+24.09\pm9.94\degree$. Both comparisons from simulation therefore carry over to hardware, and the gap between the image-space encoder and the point cloud widens.

\subsection{Generalization and Long-Horizon Stress}\label{sec:geometry}

The training nut alone does not show how far the zero-shot transfer reaches, so the full method is also evaluated on all twelve test objects (Figure~\ref{fig:nut-family}). Table~\ref{tab:geometry} reports ten trials per object: the M24, M30, and M36 hexagonal nuts, whose profile the demonstrations show, and the octagonal, rectangular, and round profiles of each size, which it has never seen. The study tests two kinds of generalization. Across sizes, the student must infer the nut size from the point cloud, since it receives no explicit size input. Across profiles, the question is whether the learned pinch-and-re-grasp strategy, which the demonstrations show only on hexagons, transfers to a different face topology.

The student completes all ten cycles in every trial on the three hexagonal nuts. The M30 nut shows the smallest lag, $-3.70\pm0.77\degree$ per cycle, and the M36 nut the largest, $-5.52\pm1.34\degree$. Among the unseen profiles, the octagonal and round nuts are also completed in every trial at all three sizes, and the round nut has the smallest lag at every size. On the rectangular profile, the M24 nut completes $3.0\pm1.2$ cycles, and the M30 and M36 nuts complete none. In the typical failure, the fingers turn the nut by $60\degree$ but do not establish contact with the next pair of faces in the following cycle, so the nut stays at its first $60\degree$ position. The octagonal and round profiles offer re-grasp configurations compatible with the hexagon, whereas the face topology of the rectangular profile conflicts with the learned re-grasp.

Unlike the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, the stress trials have no ten-cycle limit. The student is run on hardware for as many cycles as it sustains. Every revolution advances the nut by one pitch down the bolt, so after enough cycles the nut leaves the axial range that the ten-cycle demonstrations cover. The stress trials measure how far the learned behavior reaches beyond this range. The student sustains between 15 and 22 cycles before it fails, depending on nut size, profile, and test conditions, which extends the demonstrated horizon by at least half.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:exp-summary}

In simulation, the full method trained on the demonstrations of the hybrid teacher completes all ten cycles of the M24 task in every trial with a cyclic error of $-1.77\pm1.02\degree$. Every ablation fails earlier. The single-cycle student does not follow the axial advance of the nut (RQ1), and the ResNet encoder and both regression heads fall short of the full student design (RQ2). On hardware, the student transfers zero-shot and completes all ten cycles on the training nut with a cyclic error of $-4.21\pm1.20\degree$ (RQ3). It also completes all ten cycles on the M30 and M36 hexagonal nuts and on the unseen octagonal and round profiles. It fails on the rectangular profile, and in the stress trials it sustains 15 to 22 cycles. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets the simulation and hardware results together.
