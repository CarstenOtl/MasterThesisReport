\chapter{Experiments}\label{ch:experiments}%

This chapter evaluates the method of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} in simulation, where every component can be varied under matched conditions, and on the physical system, where the student is deployed zero-shot. It answers the three research questions of Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} defines the evaluation ladder and the metrics. Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the experimental setup, including the hand--arm platform, the camera, the test objects, the deployment controller, and the zero-shot transfer protocol. Section~\ref{sec:ablations} states the questions that the ablation studies answer and defines the students designed to answer them. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, starting with the teacher as the simulation baseline, followed by the ablations in simulation and on hardware and the generalization across nut sizes and geometries. Section~\ref{sec:exp-summary} summarizes the chapter.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:evaluation}

A failed closed-loop rollout can originate in the supervised fit, in the command conversion and controller, or in the distribution shift between teacher and student states. The three evaluation modes in Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} separate these causes, and each mode makes a stronger claim than the previous one.

\begin{figure}[htb]%
    \centering%
    \input{figures/ladder.tikz}%
    \caption{Evaluation ladder. Each mode closes one more part of the control loop. A failure in a mode points to the causes listed below it.}%
    \label{fig:ladder}%
\end{figure}%

In the offline teacher-fit mode, every recorded teacher observation of a demonstration is fed to the student, and the sampled chunk is compared with the recorded command chunk. The reported quantities are the \acs{RMSE} of the first action and of the full chunk and the per-joint errors over time. Passing shows that the checkpoint can represent the observation-to-action mapping under the chosen alignment. Controller correctness and closed-loop success are tested by the next two modes. In the teacher-forced clock mode, the student's predicted commands are executed in live physics, but the student receives the recorded teacher observations selected on the recorded playback timeline, and for progress-conditioned checkpoints the same timeline supplies $p_t$. The realized joints, tool center point, and nut pose are compared with the recording. Passing shows that the learned action map, the command conversion, the controller, and the physics reproduce the nominal behavior once closed-loop observation drift is removed. Only the normal closed-loop mode, in which the student consumes live observations and its errors feed back into later observations, measures task success and compounding error. It is the mode of all results that claim success. The two diagnostic modes are used in that order to localize a failure before expensive retraining. A bad offline fit points to capacity, normalization, or alignment. A good fit with bad teacher-forced execution points to the command contract. Good teacher-forced execution with a bad closed loop points to distribution shift and recovery, which online labels or disturbance augmentation address.

Each condition is evaluated over $N_{\mathrm{tr}}=10$ trials with up to ten cycles per trial, where each cycle consists of a nominal $60\degree$ clockwise turn followed by release and return. A cycle counts as completed when the release has been triggered and the return has been validated against the reset pose within its 16\,s timeout (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}). A trial ends at the first transition failure, early termination, or time limit. Three quantities are reported. The mean completed cycle count $\bar{C}=N_{\mathrm{tr}}^{-1}\sum_i C_i$ counts, for trial $i$, the cycles $C_i\in\{0,\dots,10\}$ completed before failure or timeout. Incomplete cycles are excluded, and higher is better. The cyclic tracking error is computed for every completed cycle $k$ of trial $i$ as $e_{i,k}=\Delta\varphi_{i,k}-60\degree$, where $\Delta\varphi_{i,k}=\varphi_{i,k}-\varphi_{i,k-1}$ is the clockwise nut rotation. Over all $M=\sum_i C_i$ completed cycles the mean $\mu_{\mathrm{cyc}}=M^{-1}\sum_{i,k}e_{i,k}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{cyc}}$ are reported, where negative values indicate lag and positive values overshoot, and a mean closer to zero with a smaller deviation is better. If $M=0$ both statistics are undefined. The ten-cycle tracking error is computed over the set $I_{10}=\{i:C_i=10\}$ of trials that complete all cycles as $e_{10,i}=\sum_{k=1}^{10}\Delta\varphi_{i,k}-600\degree$, reported as mean $\mu_{10}$ and standard deviation over $I_{10}$. All errors are in degrees. In simulation the nut angle is read from the simulator. On hardware it is read from an angular scale mounted under the bolt (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

The robot is a seven-DoF torque-controlled \ac{FR3} arm with a five-fingered Inspire RH56DFX right hand mounted on its flange (Figure~\ref{fig:platform}). The hand has twelve finger joints driven by six motors. The non-thumb fingers each have one actuated flexion joint with a mechanically coupled distal joint, and the thumb has an abduction joint and a coupled flexion chain. Only three hand degrees of freedom are commanded by the policies, thumb abduction, thumb flexion, and index flexion, which together form the pinch used for threading, and the remaining fingers hold a curled posture (Section~\ref{sec:teacher}). The hand is position-controlled through its serial interface, and the arm is torque-controlled through the Franka control interface at 1\,kHz. Both teacher and student command targets at 15\,Hz.

A fixed Intel RealSense D415 on a stand provides the \acs{RGBD} observation at $640\times480$. As in simulation, the stream is center-cropped and downscaled to the $320\times180$ policy input (Figure~\ref{fig:real-setup}). The pose of the camera relative to the robot base was deduced from multiple poses of a calibrated AprilTag observed by the camera, with a translation fit \acs{RMSE} of 9.7\,mm and a rotation fit \acs{RMSE} of $0.98\degree$ over the calibration poses. The calibrated optical pose and the intrinsics are the ones used to render the simulated camera and to back-project the point cloud, so the student sees the same geometry in both domains.

The metrics of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} require the nut angle. It is read from an angular scale printed on a sheet under the bolt pedestal (Figure~\ref{fig:real-phases}), and the unwrapped angle is recorded at the end of every goal-reaching phase. The test-object family consists of M24, M30, and M36 nuts in four profiles each, hexagonal, octagonal, rectangular, and round, with matching bolts and holders (Figure~\ref{fig:nut-family}). Teacher and student training use the hexagonal profiles only. The other three profiles are held out for the generalization study of Section~\ref{sec:geometry}.

Robot commands use the same Cartesian impedance law as in simulation, so the student's bounded action reaches the arm through the shared action path of Appendix~\ref{sec:action-contract}. The deployment controller is a direct implementation of the dynamically consistent operational-space controller \eqref{eq:osc}~\cite{RoboticSystemsLab2019}, the same controller that is used in training. It uses the rotation-vector orientation error, the dynamically consistent nullspace projector with the arm mass matrix from the robot model and the hand configured as end-effector load, and no Coriolis term. The student's commands are therefore executed with the stiffness under which the teacher learned its contact behavior. Gravity is compensated by libfranka, which matches the simulation, where gravity is disabled for the arm. The controller runs at the 1\,kHz rate of the Franka control interface, while the student commands targets at 15\,Hz. The hand receives its pinch targets once per policy step. The task-space gains equal those of the simulation, 565\,N/m and 28\,N\,m/rad with critical damping, and the nullspace stiffness is 10 with critical damping. The targets pass through the same action smoothing as in training and no further low-pass filter, and the controller applies the step limit of Table~\ref{tab:controller} against the measured grasp pose in every 1\,kHz cycle. Further details on the deployment controller are given in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-ctrl-additions}.

\section{Ablation Studies}\label{sec:ablations}

The student described in Chapter~\ref{ch:method} rests on three design choices:
\begin{itemize}
  \item It is distilled from complete multi-cycle demonstrations.
  \item It perceives the scene through a point-cloud encoder.
  \item It predicts action chunks with a flow-matching head.
\end{itemize}
Each choice has a simpler or more common alternative. The ablation studies are designed to answer the following questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] Is repeating a single-cycle student enough, or does a student distilled from complete multi-cycle demonstrations sustain more consecutive cycles?
  \item[(ii)] Can an image-space encoder of the same camera data replace the point-cloud encoder? Image-space encoders with a pretrained backbone are a common choice for visuomotor policies (Section~\ref{sec:related-visuomotor}).
  \item[(iii)] Is deterministic action regression enough, or does a flow-matching head let the student sustain more consecutive cycles? Deterministic regression of the teacher's actions is the established recipe for distilling a privileged teacher into a vision-based student~\cite{Singh2025}.
\end{enumerate}

To answer these questions, five students are compared. Each ablated student replaces one of the three design choices of the full method (e), is trained on the simulated dataset of Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}, and is evaluated over $N_{\mathrm{tr}}=10$ trials on the M24 hexagonal nut. Table~\ref{tab:ablation-design} summarizes the five students.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] \textbf{Single-cycle student.} It is trained on demonstrations of one cycle, and a state machine invokes it ten times in a trial. Its temporal context is reset after every cycle.
  \item[(b)] \textbf{ResNet student.} It replaces the \ac{DP3} point-cloud encoder with a one-branch image-space encoder. Its backbone is a ResNet-18~\cite{He2016} pretrained on ImageNet image classification and truncated after its second stage. It processes RGB, log depth, and a depth-validity mask jointly in one branch. An embedding of the calibrated camera-frame XYZ coordinates is added to its feature map, and attention pooling reduces the result to a 64-D feature, the size of the point-cloud feature.
  \item[(c)] \textbf{\acs{LSTM} regression student.} It keeps the point-cloud encoder. In place of the flow-matching Transformer, it uses the recurrent backbone of DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025}. This backbone is a layer-normalized 512-unit \ac{LSTM} followed by a 512--512--256 ELU \acs{MLP}. It is trained by deterministic action regression on the same data.
  \item[(d)] \textbf{Transformer regression student.} It is inspired by \ac{ACT}~\cite{Zhao2023}. Like the full method, it keeps the point-cloud encoder, uses a Transformer of the same size, and predicts eight-step chunks that are executed with the same temporal ensembling. It regresses the chunk deterministically from learned action queries, whereas the full method generates it with flow matching.
% TODO(unsure): no implementation of the ACT-style regression baseline exists in forgeUltra (distillation/utils/action_heads/) or in ~/code/icra_2027 (LaTeX only); it is described as published in NUTS and must be implemented before ablation (d) can be retrained.
  \item[(e)] \textbf{Full method.} The student described in Chapter~\ref{ch:method}, the reference for all comparisons.
\end{enumerate}

A representation probe complements the closed-loop comparison of question (ii). One shared decoder is trained on the frozen feature of each encoder to predict the position and the orientation of the nut, which shows what each feature encodes.

The simulated comparisons hold under matched conditions, but the physical system adds sensor noise, calibration error, and real contact. The full method (e), the single-cycle student (a), and the ResNet student (b) are therefore also deployed zero-shot on the M24 hexagonal nut, with the setup of Section~\ref{sec:setup} and the same protocol. The full method answers RQ3 directly. The two ablations ask whether the answers to questions (i) and (ii) carry over to the physical system. For (a), the question is whether the advantage of multi-cycle distillation persists under real contact. For (b), it is whether the image-space representation transfers as well as the point cloud. The encoder is the component most exposed to the transfer gap. Inside the crop box, the point geometry agrees between the two domains up to depth noise and calibration error, whereas an image-space encoder faces the appearance gap of the full image. The regression students (c) and (d) are evaluated in simulation only.

The two regression students fail first. The \ac{LSTM} student (c) diverges before it completes the first cycle. The Transformer regression student (d) completes 1.4 cycles and overshoots the target turn by more than $120\degree$ per cycle.

The comparison of the five students answers the questions of Section~\ref{sec:ablations}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] Repeating a single-cycle student is not enough. The single-cycle student masters the single turn but completes about half the cycles of the full method in both domains. Repeating a single-cycle policy therefore does not sustain the cyclic task.
  \item[(ii)] An image-space encoder cannot replace the point-cloud encoder. Replacing it with the image-space encoder costs most of the cycles in both domains, and the probe locates the loss in the orientation of the nut, on which the re-grasp depends. The gap to the full method widens from 7.7 cycles in simulation to 8.9 cycles on hardware.
  \item[(iii)] Deterministic regression is not enough. Both regression students complete fewer than two cycles on average. Because (d) keeps the backbone and the chunking of the full method, the generative objective accounts for the difference.
\end{enumerate}
The students rank in the same order in simulation and on hardware.

\subsection{Generalization}\label{sec:geometry}

The training nut alone does not show how far the zero-shot transfer reaches, so the full method is also evaluated on all twelve test objects (Figure~\ref{fig:nut-family}). Table~\ref{tab:geometry} reports ten trials per object: the M24, M30, and M36 hexagonal nuts, whose profile the demonstrations show, and the octagonal, rectangular, and round profiles of each size, which it has never seen. The study tests two kinds of generalization. Across sizes, the student must infer the nut size from the point cloud, since it receives no explicit size input. Across profiles, the question is whether the learned pinch-and-re-grasp strategy, which the demonstrations show only on hexagons, transfers to a different face topology.

The student completes all ten cycles in every trial on the three hexagonal nuts. The M30 nut shows the smallest lag, $-3.70\pm0.77\degree$ per cycle, and the M36 nut the largest, $-5.52\pm1.34\degree$. Among the unseen profiles, the octagonal and round nuts are also completed in every trial at all three sizes, and the round nut has the smallest lag at every size. On the rectangular profile, the M24 nut completes $3.0\pm1.2$ cycles, and the M30 and M36 nuts complete none. In the typical failure, the fingers turn the nut by $60\degree$ but do not establish contact with the next pair of faces in the following cycle, so the nut stays at its first $60\degree$ position. The octagonal and round profiles offer re-grasp configurations compatible with the hexagon, whereas the face topology of the rectangular profile conflicts with the learned re-grasp.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:exp-summary}

This chapter evaluated the student described in Chapter~\ref{ch:method} in simulation and zero-shot on the physical system, over ten trials of up to ten threading cycles per condition. Five students were compared on the M24 hexagonal nut, the full method (e) and four ablations that each replace one of its design choices. The full method was then evaluated on twelve nuts of three sizes and four profiles.

In simulation, the full method completes all ten cycles in every trial with a cyclic error of $-1.77\pm1.02\degree$ and a ten-cycle error of $-17.7\pm3.23\degree$. Every ablation fails before the tenth cycle in every trial. For RQ1, the single-cycle student (a) completes $5.5\pm0.8$ cycles in simulation and $5.2\pm1.2$ cycles on hardware. For RQ2, the ResNet student (b) completes $2.3\pm0.9$ and $1.1\pm0.9$ cycles and overshoots the target turn by more than $20\degree$ per cycle in both domains. Its feature predicts the nut position about as well as the point-cloud feature, with 8.6\,mm against 8\,mm, but its orientation error is $31.20\degree$ against $15.16\degree$. In simulation, the \ac{LSTM} regression student (c) completes no cycle, and the Transformer regression student (d) completes $1.4\pm0.8$ cycles with an overshoot of $+124.21\pm18.50\degree$ per cycle.
