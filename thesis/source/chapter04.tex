\chapter{Experiments}\label{ch:experiments}%

This chapter evaluates the method of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} in simulation, where every component can be varied under matched conditions, and on the physical system. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} defines the trial protocol and the metrics. Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the experimental setup, including the hand--arm platform, the camera, the test objects, and the deployment controller. Section~\ref{sec:ablations} states the questions that the ablation studies answer and defines the students designed to answer them. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, starting with the teacher as the simulation baseline, followed by the ablations in simulation and on hardware and the generalization across nut sizes and geometries. Section~\ref{sec:exp-summary} summarizes the chapter.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:evaluation}

Every policy in this chapter is evaluated in closed loop over a complete multi-cycle trial, in which it acts on its own observations and its deviations carry over from cycle to cycle. Completing the cycles comes first, and tracking accuracy is compared among the policies that complete them.

Each condition is evaluated over $N_{\mathrm{tr}}=10$ trials of up to $K=10$ cycles. A cycle consists of a pinch-grasp turn with a nominal target of $60\degree$ clockwise, followed by release and return to the grasp pose. It counts as completed when the release has been triggered and the return has been validated against the reset grasp pose within its 16\,s timeout (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}). A trial ends after ten completed cycles, or earlier at the first transition failure, early termination, or time limit. Three metrics are reported.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Completed cycles.} The number $C_i\in\{0,\dots,10\}$ of cycles that trial $i$ completes, averaged over the trials. Higher is better.
  \item \textbf{Cyclic error.} The cyclic tracking error $e_k$ of \eqref{eq:errors}, the clockwise nut rotation of cycle $k$ minus $60\degree$. Its mean and standard deviation are taken over all completed cycles of all trials. The error is undefined if no cycle is completed.
  \item \textbf{Ten-cycle error.} The episodic tracking error $e_{\mathrm{ep}}$ of \eqref{eq:errors} with $K=10$, the total rotation over ten cycles minus $600\degree$. It shows whether the cyclic errors cancel or accumulate. Its mean and standard deviation are taken over the trials that complete all ten cycles. The error is undefined if no trial does.
\end{itemize}
The cycle in which a trial fails is not counted in either error. Both errors are signed and given in degrees. A negative value denotes lag and a positive value overshoot, and a mean closer to zero with a smaller standard deviation is better. In simulation the nut angle $\varphi_k$ is read from the simulator, and on hardware it is read from an angular scale under the bolt (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

The robot is a seven-DoF torque-controlled \ac{FR3} arm with a five-fingered Inspire RH56DFX right hand mounted on its flange (Figure~\ref{fig:platform}). The hand has twelve finger joints driven by six motors. The non-thumb fingers each have one actuated flexion joint with a mechanically coupled distal joint, and the thumb has an abduction joint and a coupled flexion chain. Only three hand degrees of freedom are commanded by the policies, thumb abduction, thumb flexion, and index flexion, which together form the pinch used for threading, and the remaining fingers hold a curled posture (Section~\ref{sec:teacher}). The hand is position-controlled through its serial interface, and the arm is torque-controlled through the Franka control interface at 1\,kHz. Both teacher and student command targets at 15\,Hz.

\section{Ablation Studies}\label{sec:ablations}

The student described in Chapter~\ref{ch:method} rests on three design choices:
\begin{itemize}
  \item It is distilled from complete multi-cycle demonstrations.
  \item It perceives the scene through a point-cloud encoder.
  \item It predicts action chunks with a flow-matching head.
\end{itemize}
Each choice has a simpler or more common alternative. The ablation studies are designed to answer the following questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] Is repeating a single-cycle student sufficient, or does a student distilled from complete multi-cycle demonstrations sustain more consecutive cycles?
  \item[(ii)] Can an image-space encoder of the same camera data replace the point-cloud encoder? Image-space encoders with a pretrained backbone are a common choice for visuomotor policies (Section~\ref{sec:related-visuomotor}).
  \item[(iii)] Is deterministic action regression sufficient, or does a flow-matching head let the student sustain more consecutive cycles? Deterministic regression of the teacher's actions is the established recipe for distilling a privileged teacher into a vision-based student~\cite{Singh2025}.
\end{enumerate}

To answer these questions, five students are compared. Each ablated student replaces one of the three design choices of the full method (e), is trained on the simulated dataset of Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}, and is evaluated over $N_{\mathrm{tr}}=10$ trials on the M24 hexagonal nut. Table~\ref{tab:ablation-design} summarizes the five students.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] \textbf{Single-cycle student.} It is trained on demonstrations of one cycle, and a state machine invokes it ten times in a trial. Its temporal context is reset after every cycle.
  \item[(b)] \textbf{ResNet student.} It replaces the \ac{DP3} point-cloud encoder with a one-branch image-space encoder. Its backbone is a ResNet-18~\cite{He2016} pretrained on ImageNet image classification and truncated after its second stage. It processes RGB, log depth, and a depth-validity mask jointly in one branch. An embedding of the calibrated camera-frame XYZ coordinates is added to its feature map, and attention pooling reduces the result to a 64-D feature, the size of the point-cloud feature.
  \item[(c)] \textbf{\acs{LSTM} regression student.} It keeps the point-cloud encoder. In place of the flow-matching Transformer, it uses the recurrent backbone of DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025}. This backbone is a layer-normalized 512-unit \ac{LSTM} followed by a 512--512--256 ELU \acs{MLP}. It is trained by deterministic action regression on the same data.
  \item[(d)] \textbf{Transformer regression student.} It is inspired by \ac{ACT}~\cite{Zhao2023}. Like the full method, it keeps the point-cloud encoder, uses a Transformer of the same size, and predicts eight-step chunks that are executed with the same temporal ensembling. It regresses the chunk deterministically from learned action queries, whereas the full method generates it with flow matching.
% TODO(unsure): no implementation of the ACT-style regression baseline exists in forgeUltra (distillation/utils/action_heads/) or in ~/code/icra_2027 (LaTeX only); it is described as published in NUTS and must be implemented before ablation (d) can be retrained.
  \item[(e)] \textbf{Full method.} The student described in Chapter~\ref{ch:method}, the reference for all comparisons.
\end{enumerate}

A representation probe complements the closed-loop comparison of question (ii). One shared decoder is trained on the frozen feature of each encoder to predict the position and the orientation of the nut, which shows what each feature encodes.

The simulated comparisons hold under matched conditions, but the physical system adds sensor noise, calibration error, and real contact. The full method (e), the single-cycle student (a), and the ResNet student (b) are therefore also transferred sim-to-real and evaluated on the M24 hexagonal nut, with the setup of Section~\ref{sec:setup} and the same protocol. The full method answers RQ3 directly. The two ablations ask whether the answers to questions (i) and (ii) carry over to the physical system. For (a), the question is whether the advantage of multi-cycle distillation persists under real contact. For (b), it is whether the image-space representation transfers as well as the point cloud. The encoder is the component most exposed to the transfer gap. Inside the crop box, the point geometry agrees between the two domains up to depth noise and calibration error, whereas an image-space encoder faces the appearance gap of the full image. The regression students (c) and (d) are evaluated in simulation only.

The two regression students fail first. The \ac{LSTM} student (c) diverges before it completes the first cycle. The Transformer regression student (d) completes 1.4 cycles and overshoots the target turn by more than $120\degree$ per cycle.

The comparison of the five students answers the questions of Section~\ref{sec:ablations}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] Repeating a single-cycle student is not sufficient. The single-cycle student completes about half the cycles of the full method in both domains, 5.5 and 5.2 against 10.0.
  \item[(ii)] An image-space encoder cannot replace the point-cloud encoder. It reduces the completed cycles to 2.3 in simulation and 1.1 on hardware, and the probe locates the loss in the orientation of the nut, on which the re-grasp depends. The gap to the full method widens from 7.7 cycles in simulation to 8.9 cycles on hardware.
  \item[(iii)] Deterministic regression is not sufficient. Both regression students complete fewer than two cycles on average. Because (d) keeps the backbone and the chunking of the full method, the generative objective accounts for the difference.
\end{enumerate}
The students rank in the same order in simulation and on hardware.

\subsection{Generalization}\label{sec:geometry}

The training nut alone does not show how far the sim-to-real transfer reaches. The full method is therefore also evaluated on all twelve test objects (Figure~\ref{fig:nut-family}). Table~\ref{tab:geometry} reports ten trials per object: the M24, M30, and M36 hexagonal nuts, whose profile the demonstrations show, and the octagonal, rectangular, and round profiles of each size, which it has never seen. The study tests two kinds of generalization. Across sizes, the student must infer the nut size from the point cloud, since it receives no explicit size input. Across profiles, the question is whether the learned pinch-and-re-grasp strategy, which the demonstrations show only on hexagons, transfers to a different face topology.

The student completes all ten cycles in every trial on the three hexagonal nuts. The M30 nut shows the smallest lag, $-3.70\pm0.77\degree$ per cycle, and the M36 nut the largest, $-5.52\pm1.34\degree$. Among the unseen profiles, the octagonal and round nuts are also completed in every trial at all three sizes, and the round nut has the smallest lag at every size. On the rectangular profile, the M24 nut completes $3.0\pm1.2$ cycles, and the M30 and M36 nuts complete none. In the typical failure, the fingers turn the nut by $60\degree$ but do not establish contact with the next pair of faces in the following cycle, and the nut therefore stays at its first $60\degree$ position. This suggests that the octagonal and round profiles still present a graspable pair of surfaces after each $60\degree$ turn, whereas the rectangular profile, whose face pairs lie $90\degree$ apart, does not present a pair of faces at the learned re-grasp pose.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:exp-summary}

This chapter evaluated the student described in Chapter~\ref{ch:method} in simulation and, after sim-to-real transfer, on the physical system, over ten trials of up to ten threading cycles per condition. Five students were compared on the M24 hexagonal nut, the full method (e) and four ablations that each replace one of its design choices. The full method was then evaluated on twelve nuts of three sizes and four profiles.

In simulation, the full method completes all ten cycles in every trial with a cyclic error of $-1.77\pm1.02\degree$ and a ten-cycle error of $-17.7\pm3.23\degree$. Every ablation fails before the tenth cycle in every trial. For RQ1, the single-cycle student (a) completes 5.5 cycles in simulation and 5.2 on hardware. For RQ2, the ResNet student (b) completes 2.3 and 1.1 cycles, and its feature loses about half of the nut-orientation accuracy of the point-cloud feature. The regression students (c) and (d) complete fewer than two cycles in simulation.

For RQ3, the full method completes all ten cycles in every trial on hardware, with a cyclic error of $-4.21\pm1.20\degree$ and a ten-cycle error of $-42.10\pm4.37\degree$, and the deployed students keep their simulated ranking. It also completes all ten cycles in every trial on the M30 and M36 hexagonal nuts, whose sizes the demonstrations do not contain, and on the unseen octagonal and round profiles at all three sizes. The round nut shows the smallest lag at every size. On the rectangular profile, the student completes $3.0\pm1.2$ cycles on the M24 nut and none on the M30 and M36 nuts.
