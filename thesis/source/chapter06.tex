\chapter{Discussion}\label{ch:discussion}%

This chapter interprets the results of Chapters~\ref{ch:sim} and~\ref{ch:real}. Section~\ref{sec:disc-ablations} answers the three research questions of Section~\ref{sec:research-questions} as far as the available results allow. Section~\ref{sec:disc-online} explains why the deployed student is distilled offline rather than with online \ac{DAgger}, and Section~\ref{sec:disc-contracts} draws the lesson of the action and observation contracts. Section~\ref{sec:disc-assembly} returns to the assembly motivation and asks how far the demonstrated skill is from industrial threading. Section~\ref{sec:disc-limitations} states the limitations of the work.

\section{Interpretation per Research Question}\label{sec:disc-ablations}

\paragraph{Decomposition during data generation.}
All three research questions presuppose a teacher that produces complete multi-cycle demonstrations, so the teacher result comes first. \ac{PPO} learns the threading turn once the thread is constrained kinematically and the learned task is one turn from an engaged nut. The kinematic constraint makes the learning problem well posed: it ties axial progress to rotation, so the progress reward can only be earned by turning. The teacher also answers difficulty~(b) of Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-dexterity}: with the hand restricted to three commanded pinch degrees of freedom, it acquires the pinch grasp and keeps it under the turning load, and losing the grasp after it has latched ends the episode, which makes grasp retention a hard constraint (Section~\ref{sec:teacher}). Trained under initial-yaw, axial-height, and controller randomization, the retained teacher, chained into the hybrid teacher, completes ten consecutive cycles in all 32 candidate environments (Section~\ref{sec:sim-teacher}). The \ac{RL} teacher solves one turn, and the hybrid rollout extended it to ten consecutive cycles. The turn also needs no force sensing: the actor of the retained teacher observes no force channel. Hardware-equivalent force observations are implemented for teacher and student and remain to be evaluated (Section~\ref{sec:sim-robustness}).

\paragraph{RQ1: unified multi-cycle student.}
RQ1 tests the deployment side of difficulty~(c) of Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-horizon}. The full method completes all ten cycles in every simulated trial and lags the target by less than two degrees per cycle. The content of the data is the likely reason. In a single-cycle demonstration the nut is always grasped at its reset height, so a student trained on it never has to read the height from the point cloud. In a multi-cycle demonstration the nut descends by one sixth of a pitch per cycle, and the student learns to track it. The global progress scalar supports the multi-cycle student, but it does not supply the axial information. That information comes from the coverage of the axial state in the demonstrations. The single-cycle ablation (a) receives the same progress scalar and therefore isolates this effect; its result is \tbd. RQ1 thus tests the principle that unifying the complete cyclic behavior in one dataset, rather than invoking a per-cycle policy repeatedly, yields a policy that sustains consecutive cycles.

\paragraph{RQ2: student design.}
RQ2 has two parts, the visual representation and the action head, and each of the ablations (b) to (d) changes one of them. On the representation side, the student does not need to localize the nut to sub-millimeter accuracy. The engagement of the nut on the bolt and the compliance of the impedance controller absorb lateral error. What the student needs is the orientation of the nut faces for the re-grasp and the height of the nut for the approach. A representation that encodes position but not orientation would let the fingers close on the right spot with the wrong face alignment. The point-cloud encoder consumes the metric geometry of nut, bolt, and hand directly. It has about 60\,000 parameters and no pretrained image backbone. The encoder of the ten-cycle student was initialized from a pose-regression pretraining on rendered nut sweeps, which reached 5.9\,mm and $46\degree$ on held-out sweeps, and was then trained end to end with the flow objective and the auxiliary nut-position head (Section~\ref{sec:sim-vision}). The nut-pose accuracy of the trained student encoder was not measured; the pose probe of ablation (b) is designed to test whether its feature carries the face orientation. The image-space encoder of ablation (b) also receives depth and per-pixel coordinates, so (b) tests whether the geometric structure of the representation, rather than the depth input alone, decides the closed-loop result. Random point sampling adds an augmentation that the image encoder does not receive. The closed-loop ablation (b) and the pose probe quantify the difference; their results are \tbd.

On the head side, the chunked Transformer regression head (d) shares the backbone, the chunk length, and the temporal ensembling of the flow student, so the difference between (d) and (e) isolates the generative objective. Two mechanisms could favor the flow head. The first is multimodality of the labels. For near-identical observations, the dataset contains the teacher's nominal action in one demonstration and its recovery after an impulse in another. A squared-error head converges to their average, an action that neither demonstration contains, whereas the flow head samples one of them. The second is the structure of the prediction. The flow head predicts a velocity from a noisy chunk toward the data, a denoising problem that may be easier to fit than a direct regression from observation to chunk. Through the sixteen integration steps it refines all eight steps of the chunk jointly, with full attention among them. The recurrent regression head (c) additionally removes chunking. The results of (c) and (d) on the simulated dataset are \tbd.

\paragraph{RQ3: zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.}
RQ3 tests the transfer side of difficulty~(a) of Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-sim2real}. The student is trained on simulated data only, and its transfer rests on four properties of the pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:sim2real}). The calibrated camera model is meant to make the simulated and real point clouds geometrically comparable. The crop box removes the background, which is where rendered and real images differ most. Lighting, texture, and background randomization, together with image-space color augmentation, are meant to cover the remaining appearance variation of the points that enter the encoder. The shared Cartesian action path executes the student's commands through a controller that reproduces the simulated control law, and the teacher was trained under randomized gains and noisy pose inputs. The same geometric property that RQ2 relies on matters here: inside the crop box the point geometry is identical in both domains up to depth noise and calibration error, whereas an image-space encoder faces the appearance gap of the full image. The per-point colors still carry the appearance gap of nut, bolt, and hand. The approach requires no real-world data collection and no training on the robot. The hardware results that test it are \tbd.

\section{Offline versus Online Distillation}\label{sec:disc-online}

The project set out to distill the teacher online with \ac{DAgger}, as DextrAH-G and DextrAH-RGB do~\cite{Lum2024,Singh2025}, and it ends with an offline flow student. The comparison in Section~\ref{sec:sim-online} shows the difference. Depending on who controls the environment, the online recurrent students learn the approach, the turn, or the turn and the transition, and none completes more than two cycles. The evaluation ladder shows that they fit their labels and execute them correctly under teacher-forced observations. The offline flow student sustains ten cycles under its own observations, and three properties plausibly explain the difference. Its training data contain explicit recoveries from disturbances in every phase. Its chunked generative prediction with temporal ensembling smooths single-step errors. Its progress and phase conditions keep it synchronized with the physical cycle.

The advantage of \ac{DAgger}, labels on student-visited states, is obtained here in a different form. The impulses move the teacher into off-nominal states and record its recovery. The online flow variant cannot provide labels on student-visited states as implemented. Its chunked labels must be produced by the teacher alone, so it runs at $\beta=1$, obtains labels on teacher-visited states only, and reduces to online collection of the same data. The same constraint rules out the mixture schedules that the one-step recurrent student uses. An online variant on student-visited states would need multi-step teacher recovery fragments collected from student states.

The offline dataset has two further practical advantages. The ten-cycle dataset is rendered once under visual randomization and shared unchanged by the full method and ablations (b) to (d), so these comparisons differ only in the component under test. And one student trains in about thirty minutes.

\section{Action and Observation Contracts}\label{sec:disc-contracts}

The project implemented two action contracts and settled on the Cartesian one. The joint-PD contract was motivated by deployment convenience, because a joint-space command is the native interface of most robot drivers. Recorded joint references replayed at the physics rate do reproduce the teacher motion, with an arm-joint \acs{RMSE} of 0.0037\,rad (Section~\ref{sec:action-contract}). As a learning target, however, the Cartesian contract is the stronger choice. The joint-PD contract converts the teacher's bounded Cartesian action into seventeen physical-unit references at the physics rate and asks the student to emit one such reference per 15\,Hz step. This introduces a hold error that the teacher never produced. It mixes physical units with widely different scales in one regression target (Section~\ref{sec:action-contract}). It also removes the bolt-relative structure of the action, which expresses every target relative to the bolt tip. The Cartesian contract keeps the student in the teacher's own action space and executes it through the unchanged controller, including the action smoothing that the teacher was trained with. With the same demonstrations, it turns an equally good offline fit into closed-loop progress (Section~\ref{sec:sim-contract}). A student should therefore imitate the action the teacher produced, in the units the teacher produced it, and let the controller do the conversion it was designed for.

The observation contract follows the same logic. The student receives the previous filtered action, which the teacher also observes, the positions and velocities of the ten controlled joints, which the teacher actor does not observe, and two low-dimensional conditions that the demonstration provides without privileged state. The global progress scalar assumes a nominally timed trial. In the ten-cycle simulation run, the student's release times drift behind those of its source demonstration by 7.4\,s at the tenth cycle without affecting completion. The process-phase encoding keeps the student synchronized within each cycle, and the drift does not lead to a failed transition. For trials that run much longer than the demonstrations, the phase should be inferred from the observation history inside the policy rather than supplied as an external signal. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} returns to this as future work.

\section{What This Means for Robotic Assembly}\label{sec:disc-assembly}

The motivation of this thesis was assembly with threaded joints (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}). Measured against an industrial fastening step, the demonstrated skill covers the run-down of an engaged nut and leaves four gaps.

The first gap is the start of the task. Every trial starts with the nut already engaged on the bolt (Section~\ref{sec:thread-model}). In assembly the nut arrives loose and has to be seated on the thread first, which is a separate skill with a narrow entry configuration. The project has a pickup teacher that grasps and lifts a loose nut, with 99.8\,\% pinch success on the \ac{FR3} in simulation, and a full-task variant of the hybrid teacher that prepends it (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-fulltask}). In the single-seed action-contract study, however, a privileged student held the engagement goal in only 2 of 10 trials (Table~\ref{tab:contract}). Engagement is therefore the least solved part of the complete task.

The second gap is the end of the task. A trial ends after ten turns of $60\degree$, which advance the nut by one and two thirds of a pitch, 5.0\,mm on the M24 nut. The nut is never seated against a part, and it is never tightened to a preload.
% TODO(cite): tightening to a specified torque or preload as the final step of threaded-joint assembly.
The simulated thread resists the turn only with a smoothed friction torque whose Coulomb term is $2\times10^{-3}$\,N\,m, which models a free-running nut. The actor observes no force, and the reward penalizes excess wrist force. A tightening phase would demand that the pinch transmit a much larger torque and that the policy sense when the joint is tight, which neither the thread model nor the observation contract supports.

The third gap is the grasp. The student executes one strategy, a thumb--index pinch with three commanded hand joints, learned on the face topology of a hexagon. People change the grasp as the resistance grows (Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-dexterity}), and a tightening phase would likely require such a change. The held-out octagonal, rectangular, and round profiles of Section~\ref{sec:real-geometry} test how far the single strategy carries; their results are \tbd.

The fourth gap is cycle time. The ten-cycle student completed its simulated trial in 1072 control ticks at 15\,Hz, about 71\,s, or roughly seven seconds per $60\degree$ cycle (Section~\ref{sec:sim-vision}). The phase-conditioned privileged student showed the same cycle durations of 6.7 to 7.1\,s (Section~\ref{sec:sim-conditioning}). In the demonstrations, release and return take a nearly fixed time in every cycle, since their scripted waypoints are interpolated over 0.7\,s per segment and 1.0\,s for the final return. Because each cycle turns the nut by $60\degree$, a full revolution takes six cycles, which is far slower than the continuous rotation of a powered spindle.

These gaps do not erase what the result shows. In simulation, a learned policy controlled a dexterous hand through ten cycles of grasp, turn, release, and return, and it re-grasped the nut at the angle and height that the previous cycle left it. It perceives the nut from one fixed camera, and it is trained without any real data. The same hand also picks up a loose nut in simulation. Whether this versatility outweighs the speed of a dedicated tool depends on the application, and the remaining gaps define the work that separates the two (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:disc-limitations}

Section~\ref{sec:disc-assembly} described the task-level gaps to industrial threading; this section states the limitations of the method and of the evidence. The demonstrated behavior is a pinch-grasp re-grasp strategy, and the student has no mechanism to discover a different grasp for a profile that requires one. The demonstrations start at the nominal nut height and cover only the 5.0\,mm of axial travel of ten cycles, and the global progress clock is defined over the nominal trial length. The teacher's thread model is a kinematic simplification. In simulation the nut therefore cannot jam or cross-thread, and the thread resists only through a small friction torque on the revolute joint. The randomization ranges that are meant to cover the resulting sim-to-real gap have not yet been fitted to measured statistics of the physical sensor. The camera calibration has a translation fit \acs{RMSE} of 27\,mm and a rotation fit \acs{RMSE} of $3.5\degree$ (Section~\ref{sec:real-setup}); it is measured but not deployment-validated, and the dependence of the student on calibration error has not been quantified. The evaluation uses ten trials per condition and reports means and standard deviations without confidence intervals. Several design ablations in Chapter~\ref{ch:sim} are matched single rollouts at one seed, which establish the direction of an effect but not its variance. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} summarizes what the thesis achieved and turns these gaps and limitations into future work.
