\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

Cyclic nut threading is decomposed into the ordered phase sequence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phases}
  \mathcal{S}=\big(\underbrace{\text{approach},\;\text{grasp},\;\text{rotate},\;\text{goal reaching}}_{\MRL:\;\piT},\;\underbrace{\text{release},\;\text{return and reset}}_{\Mscript:\;\kappa}\big).
\end{equation}
The active phase at control step $t$ is $m_t\in\Mset=\MRL\cup\Mscript$. The four phases in $\MRL$ are executed by a learned teacher policy $\piT$; the two transition phases in $\Mscript$ are executed by scripted controllers $\kappa_m$. The split is a property of the data-generation stage only: the deployed student receives neither the phase label as a switch nor separate networks per phase.

Let $K$ be the required number of cycles, $\Delta\varphi^{*}$ the desired clockwise rotation per cycle, and $\varphi_k$ the unwrapped nut angle about the bolt axis after the goal-reaching phase of cycle $k$, with $\varphi_0$ the angle at the start of the trial. The cyclic tracking error, the episodic tracking error, and the tracking cost of a trajectory $\tau$ are defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:errors}
  e_k=\varphi_k-\varphi_{k-1}-\Delta\varphi^{*},\qquad
  e_{\mathrm{ep}}=\varphi_K-\varphi_0-K\Delta\varphi^{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}e_k,\qquad
  E(\tau)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}e_k^{2}+\beta_{E}\,e_{\mathrm{ep}}^{2}.
\end{equation}
Positive errors indicate overshoot and negative errors lag; the cost squares them so that overshoot in one cycle cannot cancel lag in another, and the weight $\beta_E$ trades accumulated drift against cycle-wise accuracy. Let $C(\tau)\in\{0,\dots,K\}$ count the cycles completed before failure or timeout and let $\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}$ denote the policies that execute the phases of $\mathcal{S}$ in order in every cycle. The research problem is to find the policy that chains all phases with maximum completion success and, among those, minimum tracking cost:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
  \Pi_{\mathrm{succ}}=\argmax_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}}\;\Pr_{\tau\sim p_\pi}\!\left[C(\tau)=K\right],\qquad
  \pi^{*}=\argmin_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{succ}}}\;\E_{\tau\sim p_\pi}\!\left[E(\tau)\mid C(\tau)=K\right].
\end{equation}
Unless a study states otherwise, $K=10$ and $\Delta\varphi^{*}=60\degree$, which corresponds to one face of a hexagonal nut per cycle. The policy $\pi^{*}$ is learned from fixed hybrid-teacher demonstrations rather than by optimizing \eqref{eq:objective} on hardware, because on-robot optimization of a contact-rich cyclic task is neither safe nor sample-efficient.

The task is partially observable at deployment, so it is a \ac{POMDP}: the \ac{MDP} of Section~\ref{sec:rl-background} is extended by an observation model $O(\vec{z}_t\mid\vec{x}_t)$, and the policy acts on observations $\vec{z}_t$ instead of states~\cite{Sutton2018}. The simulator state $\vec{x}_t$ contains the nut pose, the contact state, and the controller parameters; the student observes only an aligned \acs{RGBD} image, its proprioception, and two low-dimensional conditioning signals introduced in Section~\ref{sec:student}. A single observation is then no longer sufficient to act optimally, because the information that disambiguates the state may lie in earlier observations. The teacher--student structure of the method separates the two difficulties of this \ac{POMDP}: the teacher solves the control problem with privileged state, and the student learns to perceive and to reproduce the teacher's behavior from sensor data.

\section{Simulated and Physical System}\label{sec:system}

The method is trained in simulation on a model of the physical hand--arm system and deployed on that system. This section describes the platform, the thread model that makes learning in simulation tractable, and the action contract that teacher, student, and robot share.

\subsection{Platform}\label{sec:platform}

The robot is a seven-DoF torque-controlled \ac{FR3} arm with a five-fingered Inspire RH56DFX right hand mounted on its flange (Figure~\ref{fig:platform}). The hand has twelve finger joints driven by six motors. The non-thumb fingers each have one actuated flexion joint with a mechanically coupled distal joint, and the thumb has an abduction joint and a coupled flexion chain. Only three hand degrees of freedom are commanded by the policy: thumb abduction, thumb flexion, and index flexion, which together form the pinch used for threading. The remaining fingers hold a curled posture. This restriction narrows the solution space of the dexterous hand to one grasp strategy, as difficulty~(b) of Section~\ref{sec:problem-setup} requires. The nut is threaded onto a bolt that stands on a 5\,cm pedestal on a 60\,cm by 80\,cm bench. The robot base is mounted at tabletop height, 46.5\,cm from the near table edge, so that the reach envelope lies above the work surface. A fixed \acs{RGBD} camera provides the observation; its extrinsic calibration relative to the robot base was measured on the physical cell and is reproduced in simulation.

\subsection{Simplified Thread Simulation}\label{sec:thread-model}

The thread model is the first answer to difficulty~(a) of Section~\ref{sec:problem-setup}: the skill is learned in simulation, so the simulated thread has to behave like a mating thread in each of the hundreds of parallel environments that \ac{PPO} needs. The default Isaac~Sim collision pipeline does not provide this: with \ac{SDF} mesh colliders on nut and bolt, the simulated thread contact is not accurate enough to reproduce a mating thread. A kinematic thread model also gives the policy a well-posed learning signal: with \ac{SDF} thread colliders, axial progress of the nut can be obtained by pressing it along the bolt axis, whereas a kinematic coupling makes axial progress a consequence of rotation alone, so that progress can only be earned by turning. The thread model used for teacher training therefore replaces the thread contact by a kinematic coupling, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:thread-model}. The nut is attached to the bolt by a passive revolute joint about the bolt axis and a coaxial, position-controlled prismatic joint. With prismatic coordinate $d_t$, unwrapped and direction-corrected revolute coordinate $\theta_t$, thread pitch $\rho$, and the index $b_k$ of the step at which the current turn started, the prismatic target is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thread}
  d^{*}_t-d_{b_k}=-\frac{\rho}{2\pi}\left(\theta_t-\theta_{b_k}\right),
\end{equation}
so that one revolution produces one pitch of axial travel. The prismatic joint tracks $d^{*}_t$ with a stiff drive (stiffness $2\times10^{5}$\,N/m, damping 200\,N\,s/m, force limit 1000\,N), whose finite stiffness permits small deviations from the ideal helix while the revolute--prismatic pair constrains lateral motion and keeps the nut near the commanded path. The finger--nut contact forces remain fully simulated: the policy must physically grip and turn the nut. Thread resistance is modeled on the revolute joint by a smoothed Coulomb and viscous friction torque
\begin{equation}\label{eq:friction}
  \bar{\tau}_{f,t}=-\tau_c\tanh\!\left(\frac{\omega_t}{\omega_s}\right)-c_v\,\omega_t,
\end{equation}
with revolute velocity $\omega_t$, Coulomb torque $\tau_c=2\times10^{-3}$\,N\,m, viscous coefficient $c_v=2\times10^{-4}$\,N\,m\,s/rad, and smoothing speed $\omega_s=0.1$\,rad/s. The nut carrier has a mass of 1\,g, and the episode ends when the nut is unscrewed one full turn above its reset pose, and the prismatic limits keep a 0.2\,mm margin above the physical lower stop. The pitches are 3.0\,mm, 3.5\,mm, and 4.0\,mm for the M24, M30, and M36 nuts. The model has one deliberate simplification beyond contact: the nut is initialized already engaged with the bolt. Thread engagement from a loose nut is a separate skill with a narrow entry configuration, and it is outside the scope of this thesis. The simplified contact opens a gap between the simulated and the physical thread, which the transfer design of Section~\ref{sec:sim2real} has to bridge.

\subsection{Action Contract and Controller}\label{sec:action-contract}

The teacher acts in a 9-D vector $\vec{a}_t\in[-1,1]^9$, which is decoded into a Cartesian target for the hand's grasp frame and three pinch-joint targets. The student and the scripted transitions use the same decoder (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-rollout}). The grasp frame has its origin at the midpoint of the thumb and index fingertips. Its $x$ axis is the thumb-to-index direction, along which the pinch closes, and its $z$ axis is the approach direction, fixed at reset in flange coordinates. The first three components set a position offset relative to an action frame, which is the bolt tip during threading and the held nut during pickup. Because the target is expressed relative to the bolt, the controller draws the grasped nut toward the bolt wherever nut and robot are, and a zero action does not hold the current pose. The next three components set roll, pitch, and yaw around a neutral grasp orientation, and the last three command thumb abduction, thumb flexion, and index flexion. The raw action is smoothed by an exponential moving average, and the resulting target is clipped to a bounded step from the current pose, so that one command cannot demand a large jump. Table~\ref{tab:controller} lists the scales and limits.

The Cartesian target is tracked by the operational-space impedance controller of \eqref{eq:osc}, inherited from Factory and FORGE~\cite{Narang2022,Noseworthy2025}. The stiffness matrix $\mat{K}_p$ is diagonal in world axes, so the impedance ellipsoid is aligned with the vertical bolt axis, and $\mat{K}_d=2\sqrt{\mat{K}_p}$ damps every axis critically. The posture term pulls the arm toward its reset configuration through the dynamically consistent nullspace projector of Section~\ref{sec:osc-background}, so that posture torques do not accelerate the grasp frame, rather than the damped kinematic projector of the libfranka example controller, which decouples the two only statically (Section~\ref{sec:real-setup}). In simulation, the Jacobian $\mat{J}$ and the arm mass matrix $\mat{M}$ come from the PhysX articulation. $\mat{J}$ is the flange Jacobian shifted to the grasp point by the flange-to-grasp-point lever arm $\vec{r}$, $\mat{J}_v=\mat{J}_{v,\mathrm{flange}}-[\vec{r}]_\times\mat{J}_{\omega,\mathrm{flange}}$, and $\mat{M}$ is the arm block of the generalized mass matrix, which includes the inertia of the attached hand. Gravity is disabled for the arm links, and the controller output is applied directly as arm joint torque; the finger joints are position-controlled by the simulator's implicit \acs{PD} drives. On the physical \ac{FR3}, the same law runs at 1\,kHz with $\mat{J}$ and $\mat{M}$ from the libfranka robot model and gravity compensated by libfranka (Section~\ref{sec:real-setup}). Table~\ref{tab:controller} compares the two implementations.

Each force channel of the teacher (Section~\ref{sec:teacher}) has a counterpart on the physical system. The joint torques are the reaction torques that PhysX projects onto the arm joint axes, not the commanded torques, so contact loads appear in them. Because the simulated arm carries no gravity load, their counterpart on the \ac{FR3} is the external-torque estimate \texttt{tau\_ext\_hat\_filtered} that libfranka derives from the joint-torque sensors, not the raw sensor torque. The fingertip forces are the pad contact forces along the pad normal, the only component that the fingertip sensors of the hand measure. The student observes neither channel (Section~\ref{sec:student}).

The shared Cartesian contract is a design decision, because the project also implemented a joint-space alternative. In this 17-D impedance joint-PD contract, the student emits seven arm position references, seven arm velocity references, and three pinch positions, which a joint-space impedance controller tracks as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pd}
  \vec{\tau}=\vec{g}(\vec{q})+\mat{K}_p^{\mathrm{hw}}\left(\vec{q}_{\mathrm{ref}}-\vec{q}\right)+\mat{K}_d^{\mathrm{hw}}\left(\dot{\vec{q}}_{\mathrm{ref}}-\dot{\vec{q}}\right),
\end{equation}
with gravity compensation $\vec{g}(\vec{q})$ owned by the controller. Teacher labels are converted by solving the Cartesian controller output for the equivalent joint references, $\vec{q}_{\mathrm{ref}}=\vec{q}+(\mat{K}_p^{\mathrm{hw}})^{-1}\vec{\tau}_{\mathrm{OSC}}$ and $\dot{\vec{q}}_{\mathrm{ref}}=\dot{\vec{q}}$. Replayed at the physics rate on the earlier humanoid-arm platform, these references reproduce the teacher motion with an arm-joint \acs{RMSE} of 0.0037\,rad. As a learning target, however, the contract has two drawbacks. The equivalent joint target changes within the eight physics steps over which one 15\,Hz command is held, so a zero-order hold introduces a target error of 0.010 to 0.015\,rad, and the 17 commands mix physical units with widely different scales. All reported results use the Cartesian contract, and the 17-D contract is retained as an ablation (Section~\ref{sec:sim-contract}).

\section{Privileged Teacher Training}\label{sec:teacher}

The privileged teacher answers difficulty~(b) of Section~\ref{sec:problem-setup}. It learns to approach the nut, acquire the pinch grasp, keep it under load while turning, and stop at the target, all through the commanded hand and arm degrees of freedom of Section~\ref{sec:platform}. Because it observes privileged simulator state, it solves this control problem without having to perceive the nut (Section~\ref{sec:problem}).

The teacher for the learned phases in $\MRL$ is a recurrent policy, which keeps a hidden state $\vec{h}_t$ that integrates the history of past observations. It is trained with \ac{PPO} (Section~\ref{sec:rl-background}) in an asymmetric actor--critic~\cite{Pinto2017}, in which the critic observes more of the simulator state than the actor. Because the critic is discarded after training, this additional state shapes the gradients without entering the policy. The actor observation $\vec{z}^{\pi}_t=g_\pi(\vec{x}_t,\vec{\xi}_t)$ selects the actor-visible components of the simulator state and applies observation noise $\vec{\xi}_t$. Its 46 dimensions form four groups. The pose group holds the fingertip (grasp-frame) position relative to the bolt frame and its quaternion, the end-effector linear and angular velocity obtained by finite differences, the fingertip position relative to the held nut, and the nut quaternion. The task group holds the position and quaternion error to the current turning target and a grasp-phase flag that indicates whether the proper grasp has latched. The force group holds two fingertip forces and seven arm joint torques. The last group is the previous filtered action. In the fingertip quaternion the $w$ and $z$ components are zeroed, in the angular velocity the $x$ and $y$ components, and in the previous action the roll and pitch entries.

The force group mirrors the sensors of the physical system. Each fingertip channel is the flexion force that the thumb or index pad exerts along the pad normal of its calibrated tip frame. It is computed from the total contact force on the pad, regardless of the body in contact, because the physical fingertip sensors observe neither the other force components nor which body presses on them. Each torque channel is the joint torque that the simulator projects onto one arm joint axis, divided by the effort limit of that joint, 87\,N\,m for joints one to four and 12\,N\,m for joints five to seven. Position terms receive Gaussian noise with 0.25\,mm standard deviation per axis, orientation terms a random-axis rotation of 0.1\degree, and the bolt frame a per-episode offset of 1\,mm, which models calibration error. The force group receives a per-episode gain and offset and per-step noise, which model sensor calibration and noise (Table~\ref{tab:dr}). The critic observes clean values of the same quantities, partly in absolute rather than relative form, and extends them with the arm joint positions, the complete nut and bolt pose, and the sampled controller gains, smoothing factor, and thresholds; it has 76 dimensions. Table~\ref{tab:teacher-obs} in the appendix lists the exact components and widths.

Actor and central critic share the same architecture: a two-layer \ac{LSTM}~\cite{Hochreiter1997} with 1024 units per layer and layer normalization, followed by a 512--128--64 \acs{MLP} with ELU activations. Recurrent sequences span 128 control steps, the rollout horizon is 256 steps, and the actor parameterizes a diagonal Gaussian over the normalized action with a state-dependent standard deviation. The teacher is optimized with the shaped reward
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward}
\begin{aligned}
  r_t&=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{R}} w^{r}_{i}R_{i,t}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}} w^{p}_{j}P_{j,t},\\
  \mathcal{R}&=\{\text{center},\text{face},\text{grasp},\text{hold},\text{turn},\text{target}\},\\
  \mathcal{P}&=\{\text{mag},\Delta a,\text{align},\text{force},\text{settle},\text{wrench},\text{clear},\text{contact}\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The reward terms follow the three phases of the learned behavior. During approach, $R^{\mathrm{center}}$ rewards motion of the grasp center toward the nut center and $R^{\mathrm{face}}$ rewards motion of the thumb and index pads toward the two designated opposing faces of the nut. Both pay $30$ times the newly achieved fraction of the initial gap and stop paying once the grasp has latched, so repeated motion cannot accumulate reward. A proper grasp is defined as simultaneous thumb and index contact within 1\,cm of the designated faces with a pinch line nearly perpendicular to the nut axis. $R^{\mathrm{grasp}}$ pays a one-time bonus of $150$ when the proper grasp has held for ten consecutive steps, which latches the grasp. $R^{\mathrm{hold}}$ pays $0.2$ in every step in which the proper grasp holds with both pads pressing with at least 1\,N, before and after the latch. A complete 30\,s episode of holding pays 90, less than the target bonus, so holding without turning is worth less than threading. During turning, $R^{\mathrm{turn}}$ pays $60$ times the newly achieved fraction of the keypoint distance to the helical target pose that remained when the grasp latched, and pays only while the proper grasp remains live. At completion, $R^{\mathrm{target}}$ pays $250$ once, on the step at which the episode succeeds.

The penalties discourage large commands ($P^{\mathrm{mag}}$, weight $10^{-3}$ on the normalized commanded translation and yaw), abrupt action changes ($P^{\Delta a}$, $0.1$), misalignment of the hand frame with the nut frame ($P^{\mathrm{align}}$, $0.02$), excess grip force ($P^{\mathrm{force}}$, $0.05$ per newton of summed thumb and index pad contact force above a per-episode threshold drawn from $U(5,10)$\,N), and residual rotation near the target ($P^{\mathrm{settle}}$, $0.25\,\omega_t^2$ from $50\degree$ onward). $P^{\mathrm{wrench}}$ prices the wrench that the thread joint transmits to the nut, except the torque about the bolt axis that threading requires. Lateral force above 2\,N costs 0.05 per newton, axial force above 5\,N costs 0.05 per newton, and tilting torque above 0.05\,N\,m costs 1.0 per newton metre. The weight of $P^{\mathrm{wrench}}$ rises with the initial-yaw curriculum described below, from zero at its first level to one at its full range, so that the policy learns to thread before the force limits apply. $P^{\mathrm{clear}}$ and $P^{\mathrm{contact}}$ keep the arm off the table: the first is $2.0$ times the squared normalized violation of a 3\,cm clearance margin between arm and table, and the second $0.05$ per newton of arm--table contact force above 2\,N.

Two settings of the simulated hand bound the grasp itself. The effort of the three pinch joints is limited to 0.5\,N\,m, down from the uniform 20\,N\,m of the hand drives. A replay of a policy trained without this limit gave median pad forces of about 43\,N at the thumb and 38\,N at the index. Limits of 1.0\,N\,m and 0.3\,N\,m reduced them to about 27/21\,N and 12/7\,N, whereas 0.1\,N\,m let the grip collapse, and 0.5\,N\,m lies between the two working values. The thumb and index pad colliders use a friction coefficient of 1.5 instead of the robot-wide 0.75, which the simulator averages with the nut's 1.0 to a pad--nut coefficient of 1.25.

Each episode samples a target turn $\theta^{*}\sim U(55\degree,65\degree)$ and builds the observed target pose at $\theta^{*}$, so the policy cannot settle on one fixed stopping angle and has to read the target from its observation. An episode succeeds when the clockwise rotation lies within $2.5\degree$ of $\theta^{*}$ under a live proper grasp for eight consecutive steps (0.53\,s), so overshoot fails. Success ends the episode, so the target bonus is paid once. An earlier formulation paid the bonus over the first steps of every stretch inside the success region and was exploited by rocking the nut back and forth across the success angle. Once the initial-yaw curriculum is complete, the required hold grows by one step per promotion up to twelve steps (0.8\,s). Each promotion requires a success rate of at least 50\,\% over completed episodes and at least five episode lengths since the previous change. An episode also terminates when the nut leaves the bolt by more than 3\,cm, tilts by more than $60\degree$, or releases the thread, when the grasp is lost after it has latched, and at the 30\,s time limit. The grasp-loss rule turns grasp retention into a hard constraint rather than a shaped term.

Robustness is obtained by domain randomization of the nut mass ($\pm5$\,g), the bolt friction (static coefficient in $[0.25,1.25]$, assigned once per environment), the controller parameters of Section~\ref{sec:action-contract}, the observations, and the initial state; Table~\ref{tab:dr} lists the ranges. The pose noise is small in absolute terms, a quarter of a millimeter and a tenth of a degree, because the actor's inputs are relative poses that the student will later infer from a calibrated camera. The per-episode bolt-frame offset of one millimeter is the term that models calibration error and forces the teacher to close the loop on the nut rather than on the bolt frame. The initial nut rotation is randomized by an automatic curriculum: the yaw range starts at zero and expands by $5\degree$ whenever the success rate over completed episodes reaches 50\,\%, up to $\pm30\degree$, with at least five episode lengths between expansions. The initial axial height is sampled uniformly up to 20\,mm below the nominal spawn while reserving room for the largest target turn of $65\degree$.

\section{Hybrid Teacher and Demonstration Generation}\label{sec:hybrid}

The privileged teacher turns the nut once. The hybrid teacher answers the data side of difficulty~(c) of Section~\ref{sec:problem-setup}: it chains the learned turn with scripted release and return, so that each phase is specified by the mechanism that specifies it most simply, and it rolls the chain out in simulation to produce complete multi-cycle demonstrations. This section describes the cyclic rollout, its full-task variant, the disturbance augmentation, and the dataset. It also collects the design choices that answer the transfer side of difficulty~(a) (Section~\ref{sec:sim2real}), because most of them are fixed when the demonstrations are rendered.

\subsection{Cyclic Teacher Rollout in Simulation}\label{sec:hybrid-rollout}

Let $m_t\in\Mset$ denote the active phase, $\vec{x}_t$ the full simulator state, $\vec{z}^{\pi}_t$ the noisy actor observation, and $\vec{h}_t$ the actor's recurrent state. During rollout the teacher is queried for its deterministic mean action $\bar{\pi}_{\mathrm{T}}(\vec{z}^{\pi}_t,\vec{h}_t)=\E_{\vec{a}\sim\piT(\cdot\mid\vec{z}^{\pi}_t,\vec{h}_t)}[\vec{a}]$. The hybrid teacher is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hybrid}
  \piHT(\vec{x}_t,m_t)=
  \begin{cases}
    \bar{\pi}_{\mathrm{T}}(\vec{z}^{\pi}_t,\vec{h}_t), & m_t\in\MRL,\\
    \kappa_{m_t}(\vec{x}_t), & m_t\in\Mscript,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_{m_t}$ is the scripted controller of the active transition phase. Both branches produce commands in the same filtered, bolt-relative Cartesian action space, so the recorded label is a single 9-D signal throughout the cycle; the scripted Cartesian targets are inverse-decoded through the action mapping of Section~\ref{sec:action-contract}, and all labels are the filtered target divided by a fixed per-component scale of $[3,4,16,3,3,3,2,2,2]$, which bounds the scripted transitions to $[-1,1]$, and the executing adapter compensates the action smoothing analytically so that the controller receives exactly the requested target. This unified label space is what makes the demonstration a coherent supervision signal for one student: at no step does the label change meaning or units.

Release is triggered when the clockwise nut rotation since the start of the cycle comes within $5\degree$ of the cycle's target turn, at $55\degree$ for the nominal $60\degree$ turn; no grasp condition is checked at release. Four Cartesian waypoints follow: the first opens the thumb and index while the arm holds its pose, and the next three retract the hand outward along the ray from the nut center through the grasp center and raise it. A final return segment brings the hand back to the initial grasp pose. Each waypoint segment is interpolated over 0.7\,s and the final return over 1.0\,s; the return is physically gated and counts as complete only when the grasp frame is within 5\,mm and $5\degree$ of the reset frame and the hand joints within 0.15\,rad of their reset values, with a 16\,s timeout that marks the cycle as a transition failure. The waypoints were authored once by a collision-safe retargeting from a captured release pose. While the hand is away from the nut the thread joint is held, so the nut cannot drift between cycles. On a completed return the turning reference is rebased without resetting physics. The coordinator measures the residual by which the total nut rotation since the start of the first cycle misses the nearest multiple of $60\degree$. It sets the next target turn to $60\degree$ minus this residual, clamped to the trained range $[55\degree,65\degree]$ of target turns, so that a short turn is made up in the next cycle and the nut stays on the $60\degree$ grid of the hexagon. The frozen threading policy receives the nut orientation with whole $60\degree$ steps removed, which the hexagonal nut cannot distinguish, so that the residual remains visible within the $\pm30\degree$ initial-yaw range of training; its recurrent state and action history are reseeded. Because the simulated nut quaternion changes sign whenever the unwrapped thread angle passes $\pm180\degree$, the target-error quaternion is kept in the hemisphere $w\geq0$ that the teacher saw in training. The physical nut pose is not reset, so the demonstration contains the axial advance of the nut over ten cycles. The teacher population was first qualified for one period only; repeated cycles are a property of the hybrid rollout, not of the \ac{RL} teacher alone. Figure~\ref{fig:datacollection} shows the coordinator that sequences one cycle.

Two details of the coordinator matter for the quality of the labels. First, the scripted transitions never write physics state: a transition may request that the thread joint be held while the hand is away, but it repairs nothing by teleporting the nut, so every recorded frame is a physically consistent consequence of the recorded commands; an earlier implementation that snapped the nut back at the policy rate produced visible reversals of the nut at release that the student would have imitated. Second, the release gate is physical rather than timed: release is decided from the measured nut rotation, and the return is validated against the reset frame, so the phase labels in the recording coincide with physical events. Both properties allow the recorded phase to serve as a conditioning signal at deployment. There, the return is validated against the reset grasp pose with the same tolerances, while the release, for which the physical cell has no nut-angle sensor, is derived from the measured yaw of the grasp frame as a proxy for the nut rotation.

\subsection{Full-Task Variant with Pickup and Handover}\label{sec:hybrid-fulltask}

The hybrid teacher also has a full-task variant that prepends the pickup expert and a scripted pickup-to-threading handover (open the hand, move to the threading reset pose, close, settle, with phase durations of 0.4, 1.5, 0.4, and 0.25\,s) to the cyclic threading teacher. The resulting demonstrations start from a loose nut on the table. Both variants and their single-phase special cases (pickup only, one threading turn, cyclic threading) are selected by one training-environment option and expose the same supervision contract, so that a student trained on either variant can be evaluated with the same tools. Because thread engagement is outside the scope of this thesis (Section~\ref{sec:thread-model}), the reported threading results use the cyclic variant from an engaged nut.

\subsection{Disturbance Augmentation}\label{sec:impulses}

Twenty demonstrations contain too little variation for a student that must recover from its own deviations. Trajectory diversity is therefore increased at collection time by applying random impulses to the arm: in every contiguous teacher-phase segment, that is, in every policy, waypoint-following, and return segment of every cycle, one impulse with a magnitude of 0.05 to 0.25\,N\,s is applied to a random arm link between 0.05 and 0.20\,s after the segment begins, giving 30 impulses per ten-cycle demonstration. The teacher, which is a closed-loop controller, reacts to the disturbance, and the recorded recovery is part of the label. The teacher is queried for its mean action and controls every step ($\beta=1$), so each recovery segment is the teacher's own response to the state that precedes it. Besides the impulses, the demonstrations vary through the task randomization that remains active during collection (Section~\ref{sec:dataset}). Demonstrations in which the teacher fails to complete all cycles are rejected before materialization; the retained set consists only of explicitly successful episodes.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer}\label{sec:sim2real}

The student is trained on the simulated demonstrations alone, $\Dset=\Dsim$, and deployed on the physical robot without real-world training data. Section~\ref{sec:thread-model} answered the simulation side of difficulty~(a); this subsection answers its transfer side. The simplified thread model and the rendered images open a gap in contact and perception between the two domains, and four properties of the pipeline are designed to carry the student across it. RQ3 asks whether they suffice, and Chapter~\ref{ch:real} tests it.

The first is a shared camera model. The extrinsic pose of the physical camera relative to the robot base is measured on the physical cell by an eye-to-hand calibration, and the calibrated pose and intrinsics are used to render the simulated camera; the physical $640\times480$ stream is center-cropped to $640\times360$ and downscaled by two, and the simulated camera renders $320\times180$ images directly with the intrinsics of that crop scaled by one half. Simulated and real point clouds are therefore expressed in the same frame and have the same resolution and field of view.

The second is a metric representation. The student encodes a point cloud back-projected from the depth image (Section~\ref{sec:student}), not the image itself. Points are kept only inside a fixed crop box around the workspace and within a valid depth range, so that the background, which differs most between the rendered and the physical scene, never enters the representation, and the geometry of nut, bolt, and hand is identical in both domains up to depth noise and calibration error. Random sampling of 4096 points per frame during training acts as an additional augmentation of the point set.

The third is visual domain randomization at collection time. The demonstrations are rendered with randomized dome lighting, tabletop texture, nut and bolt textures, floor texture, and image background in the style of DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025}, each component switchable individually. Because the offline loader cannot regenerate scene appearance from stored images, this variation is recorded into the dataset once and reused unchanged by every student trained on it. During training, the stored RGB images are further perturbed by saturation, contrast, and brightness factors in $[0.5,1.5]$, hue shifts in $[-0.15,0.15]$, and motion blur with probability 0.1. The depth channel is not modified by the offline trainer; the encoder pretraining of Section~\ref{sec:student} implements an optional depth-corruption model of a commodity \acs{RGBD} sensor with Gaussian and range-dependent axial noise, low-frequency spatial noise, millimeter quantization, dropout at depth discontinuities, correlated missing regions, and uniform dropout; the retained encoder was pretrained on noise-free depth.

The fourth is a shared action path. The student imitates the bounded, bolt-relative Cartesian target that the controller received during the demonstrations, and on the physical robot this action is executed by the Cartesian impedance controller that reimplements the simulated control law (Section~\ref{sec:action-contract}). The teacher was trained under randomized controller gains, smoothing factors, and dead zones and under noise on every pose and force input (Table~\ref{tab:dr}), and these randomizations stay active while the demonstrations are collected. The demonstrations therefore pair each commanded target with a range of controller responses and sensing errors rather than with one nominal response.

\subsection{Dataset Format}\label{sec:dataset}

The demonstrations are collected from the M24 teacher of Section~\ref{sec:teacher} with two training mechanisms switched off: success does not end the episode, and the target turn is not sampled but follows the $60\degree$ grid of Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-rollout}. Every environment starts from the nominal pose, without initial yaw and with the nut at the top of the thread, so that ten $60\degree$ turns, 1.67 revolutions or 5\,mm of axial travel on the 3\,mm pitch, fit on the bolt. All other randomization of Table~\ref{tab:dr} remains active, and an episode may last up to 200\,s.

Each demonstration is materialized as one episode file with aligned 15\,Hz samples. A sample holds the RGB image and the metric depth image at $320\times180$ pixels, rendered with the intrinsics of the centered $640\times360$ crop of the calibrated $640\times480$ sensor scaled by one half; the 29-D proprioception, consisting of the ten controlled joint positions, their velocities, and the previous filtered 9-D action; the executed 9-D command; the active process phase; the nut pose for privileged ablations and auxiliary supervision; and the timestamp. The loader derives the normalized task progress from the timestamps. The recorder stores the 17-D joint-PD equivalent of every command alongside, so that both action contracts can be trained from one collection. Episode files contain no reset rows, so no chunk spans a physical reset, chunks that touch a placeholder command are dropped, phase boundaries are retained so that the student learns the transitions, and terminal chunks are padded with the final valid command so that the last observations of an episode remain conditioning states. The retained M24 dataset contains 20 successful ten-cycle demonstrations collected from 32 parallel environments, with \tbd{} frames in total (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}).

\section{Student Policy Distillation by Conditional Flow Matching}\label{sec:distillation}

The student answers the deployment side of difficulty~(c) of Section~\ref{sec:problem-setup}. The hybrid teacher cannot be deployed, because both its learned policy and its scripted controllers act on privileged simulator state \eqref{eq:hybrid}. The student replaces it with one visuomotor policy that executes every phase of every cycle from sensor data, without a phase switch or separate networks. RQ1 asks whether this unified student sustains consecutive cycles better than a single-cycle policy invoked repeatedly. RQ2 asks which student design distills the demonstrations best; the alternative encoders of Section~\ref{sec:student} and the regression baselines of Section~\ref{sec:baselines} are its comparisons.

\subsection{From Behavior Cloning to Online Distillation for Cyclic Tasks}\label{sec:why-dagger}

Student learning is posed as supervised imitation from the fixed dataset $\Dset$. Section~\ref{sec:il-background} explained why plain behavior cloning is fragile over long horizons: the student is evaluated on the states that its own errors produce. For cyclic threading the mechanism is concrete. A student that grasps a millimeter too high applies a slightly different torque, the nut advances a little less, and after a few cycles the fingers close on the edge of the nut instead of on its faces. Three measures counter this compounding in the present method, and they are complementary. First, the demonstrations themselves contain recoveries, because the closed-loop teacher was disturbed by impulses during collection; the student thus sees off-nominal states with correct labels, which is the effect \ac{DAgger} produces by querying the expert on student-visited states, obtained here without an expert that can be queried at arbitrary states. Second, the student predicts action chunks and executes a temporal ensemble of overlapping chunks, which filters the single-step noise that would otherwise be amplified by the controller. Third, where a queryable expert exists, in simulation, a flow-matching student of the same architecture can be trained online with the hybrid teacher labeling the states that the student visits; this online variant is described in Section~\ref{sec:online-flow}; its comparison with the offline variant in Chapter~\ref{ch:sim} is pending. The deployed student is trained offline on $\Dset$: the ten-cycle dataset is rendered once under visual randomization and shared by the full method and ablations (b) to (d) of Chapter~\ref{ch:sim}. Online \ac{DAgger} serves as a comparison in simulation.

\subsection{Flow Matching for Conditional Action Chunks}\label{sec:flow-matching}

Flow matching learns a generative model as the solution of an \ac{ODE}. Let $\vec{x}\in\R^{d}$ denote a sample, let $p_0=\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\mat{I})$ be the source distribution, and let $p_1=q$ be the data distribution. A time-dependent vector field $\vec{v}:\R^{d}\times[0,1]\to\R^{d}$ defines a flow $\psi_\lambda$ through $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\psi_\lambda(\vec{x})=\vec{v}(\psi_\lambda(\vec{x}),\lambda)$ with $\psi_0(\vec{x})=\vec{x}$, and the flow transports $p_0$ to the marginal path $p_\lambda=[\psi_\lambda]_{\#}p_0$. The flow-matching objective regresses a parametric field $\vfield$ onto a target field $\vec{u}_\lambda$ that generates a prescribed path $p_\lambda$ from $p_0$ to $p_1$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fm}
  L_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta)=\E_{\lambda\sim U[0,1],\,\vec{x}\sim p_\lambda}\left\|\vfield(\vec{x},\lambda)-\vec{u}_\lambda(\vec{x})\right\|_2^{2}.
\end{equation}
This objective cannot be evaluated directly, because the marginal path and its field are defined only implicitly by the data. Conditional flow matching~\cite{Lipman2023} resolves this by constructing the marginal path as a mixture of conditional paths $p_\lambda(\vec{x}\mid\vec{x}^{1})$, one per data sample $\vec{x}^{1}\sim q$, each with a known conditional field $\vec{u}_\lambda(\vec{x}\mid\vec{x}^{1})$. The marginal field is the posterior-weighted average of the conditional fields,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:marginal-field}
  \vec{u}_\lambda(\vec{x})=\int\vec{u}_\lambda(\vec{x}\mid\vec{x}^{1})\,\frac{p_\lambda(\vec{x}\mid\vec{x}^{1})\,q(\vec{x}^{1})}{p_\lambda(\vec{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\vec{x}^{1},
\end{equation}
and the conditional objective
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cfm}
  L_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta)=\E_{\lambda\sim U[0,1],\,\vec{x}^{1}\sim q,\,\vec{x}\sim p_\lambda(\cdot\mid\vec{x}^{1})}\left\|\vfield(\vec{x},\lambda)-\vec{u}_\lambda(\vec{x}\mid\vec{x}^{1})\right\|_2^{2}
\end{equation}
has the same gradient with respect to $\theta$ as \eqref{eq:fm}; the two differ only by a constant that does not depend on $\theta$, which follows by expanding the squares and using \eqref{eq:marginal-field}. The conditional path can therefore be chosen for convenience. The optimal-transport path takes the conditional distribution to be Gaussian with mean $\lambda\vec{x}^{1}$ and standard deviation $1-\lambda$, so that a sample is the straight-line interpolant
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interpolant}
  \vec{x}^{\lambda}=(1-\lambda)\vec{x}^{0}+\lambda\vec{x}^{1},\qquad \vec{x}^{0}\sim p_0,
\end{equation}
and the conditional field along this line is constant, $\vec{u}_\lambda(\vec{x}^{\lambda}\mid\vec{x}^{1})=\vec{x}^{1}-\vec{x}^{0}$. Straight conditional paths make the learned marginal field nearly straight as well, which is why few integration steps suffice at sampling time.

The student applies this construction to action chunks conditioned on the observation. At control step $t$ the student observes $\obs_t=(\vec{O}^{\mathrm{RGBD}}_t,\vec{s}_t,p_t,\vec{e}^{m}_t)$, consisting of the \acs{RGBD} image, the proprioception $\vec{s}_t$, the normalized task progress $p_t$, and the process-phase encoding $\vec{e}^{m}_t$, and learns the distribution of the executed $H$-step action chunk. The supervision target is the demonstrated chunk $\Uc^{D}_t=[\act_t,\dots,\act_{t+H-1}]\in\R^{H\times9}$, the observation is encoded into a condition vector $\cond_t$ by the network of Section~\ref{sec:student}, and the distillation objective is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:student-objective}
  \theta^{*}=\argmin_\theta\;\E\left\|\vfield\!\left(\Uc^{\lambda}_t,\lambda\mid\cond_t\right)-\dot{\Uc}^{\lambda}_t\right\|_2^{2},\qquad
  \Uc^{\lambda}_t=(1-\lambda)\Uc^{0}_t+\lambda\Uc^{D}_t,\quad
  \dot{\Uc}^{\lambda}_t=\Uc^{D}_t-\Uc^{0}_t,
\end{equation}
where the expectation is over $(\obs_t,\Uc^{D}_t)\sim\Dset$, $\Uc^{0}_t\sim\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\mat{I})$, and $\lambda\sim U[0,1]$. The learned field transports Gaussian noise to the conditional action distribution $p_\theta(\Uc_t\mid\obs_t)$. At inference, a chunk is generated by drawing $\Uc^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\mat{I})$ and integrating the \ac{ODE} with the explicit Euler method on a uniform grid of $N$ steps,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler}
  \Uc^{\lambda_{n+1}}=\Uc^{\lambda_{n}}+\frac{1}{N}\,\vfield\!\left(\Uc^{\lambda_n},\lambda_n\mid\cond_t\right),\qquad \lambda_n=\frac{n}{N},\quad n=0,\dots,N-1,
\end{equation}
with $N=16$, the value used for evaluation in simulation. The deployment runner keeps $N=16$ on a GPU and reduces it to two on the CPU-only workcell computer to meet the 15\,Hz period (Chapter~\ref{ch:real}). The condition $\cond_t$ is computed once per chunk, so the image is encoded once and the $N$ velocity evaluations cost only the Transformer of Section~\ref{sec:student}.

Three properties distinguish this head from the alternatives that the thesis compares against. Relative to a deterministic regression head, which minimizes a squared error between a single predicted chunk and the label and therefore converges to the conditional mean, the flow head represents multimodal labels; this matters because the teacher's recoveries after impulses are state-dependent in ways the observation does not fully resolve, so that near-identical observations can carry the nominal action in one demonstration and a recovery in another. Relative to a diffusion head, which learns the score of a noised data distribution and samples by a stochastic reverse process with many steps along curved paths, the flow head learns a deterministic \ac{ODE} along nearly straight paths, trains with a plain regression target without a noise schedule, and samples in a few deterministic Euler steps. The remaining stochasticity of the flow head comes from the source sample only; a zero-noise sample at the source mean is available as a diagnostic.

\subsection{Student Architecture as Implemented}\label{sec:student}

Figure~\ref{fig:student} shows the student. It consists of a vision encoder, a proprioception encoder, a fusion and conditioning stage, and the velocity network.

\paragraph{Observation encoding and late fusion.}
Following DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025}, the visual and proprioceptive streams are encoded separately and fused by concatenation, with the visual feature placed before the encoded proprioception. The 29-D proprioception $\vec{s}_t$, consisting of the ten controlled joint positions, the ten joint velocities, and the previous filtered 9-D action, is encoded by a 128--128 \acs{MLP} with ELU activations. The concatenation of the 64-D visual feature and the 128-D proprioceptive feature is projected by a linear layer with ELU activation to the 256-D condition. In the privileged ablations of Chapter~\ref{ch:sim} the vision encoder is replaced by the identity and the nut position (3-D) or the nut position with its canonical axis-angle orientation (6-D) is appended to the proprioception instead; in the ablation that removes the hand velocities, the three pinch-joint velocities are dropped and the proprioception becomes 26-D.

\paragraph{Vision encoder.}
The retained encoder follows \ac{DP3}~\cite{Ze2024}. The depth image is back-projected through the calibrated intrinsics into camera-frame points; points with non-finite depth, depth outside $[0.10,2.0]$\,m, or coordinates outside a fixed crop box that covers the workspace with a 40\,mm margin are rejected; the remaining points are centered and scaled by fixed workspace constants and carry the color of their pixel, normalized to $[-1,1]$. From the valid points, 4096 are sampled, randomly during training and with a fixed priority at evaluation; encoder pretraining fails fast if fewer than 128 valid points remain, and every frame of the retained dataset keeps more than 10\,000 valid points (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). A compact PointNet with pointwise layers of width 64, 128, and 256, layer normalization, ReLU activations, and global max pooling, followed by an ELU projection, yields the 64-D visual feature $\vec{v}_t$. The encoder has 59\,648 parameters and no generic pretrained backbone; it is pretrained on the task scene and then fine-tuned inside the student, as described below. The alternative encoders prepared for comparison in Chapter~\ref{ch:sim} share the same 64-D interface; two of them are described here. An image-space encoder applies an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 stage-2 trunk to the concatenation of RGB, log depth, and a validity mask, adds an embedding of the calibrated camera-frame XYZ pooled on the 6 by 10 token grid, and applies one spatial self-attention layer and learned-query pooling; this is the ablation (b) of Chapter~\ref{ch:sim}. An Adapt3R-style encoder~\cite{Wilcox2025} extracts dense features with a frozen ResNet-18 layer-1 backbone, localizes 512 of them in 3D through the depth map, expresses the points in the live grasp frame, and pools them by learned-query attention. All encoders consume the same $320\times180$ \acs{RGBD} pair.

\paragraph{Encoder pretraining in parallel scenes and fine-tuning.}
The point-cloud encoder is first trained alone, on a pose-regression task in parallel simulated scenes, and then fine-tuned end to end inside the student. The pretraining environment replicates 16 robot-free scenes in one simulator instance, each with the workcell table, the M24 nut and bolt at the teacher-training pose, and the calibrated camera. In every scene a gravity-free, collision-free nut follows an independent six-DoF path through the visible workspace, and one tiled camera renders all scenes in a single call. The fresh \acs{RGBD} batch is consumed immediately by the encoder and a pose decoder, which predicts the camera-frame nut position and a six-value rotation with a loss that treats the six $60\degree$ rotations of the hexagonal nut as equivalent; no dataset is stored, and simulator ground truth enters only as the decoder target. The retained run consumed 32\,000 frames over 2000 optimizer steps with RGB augmentation and noise-free depth. Validation on a held-out trajectory selected the checkpoint at step 1000, with a mean position error of 5.87\,mm and a symmetry-aware mean rotation error of $46.2\degree$. Only the encoder weights are transferred to the student. The pose decoder is discarded, because the nut positions of the demonstrations follow a different distribution, and the student's auxiliary head is trained from scratch. All encoder parameters remain trainable and are optimized jointly with the flow head by the same optimizer and learning rate, so the representation adapts to the hybrid-teacher scenes and to the action objective.

\paragraph{Conditioning on progress and phase.}
Two low-dimensional conditions are added to the 256-D condition through separate learned embeddings, each a 1--256--256 or 3--256--256 \acs{MLP} with ELU activation. The global trajectory progress $p_t\in[0,1]$ maps the first timestamp of each demonstration to 0 and the last to 1; at deployment it advances with elapsed time from the start of the trial, normalized by the median demonstration duration stored in the checkpoint, and is clamped to 1 afterwards. It is a monotonic clock for the complete behavior and nothing more: it is not a teacher-stage selector, it is not periodic, and it does not encode the cycle index; it tells the student how far into a nominally timed trial it is, which disambiguates, for instance, the first and the last approach of a trial that otherwise look alike. The process-phase encoding $\vec{e}^{m}_t$ is a three-way one-hot over the policy-controlled phases, the release waypoints, and the return; during training it is derived from the recorded phase labels, and during evaluation it is published by the cyclic coordinator, in simulation from the physical events of Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-rollout} and on the robot from the release proxy and return gate, so that a cycle that takes longer than nominal cannot select the wrong phase. The phase encoding is a synchronization aid: it does not switch networks or action contracts, and the student predicts the transition actions itself.

\paragraph{Velocity network.}
The velocity field is a non-causal trajectory Transformer. The noisy chunk $\Uc^{\lambda}$ is split into $H=8$ action tokens of dimension 9, each embedded linearly into the 512-D model width; a condition token embeds $\cond_t$ and a time token embeds the flow time $\lambda$ through a 64-D sinusoidal encoding. Learned positional embeddings mark the ten token slots, the condition and time tokens and the eight chunk offsets; no absolute time enters through them. Four pre-norm Transformer encoder layers with eight attention heads, a 2048-D GELU feed-forward block, and no dropout apply full self-attention over all tokens, so that every future step may attend to the whole noisy chunk when predicting its velocity, and a linear output layer maps each action token to its 9-D velocity. The model keeps an exponential moving average of its parameters with decay 0.999, and inference uses the averaged weights. An auxiliary head, a 64--128--3 \acs{MLP} on the visual feature, regresses the nut position in normalized coordinates with unit weight; it regularizes the visual feature toward spatially meaningful content and is discarded at deployment. Training uses the AdamW optimizer with learning rate $10^{-4}$, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, batches of 64 windows, 300 epochs, and DextrAH-style RGB augmentation of the stored images, with saturation, contrast, and brightness factors in $[0.5,1.5]$, hue shifts in $[-0.15,0.15]$, and motion blur with probability 0.1.

\paragraph{Action chunking and execution.}
With $H=8$ and 15\,Hz, one chunk covers 0.53\,s. Chunk targets begin at the sample paired with the observation (target offset zero), which is the correct alignment for the Cartesian contract because the recorder stores the pre-step state together with the command acting on it. At deployment the student replans at every control step, and the command sent to the controller is a temporal ensemble of all still-valid proposals for the current tick with weights $0.5^{k}$ for a proposal of age $k$; a decay of zero recovers strict first-action execution. The ensemble operates in action space and is distinct from the parameter-space \acs{EMA}. The executed 9-D action is clipped to $[-1,1]$, decoded to the filtered Cartesian target, and passed through the unchanged controller path of Section~\ref{sec:action-contract}. In simulation the action smoothing of that path is inverted analytically, so that the controller receives exactly the predicted target; on the robot the decoded target is handed to the Cartesian controller directly. \paragraph{Normalization.}
The 9-D label is the filtered Cartesian target divided by a fixed scale for each component, which bounds the scripted transitions to $[-1,1]$, so no action statistics are learned. The joint positions and velocities of the proprioception are consumed in physical units without standardization, and its previous-action entries in the bounded label units; the nut position of the auxiliary head is standardized by dataset statistics stored in the checkpoint.

\subsection{Offline Training}\label{sec:offline-flow}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:offline} summarizes offline distillation. The dataset consists of the simulated hybrid-teacher rollouts, windows of $H$ consecutive commands are built from each episode without crossing episode boundaries, and the velocity field is trained with \eqref{eq:student-objective} while an \acs{EMA} copy of the parameters is maintained.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]%
    \caption{Offline flow distillation}\label{alg:offline}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require hybrid teacher $\piHT$, horizon $H$
        \State Collect simulated rollouts $\Dsim$ with $\piHT$ under random arm impulses and visual domain randomization
        \State Form $H$-step chunks from $\Dset=\Dsim$, masking resets and padding terminal chunks
        \Repeat
            \State Sample $(\obs_t,\Uc^{D}_t)\sim\Dset$, $\Uc^{0}_t\sim\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\mat{I})$, $\lambda\sim U[0,1]$
            \State Encode the point cloud and fuse it with proprioception, progress, and phase to obtain $\cond_t$
            \State Update $\theta$ by one gradient step on \eqref{eq:student-objective} plus the auxiliary nut-position loss
            \State Update \acs{EMA} parameters $\bar{\theta}\leftarrow\alpha\bar{\theta}+(1-\alpha)\theta$
        \Until{convergence}
        \State \Return student velocity field $\vec{v}_{\bar{\theta}}$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}%

\subsection{Online \acs{DAgger} with a Flow-Matching Head}\label{sec:online-flow}

The online variant replaces the fixed dataset by live teacher labels. The simulator is driven by a mixture of teacher and student control with teacher probability $\beta$, the hybrid teacher labels every visited state with its 9-D command, and the flow head is trained on the labels as they arrive. The registered online recipe differs from the offline student in four respects: it uses the two-branch \acs{RGBD} encoder instead of the point-cloud encoder, it supervises the teacher's normalized native action instead of the rescaled filtered target, it has no process-phase conditioning, and it weights the auxiliary loss with 0.01. Two aspects differ from the recurrent \ac{DAgger} of DextrAH. First, the label of a chunked student is a sequence of $H$ future teacher commands, not a single command. The trainer collects eight consecutive live teacher commands, pairs the sequence with the observation at its first step, and uses stride-one overlapping supervision, so that once the initial horizon is full one chunk completes at every control step; sequences that cross a reset or the scripted handover are discarded. Second, the chunk label is only a valid sample of $p(\Uc_t\mid\obs_t)$ if the teacher itself produced the states on which the later commands were computed. If the student had controlled some of those steps, the later labels would describe the teacher's reaction to a student-induced future, not the teacher's trajectory from the conditioning observation. Multi-step online supervision therefore fixes $\beta=1$ during collection: the teacher carries every training state, and the student is exposed to its own state distribution only at evaluation. The \acs{RGBD} latent is encoded once per step and detached before it enters the pending windows; the vision encoder is trained by the immediate nut-position auxiliary objective at a reduced learning rate of $10^{-5}$, and the delayed flow objective trains the action path from the stored latent with learning rate $10^{-4}$. Student rollouts during online training sample with eight Euler steps. Algorithm~\ref{alg:online} summarizes the loop.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]%
    \caption{Online \acs{DAgger} with a conditional flow-matching head}\label{alg:online}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require hybrid teacher $\piHT$, student $\vfield$, horizon $H$, teacher probability $\beta$
        \For{each iteration}
            \State Observe $\obs_t$ in every environment; query the teacher label $\act^{*}_t=\piHT(\vec{x}_t,m_t)$
            \State Sample a student chunk from $\vfield$ with $N$ Euler steps and take its first action $\hat{\act}_t$
            \State Step each environment with $\act^{*}_t$ with probability $\beta$, otherwise with $\hat{\act}_t$
            \State Append $\act^{*}_t$ to the pending windows that started at steps $t-H+1,\dots,t$
            \State Emit every completed window $(\obs_{t-H+1},[\act^{*}_{t-H+1},\dots,\act^{*}_t])$ and drop windows that crossed a reset
            \State Update $\theta$ on the emitted windows with \eqref{eq:student-objective}; update the \acs{EMA} parameters
        \EndFor
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}%

With a one-step horizon the constraint on $\beta$ disappears, and the intervention schedule becomes a design choice. The recurrent baseline implements three schedules: a fixed $\beta$, a linear decay from $\beta_{\mathrm{start}}$ to $\beta_{\mathrm{end}}$ over a given number of iterations, and a SafeDAgger rule that hands control to the teacher when the \acs{RMSE} between the deterministic teacher and student commands exceeds a takeover threshold of 0.10 and returns it when the error falls below a release threshold of 0.075; the hysteresis produces coherent corrective segments rather than per-step flicker. Which schedule allows a cyclic student to learn both the turn and the transitions is an experimental question that Chapter~\ref{ch:sim} answers.

\subsection{Baseline Students}\label{sec:baselines}

Three deterministic students serve as baselines. The Transformer baseline shares the observation and action contracts of the flow student, the recurrent baseline shares its action contract, and the behavior-cloning baseline shares neither. The behavior-cloning baseline consumes an eight-frame history of measured joint positions and velocities and predicts two future commands with a 512--512--512 \acs{MLP} with batch normalization; it acts at the physics rate and follows a reference cursor, which makes it a plumbing and local-imitation diagnostic rather than a candidate for deployment. The recurrent \ac{DAgger} baseline follows the DextrAH distillation recipe: a 512-unit layer-normalized \ac{LSTM} followed by a 512--512--256 ELU \acs{MLP} and a Gaussian head, trained by deterministic action regression on the teacher command, with the student's deterministic mean as the rollout command. Its observation consists of the same proprioception, the two-branch \acs{RGBD} encoder, and a four-way task-stage code, and it is trained online by \ac{DAgger} rather than from $\Dset$. The Transformer regression baseline is a deterministic variant of \ac{ACT}~\cite{Zhao2023} without the variational objective: four layers, eight heads, 512-D hidden width, 2048-D feed-forward layers, and eight learned action queries that predict an eight-step chunk.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Evaluation Ladder}\label{sec:ladder}

A failed closed-loop rollout can originate in the supervised fit, in the command conversion and controller, or in the distribution shift between teacher and student states. The three evaluation modes in Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} separate these causes, and each mode makes a stronger claim than the previous one.

\begin{figure}[htb]%
    \centering%
    \input{figures/ladder.tikz}%
    \caption{Evaluation ladder. Offline teacher-fit feeds recorded teacher observations to the student and compares predicted chunks with recorded commands without a simulator. Teacher-forced clock executes the student's commands in live physics while the student receives the recorded teacher observations on the recorded timeline, removing observation drift. Normal closed loop feeds live observations back to the student and is the only mode that measures compounding error.}%
    \label{fig:ladder}%
\end{figure}%

In the offline teacher-fit mode, every recorded teacher observation of a demonstration is fed to the student, and the sampled chunk is compared with the recorded command chunk; the reported quantities are the \acs{RMSE} of the first action and of the full chunk and the per-joint errors over time. Passing shows that the checkpoint can represent the observation-to-action mapping under the chosen alignment; it establishes neither controller correctness nor closed-loop success. In the teacher-forced clock mode, the student's predicted commands are executed in live physics, but the student receives the recorded teacher observations selected on the recorded playback timeline, and for progress-conditioned checkpoints the same timeline supplies $p_t$; the realized joints, tool center point, and nut pose are compared with the recording. Passing shows that the learned action map, the command conversion, the controller, and the physics reproduce the nominal behavior once closed-loop observation drift is removed. Only the normal closed-loop mode, in which the student consumes live observations and its errors feed back into later observations, measures task success and compounding error; it is the mode of all results that claim success. The two diagnostic modes are used in that order to localize a failure before expensive retraining: a bad offline fit points to capacity, normalization, or alignment; a good fit with bad teacher-forced execution points to the command contract; and good teacher-forced execution with a bad closed loop points to distribution shift and recovery, which is the regime that motivates online labels or disturbance augmentation.

\subsection{Metrics}\label{sec:metrics}

Unless a study states otherwise, each condition is evaluated over $N_{\mathrm{tr}}=10$ trials with up to ten cycles per trial, where each cycle consists of a nominal $60\degree$ clockwise turn followed by release and return. A cycle counts as completed when the release has been triggered and the return has been validated against the reset pose within its 16\,s timeout (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-rollout}); a trial ends at the first transition failure, early termination, or time limit. Three quantities are reported. The mean completed cycle count $\bar{C}=N_{\mathrm{tr}}^{-1}\sum_i C_i$ counts, for trial $i$, the cycles $C_i\in\{0,\dots,10\}$ completed before failure or timeout; incomplete cycles are excluded, and higher is better. The cyclic tracking error is computed for every completed cycle $k$ of trial $i$ as $e_{i,k}=\Delta\varphi_{i,k}-60\degree$, where $\Delta\varphi_{i,k}=\varphi_{i,k}-\varphi_{i,k-1}$ is the clockwise nut rotation; over all $M=\sum_i C_i$ completed cycles the mean $\mu_{\mathrm{cyc}}=M^{-1}\sum_{i,k}e_{i,k}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{cyc}}$ are reported, where negative values indicate lag and positive values overshoot, and a mean closer to zero with a smaller deviation is better; if $M=0$ both statistics are undefined. The ten-cycle tracking error is computed over the set $I_{10}=\{i:C_i=10\}$ of trials that complete all cycles as $e_{10,i}=\sum_{k=1}^{10}\Delta\varphi_{i,k}-600\degree$, reported as mean $\mu_{10}$ and standard deviation over $I_{10}$; it is undefined when no trial completes. All errors are in degrees. In simulation the nut angle is read from the simulator; on hardware it is read from an angular scale mounted under the bolt (Chapter~\ref{ch:real}).

With every component of the method and its evaluation defined, Chapter~\ref{ch:sim} applies the protocol in simulation to answer RQ1 and RQ2, and Chapter~\ref{ch:real} applies it on the physical system to answer RQ3.
