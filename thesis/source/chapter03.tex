\chapter{Methodology}\label{ch:method}%

This chapter presents the method, which learns cyclic nut threading as a single visuomotor policy. It follows one principle: decompose during data generation, unify at deployment. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the resulting pipeline of three steps. First, a privileged teacher is trained with \ac{RL} in simulation to approach the nut, grasp it, turn it, and stop at the target from the simulator state (Section~\ref{sec:teacher}). A simplified thread model makes this training tractable, and a Cartesian action contract that teacher, student, and robot share bounds its commands. Second, the teacher is chained with scripted controllers for release and return into a hybrid teacher, which is rolled out in simulation to generate complete multi-cycle demonstrations under random disturbances and visual randomization (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}). Third, the central contribution of this thesis distills these demonstrations by conditional flow matching into one visuomotor student, which encodes the \acs{RGBD} observation as a point cloud together with proprioception and executes every phase of every cycle at deployment (Section~\ref{sec:distillation}). Before these steps, Section~\ref{sec:problem} formalizes the task that the pipeline solves. The physical hand--arm system on which the method is deployed is described with the experimental setup in Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

Cyclic nut threading is decomposed into the ordered phase sequence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phases}
  \mathcal{S}=\big(\underbrace{\text{approach},\;\text{grasp},\;\text{rotate},\;\text{goal reaching}}_{\MRL:\;\piT},\;\underbrace{\text{release},\;\text{return and reset}}_{\Mscript:\;\kappa}\big).
\end{equation}
The active phase at control step $t$ is $m_t\in\Mset=\MRL\cup\Mscript$. The four phases in $\MRL$ are executed by a learned teacher policy $\piT$. The two transition phases in $\Mscript$ are executed by scripted controllers $\kappa_m$. The split exists only during data generation, and the deployed student is one network for all phases.

Let $K$ be the required number of cycles, $\Delta\varphi^{*}$ the desired clockwise rotation per cycle, and $\varphi_k$ the unwrapped nut angle about the bolt axis after the goal-reaching phase of cycle $k$, with $\varphi_0$ the angle at the start of the trial. The cyclic tracking error, the episodic tracking error, and the tracking cost of a trajectory $\tau$ are defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:errors}
  e_k=\varphi_k-\varphi_{k-1}-\Delta\varphi^{*},\qquad
  e_{\mathrm{ep}}=\varphi_K-\varphi_0-K\Delta\varphi^{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}e_k,\qquad
  E(\tau)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}e_k^{2}+\beta_{E}\,e_{\mathrm{ep}}^{2}.
\end{equation}
Positive errors indicate overshoot and negative errors lag. The cost squares them so that overshoot in one cycle cannot cancel lag in another, and the weight $\beta_E$ trades accumulated drift against cycle-wise accuracy. Let $C(\tau)\in\{0,\dots,K\}$ count the cycles completed before failure or timeout and let $\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}$ denote the policies that execute the phases of $\mathcal{S}$ in order in every cycle. The research problem is to find the policy that chains all phases with maximum completion success and, among those, minimum tracking cost:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
  \Pi_{\mathrm{succ}}=\argmax_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}}\;\Pr_{\tau\sim p_\pi}\!\left[C(\tau)=K\right],\qquad
  \pi^{*}=\argmin_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{succ}}}\;\E_{\tau\sim p_\pi}\!\left[E(\tau)\mid C(\tau)=K\right].
\end{equation}
Unless a study states otherwise, $K=10$ and $\Delta\varphi^{*}=60\degree$, which corresponds to one face of a hexagonal nut per cycle. The policy $\pi^{*}$ is learned from fixed hybrid-teacher demonstrations rather than by optimizing \eqref{eq:objective} on hardware, because on-robot optimization of a contact-rich cyclic task is neither safe nor sample-efficient.

The task is partially observable at deployment, so it is a \ac{POMDP}: the \ac{MDP} of Section~\ref{sec:rl-background} is extended by an observation model $O(\vec{z}_t\mid\vec{x}_t)$, and the policy acts on observations $\vec{z}_t$ instead of states~\cite{Sutton2018}. The simulator state $\vec{x}_t$ contains the nut pose, the contact state, and the controller parameters. The student observes only an aligned \acs{RGBD} image, its proprioception, and two low-dimensional conditioning signals introduced in Section~\ref{sec:distillation}. A single observation is then no longer sufficient to act optimally, because the information that disambiguates the state may lie in earlier observations. The teacher--student structure of the method separates the two difficulties of this \ac{POMDP}: the teacher solves the control problem with privileged state, and the student learns to perceive and to reproduce the teacher's behavior from sensor data.

\section{Privileged Teacher Policy Training}\label{sec:teacher}

The privileged teacher learns the contact-rich part of the cycle: it approaches the nut, grasps it in a thumb--index pinch, turns it, and stops at the target turn. Because it observes privileged simulator state, it solves this control problem without having to perceive the nut (Section~\ref{sec:problem}). Two components make the skill learnable in simulation, and the two subsections of this section present them in turn. Section~\ref{sec:thread-model} replaces the thread contact, which the physics engine does not resolve reliably at the scale of a thread, by a kinematic coupling in which the nut advances along the bolt only by rotating, and models the thread resistance by a friction torque on the rotation. Section~\ref{sec:teacher-training} trains the teacher on this model with \ac{PPO} in an asymmetric actor--critic. Its reward pays progress of the nut rotation toward the target turn while the grasp holds, and it penalizes the wrench that the thread transmits to the nut apart from the turning torque, so that the policy turns the nut instead of pushing or tilting it. Domain randomization of the friction, the thread resistance, the nut mass, and the controller parameters covers the gap that the simplified contact opens to the physical thread.

\subsection{Simplified Thread Simulation}\label{sec:thread-model}

Because the skill is learned in simulation, the simulated thread has to behave like a mating thread in each of the hundreds of parallel environments that \ac{PPO} needs (Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-contact}). The default Isaac~Sim collision pipeline does not provide this, because of how it resolves contact.

PhysX, like other GPU-parallel engines, resolves contact through interpenetration (Figure~\ref{fig:depenetration}). Once the gap between two bodies falls below the contact offset, the engine creates a contact. Within one time step the bodies can sink into each other by a penetration depth $d$, and the solver then pushes them apart along the contact normal with a separating velocity that grows with $d/\Delta t$ and is capped at the maximum depenetration velocity. Contact is therefore never strictly rigid. It tolerates a small overlap, and the repulsive response grows with that overlap.

This tolerance matters for threads, because the pitch of the nuts used in this thesis is only 3 to 4\,mm and the flanks and crests are smaller still. The solver resolves an overlap along the normal of the nearest surface, and which surface is nearest depends on how deep the nut has sunk (Figure~\ref{fig:thread-exploit}). As long as the nut thread overlaps only the loaded flank of the bolt, the normal points back out of the groove and depenetration restores the thread. Because the separating velocity is capped, a fast axial push can carry the nut thread past the bolt crest before the overlap is resolved. The nearest surface is then the opposite flank, and the solver pushes the nut into the next groove. The nut has advanced one pitch without turning.

With \ac{SDF} mesh colliders on nut and bolt, the simulated thread contact is therefore not accurate enough to reproduce a mating thread, and axial progress of the nut can be obtained by pressing it along the bolt axis. A policy rewarded for axial progress can learn this shortcut, which fails on hardware. The thread model used for teacher training therefore replaces the thread contact by a kinematic coupling, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:thread-model}, in which axial progress follows from rotation alone. The nut is attached to the bolt by a passive revolute joint about the bolt axis and a coaxial, position-controlled prismatic joint. With prismatic coordinate $d_t$, unwrapped and direction-corrected revolute coordinate $\theta_t$, thread pitch $\rho$, and the index $b_k$ of the step at which the current turn started, the prismatic target is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thread}
  d^{*}_t-d_{b_k}=-\frac{\rho}{2\pi}\left(\theta_t-\theta_{b_k}\right),
\end{equation}
so that one revolution produces one pitch of axial travel. The prismatic joint tracks $d^{*}_t$ with a stiff drive, whose finite stiffness permits small deviations from the ideal helix while the revolute--prismatic pair constrains lateral motion and keeps the nut near the commanded path. The finger--nut contact forces remain fully simulated, so the policy must grip and turn the nut. Thread resistance is modeled on the revolute joint by a smoothed Coulomb and viscous friction torque
\begin{equation}\label{eq:friction}
  \bar{\tau}_{f,t}=-\tau_c\tanh\!\left(\frac{\omega_t}{\omega_s}\right)-c_v\,\omega_t,
\end{equation}
with revolute velocity $\omega_t$, Coulomb torque $\tau_c$, viscous coefficient $c_v$, and smoothing speed $\omega_s$. Table~\ref{tab:sim-params} lists the drive, friction, and pitch values. Beyond the contact simplification, the nut is initialized already engaged with the bolt. Thread engagement from a loose nut is a separate skill with a narrow entry configuration, and it is outside the scope of this thesis. The simplified contact opens a gap between the simulated and the physical thread, which the domain randomization of the teacher (Section~\ref{sec:teacher-training}) is meant to cover.

\subsection{PPO Training}\label{sec:teacher-training}

The teacher for the learned phases in $\MRL$ is a recurrent policy, whose hidden state $\vec{h}_t$ integrates the history of past observations. It is trained with \ac{PPO} (Section~\ref{sec:rl-background}) in an asymmetric actor--critic~\cite{Pinto2017}. The actor observation $\vec{z}^{\pi}_t=g_\pi(\vec{x}_t,\vec{\xi}_t)$ selects the actor-visible components of the simulator state and applies observation noise $\vec{\xi}_t$. It contains the poses of the fingertips relative to the bolt and to the nut, the nut orientation, the error to the turning target, a grasp-phase flag, the previous filtered action, and a force group of two fingertip forces and seven arm joint torques that mirrors the sensors of the physical system. The critic observes noise-free absolute poses of the fingertips, the nut, and the bolt instead of the actor's relative vectors, together with the turning target, the force group, and the previous filtered action, and in addition the arm joint positions and the randomized controller parameters of the episode. It is discarded after training, so this privileged state shapes the update of the actor without ever being an input to it. Table~\ref{tab:teacher-obs} in the appendix lists all components and their noise.

The actor parameterizes a diagonal Gaussian over the normalized action $\vec{a}_t\in[-1,1]^{9}$. The 9-D action space comprises a 6-D Cartesian target for the grasp frame, expressed relative to the bolt and tracked by an operational-space impedance controller, and three commands for thumb abduction, thumb flexion, and index flexion. Each action is smoothed by an exponential moving average before it is decoded, and the student and the scripted transitions act in the same filtered space. Appendix~\ref{sec:action-contract} describes the action decoder, its filter and step limits, and the controller.

The teacher is optimized with the shaped reward
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward}
\begin{aligned}
  r_t&=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{R}} w^{r}_{i}R_{i,t}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}} w^{p}_{j}P_{j,t},\\
  \mathcal{R}&=\{\text{center},\text{face},\text{grasp},\text{hold},\text{turn},\text{target}\},\\
  \mathcal{P}&=\{\text{magnitude},\Delta a,\text{align},\text{force},\text{settle},\text{wrench},\text{clear},\text{contact},\text{limit}\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The reward follows three stages, and Table~\ref{tab:reward} in the appendix lists the exact terms and weights. During approach, $R^{\mathrm{center}}$ rewards motion toward the nut center and $R^{\mathrm{face}}$ rewards motion of the thumb and index pads toward two designated opposing faces. $R^{\mathrm{grasp}}$ marks the acquisition of a stable, proper grasp, and $R^{\mathrm{hold}}$ pays a small reward while it holds. During turning, $R^{\mathrm{turn}}$ rewards grip-maintaining progress toward the helical target pose. At completion, $R^{\mathrm{target}}$ pays a bonus once, on the step at which the episode succeeds. The approach and turning rewards pay only newly achieved progress, so repeated motion cannot accumulate reward. The penalties discourage large commands, abrupt action changes, hand--nut misalignment, a large net force of the two finger pads on the nut, residual rotation near the target, the wrench that the thread transmits to the nut apart from the turning torque, contact with or proximity to the table, and arm joint positions near their limits.

Each episode samples a target turn $\theta^{*}\sim U(55\degree,65\degree)$, so the policy has to read the stopping angle from its observation. An episode succeeds when the rotation stays within $2.5\degree$ of $\theta^{*}$ under a live proper grasp for several consecutive steps. It terminates early when the nut leaves the bolt, tilts, or releases the thread, when an arm joint approaches its limit, or when the latched grasp is lost, so grasp retention is a hard constraint. Robustness is obtained by domain randomization of the nut mass, the friction, the thread resistance, the arm joint dynamics, the controller parameters, the observations, and the initial state (Table~\ref{tab:dr} in the appendix). An automatic curriculum widens the initial nut yaw from zero to $\pm30\degree$ whenever the success rate reaches 80\,\%, and most physical randomization widens with it. The teacher used for all demonstrations of this thesis is trained on the M24 nut in 500 parallel environments for 200 epochs (Table~\ref{tab:ppo}).

\section{Dataset Construction with the Hybrid Teacher}\label{sec:hybrid}

The privileged teacher turns the nut once. The hybrid teacher chains this turn with scripted release and return and rolls the chain out in simulation to produce complete multi-cycle demonstrations, so that each phase is specified by its simplest mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-horizon}). This section defines the hybrid teacher, the disturbances and the visual randomization applied during its rollout, and the dataset that the rollout produces.

Let $m_t\in\Mset$ denote the active phase, $\vec{x}_t$ the full simulator state, $\vec{z}^{\pi}_t$ the noisy actor observation, and $\vec{h}_t$ the actor's hidden state. During rollout the teacher is queried for its deterministic mean action $\bar{\pi}_{\mathrm{T}}(\vec{z}^{\pi}_t,\vec{h}_t)=\E_{\vec{a}\sim\piT(\cdot\mid\vec{z}^{\pi}_t,\vec{h}_t)}[\vec{a}]$. The hybrid teacher is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hybrid}
  \piHT(\vec{x}_t,m_t)=
  \begin{cases}
    \bar{\pi}_{\mathrm{T}}(\vec{z}^{\pi}_t,\vec{h}_t), & m_t\in\MRL,\\
    \kappa_{m_t}(\vec{x}_t), & m_t\in\Mscript,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_{m_t}$ is the scripted controller of the active transition phase. Both branches produce commands in the same filtered, bolt-relative Cartesian action space, so the recorded label is a single 9-D signal throughout the cycle. The scripted Cartesian targets are mapped back through the action decoder of Appendix~\ref{sec:action-contract}, and every label is the filtered target divided by a fixed per-component scale that bounds the scripted transitions to $[-1,1]$ (Table~\ref{tab:flow}). Because the label keeps its meaning and units at every step, one student can be supervised on the whole demonstration.

Release is triggered when the clockwise nut rotation since the start of the cycle comes within $5\degree$ of the cycle's target turn, which lies between $55\degree$ and $65\degree$. Four Cartesian waypoints follow: the first opens the thumb and index while the arm holds its pose, and the next three retract the hand away from the nut and raise it. A final return segment brings the hand back to the initial grasp pose. The return is physically gated: it counts as complete only when the grasp frame and the hand joints are back within tolerance of their reset values, and a timeout marks the cycle as a transition failure. The position and orientation tolerance is drawn anew for every return, and a return that has settled inside the largest tolerance is accepted, so the demonstrations contain returns of different accuracy. Table~\ref{tab:hybrid-params} in the appendix lists the thresholds and timings. While the hand is away from the nut the thread joint is held, so the nut cannot drift between cycles. On a completed return the turning reference is rebased without resetting physics. The next target turn is $60\degree$ minus the residual by which the total nut rotation misses the nearest multiple of $60\degree$, clamped to the trained range of target turns, so that a short turn is made up in the next cycle and the nut stays on the $60\degree$ grid of the hexagon. The frozen threading policy receives the nut orientation with whole $60\degree$ steps removed, which the hexagonal nut cannot distinguish, so that its observation stays within the initial-yaw range of training. The physical nut pose is not reset, so the demonstration contains the axial advance of the nut over ten cycles. The teacher population was first qualified for one period only. Repeated cycles are a property of the hybrid rollout, not of the \ac{RL} teacher alone. Figure~\ref{fig:datacollection} shows the coordinator that sequences one cycle.

The scripted transitions never write physics state. A transition may request that the thread joint be held while the hand is away, but it does not reposition the nut, so every recorded frame is a physically consistent consequence of the recorded commands. The transitions are also gated by physical events. Release is decided from the measured nut rotation, and the return is validated against the reset frame, so the phase labels in the recording coincide with physical events. Both properties allow the recorded phase to serve as a conditioning signal at deployment. There, the return is validated against the reset grasp pose with the same tolerances, while the release, for which the physical cell has no nut-angle sensor, is derived from the measured yaw of the grasp frame as a proxy for the nut rotation.

Twenty demonstrations contain too little variation for a student that must recover from its own deviations. Trajectory diversity is therefore increased at collection time by applying random impulses to the arm: one impulse is applied to a random arm link shortly after the start of every policy, waypoint, and return segment of every cycle, which gives 30 impulses per ten-cycle demonstration (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). The teacher, which is a closed-loop controller, reacts to the disturbance, and the recorded recovery is part of the label. The teacher is queried for its mean action and controls every step ($\beta=1$), so each recovery segment is the teacher's own response to the state that precedes it. Besides the impulses, the demonstrations vary through the task randomization that remains active during collection, which is listed at the end of this section. Demonstrations in which the teacher fails to complete all cycles are rejected, so the dataset contains only successful episodes.

The student perceives the scene through one \acs{RGBD} camera, so the demonstrations are rendered from a simulated copy of the physical camera. The extrinsic pose of the physical camera relative to the robot base is deduced from multiple poses of a calibrated AprilTag observed by the camera. The resulting pose and the intrinsics are used to render the simulated camera during data collection and enter the student training pipeline. The physical stream is cropped and downscaled to the $320\times180$ resolution that the simulated camera renders directly. Camera geometry and field of view therefore agree between the two domains up to calibration error. To cover this error, the pose of the simulated camera is shifted at every reset by a uniform offset of up to $\pm11.2$\,mm per axis and rotated by up to $\pm1.13\degree$ per axis, sized from the residual of the hand--eye fit (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). Appearance and depth quality differ between the two domains, because a rendered scene has one clean appearance and a rendered depth image contains no sensor noise. Texture randomization covers the first difference at collection time, and depth augmentation covers the second during training.

Texture randomization follows DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025} and varies the appearance of the rendered scene. At every reset, each environment draws new materials for the tabletop, the nut, and the bolt, each with a random texture, roughness, and metallicity. The dome light, with its texture, orientation, and intensity, and the floor texture are shared by all parallel environments and are redrawn at a reset with a fixed probability. An environment can also replace the far and invalid pixels of the RGB image with a random background image. These pixels lie outside the crop box of the point-cloud encoder (Section~\ref{sec:distillation}), so the background reaches only the image-space encoders of Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments}. The robot keeps its nominal appearance. Because the randomization is drawn at reset, the appearance is constant within a ten-cycle demonstration and differs between demonstrations. The offline loader cannot regenerate scene appearance from stored images, so this variation is recorded into the dataset once and reused unchanged by every student trained on it. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists the sampled ranges, and Figure~\ref{fig:texture-randomization} shows the resulting variation of the rendered scene.

Depth augmentation corrupts the stored metric depth images during student training. Rendered depth is exact, whereas a physical \acs{RGBD} camera returns noisy depth with missing regions, and the depth determines the position of every point that the encoder receives. The corruption is drawn anew for every training batch and acts before back-projection, so each epoch presents a different corruption of the same demonstration. It first perturbs the valid pixels with Gaussian noise whose standard deviation grows with the squared depth, adds a spatially smooth offset field, and quantizes the result. It then invalidates pixels at depth discontinuities, spatially correlated regions, and isolated pixels. Invalid pixels are rejected during back-projection, so an augmented frame yields a noisier point cloud with missing regions. The parameters in Table~\ref{tab:flow} approximate a commodity \acs{RGBD} camera qualitatively, and Figure~\ref{fig:depth-augmentation} shows their effect on a depth image next to a depth frame from the physical camera. The dataset stores the randomized RGB images together with the noise-free depth, so the same demonstrations can be trained with or without the augmentation.

The demonstrations are collected from the M24 teacher of Section~\ref{sec:teacher} with two training mechanisms switched off: success does not end the episode, and the target turn is not sampled but follows the $60\degree$ grid described above. Every environment starts from the nominal pose, without initial yaw and with the nut at the top of the thread, so that ten $60\degree$ turns, 1.67 revolutions or 5\,mm of axial travel on the 3\,mm pitch, fit on the bolt. Only part of the teacher's randomization stays active during collection: the noise of the proprioceptive observations, the friction of the arm joints and of the finger pads, the pose of the camera, and the visual appearance of the scene. The other quantities, namely the nut mass, the nut, bolt, and thread friction, the arm joint armature and damping, the controller parameters, the observation noise of the nut pose and the bolt frame, and the bolt and arm start poses, are fixed at their nominal values. The release and return of the scripted transitions run with the settings of Table~\ref{tab:datasets}. A cycle that fails is not discarded together with its episode: it is retried from the checkpoint of the last completed return, at most three times, and the rows of the abandoned attempt are removed from the episode. An episode may last up to 200\,s.

Each demonstration is materialized as one episode file with aligned 15\,Hz samples. A sample holds the RGB and metric depth images at $320\times180$ pixels, the proprioceptive state together with the fingertip forces and arm joint torques, from which the loader assembles the 35-D proprioception, the executed 9-D command, the active process phase, the nut pose for privileged ablations, and the timestamp, from which the normalized task progress is derived. Action chunks never span a reset, phase boundaries are retained so that the student learns the transitions, and terminal chunks are padded with the final command so that the last observations of an episode remain conditioning states.

\section{Student Policy Distillation by Conditional Flow Matching}\label{sec:distillation}

The hybrid teacher cannot be deployed, because both its learned policy and its scripted controllers act on privileged simulator state \eqref{eq:hybrid}. The student replaces it with one visuomotor policy that executes every phase of every cycle from sensor data, which is the deployable policy that Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-horizon} requires. The section first defines the flow-matching objective of the student, then the network that encodes its observation, and finally the offline training procedure.

The action head of the student is a conditional flow-matching model~\cite{Lipman2023,Lipman2024}, whose principle Section~\ref{sec:generative-background} introduced.
The head generates action chunks. Its training target is the demonstrated chunk of the next $H$ commands, $\Uc^{D}_t=[\act_t,\dots,\act_{t+H-1}]\in\R^{H\times9}$. The chunk is conditioned on the student observation $\obs_t=(\vec{O}^{\mathrm{RGBD}}_t,\vec{s}_t,p_t,\vec{e}^{m}_t)$. The \acs{RGBD} image $\vec{O}^{\mathrm{RGBD}}_t$ of the fixed camera shows the hand, the nut, and the bolt. The proprioception $\vec{s}_t$ holds the joint positions, the arm joint velocities, the previous action, the fingertip forces, and the arm joint torques. The normalized task progress $p_t\in[0,1]$ tells the student how far into the trial it is, and the process-phase encoding $\vec{e}^{m}_t$ marks whether the policy, the release waypoints, or the return is active. The network described below encodes these signals into one condition vector $\cond_t$, which takes the place of $\obs$ in \eqref{eq:fm-loss}. Training applies the straight-line construction of \eqref{eq:fm-line} to whole chunks:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:student-objective}
  \theta^{*}=\argmin_\theta\;\E\left\|\vfield\!\left(\Uc^{\lambda}_t,\lambda\mid\cond_t\right)-\dot{\Uc}^{\lambda}_t\right\|_2^{2},\qquad
  \Uc^{\lambda}_t=(1-\lambda)\Uc^{0}_t+\lambda\Uc^{D}_t,\quad
  \dot{\Uc}^{\lambda}_t=\Uc^{D}_t-\Uc^{0}_t,
\end{equation}
where the expectation is over $(\obs_t,\Uc^{D}_t)\sim\Dset$, a Gaussian chunk $\Uc^{0}_t\sim\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\mat{I})$ of the same shape, and $\lambda\sim U[0,1]$. At run time, the student draws a Gaussian chunk $\Uc^{0}$ and follows the learned field with $N$ explicit Euler steps, $\Uc^{\lambda+1/N}=\Uc^{\lambda}+\frac{1}{N}\vfield(\Uc^{\lambda},\lambda\mid\cond_t)$, with $N=16$ in simulation (Table~\ref{tab:flow} lists the deployment setting). The condition $\cond_t$ is computed once per chunk, so the image is encoded once and each Euler step evaluates only the Transformer described below.

A generative head is used because the demonstrations are multimodal. The teacher's recoveries after impulses depend on state that the observation does not fully resolve, so near-identical observations can carry the nominal action in one demonstration and a recovery in another. A regression head averages such labels, whereas the flow head samples one of them (Section~\ref{sec:generative-background}). Among generative heads, flow matching is chosen over diffusion because it trains on a plain regression target without a noise schedule and samples in a few deterministic integration steps.

Figure~\ref{fig:student} shows the network architecture of the student, which encodes the observation $\obs_t$ into the condition $\cond_t$ and predicts the flow velocity $\vfield$ of the action chunk from it. It consists of a vision encoder, a proprioception encoder, a fusion and conditioning stage, and the velocity network.

The student encodes the visual and proprioceptive streams separately and fuses them by concatenation, following DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025}, with the visual feature placed before the encoded proprioception. Separate encoders let each stream use a network suited to its structure, a set network for the unordered points and an \acs{MLP} for the fixed-length state vector. They also make the vision encoder exchangeable without changing the rest of the policy. The 35-D proprioception $\vec{s}_t$ consists of the ten controlled joint positions, the seven arm joint velocities, the previous filtered 9-D action, the two fingertip forces, and the seven arm joint torques, and is encoded by an \acs{MLP}. It contains only quantities that the physical system measures, so the same vector is available at deployment. The fingertip forces and the arm torques add contact information that the camera does not observe directly, such as whether the pinch still loads the nut. The concatenation of the 64-D visual feature and the proprioceptive feature is projected to the 256-D condition.

The vision encoder of the student is the point-cloud encoder of \ac{DP3}~\cite{Ze2024}. A re-grasp depends on the orientation of the nut faces relative to the fingers, and over ten cycles the nut advances down the bolt, so the student must read metric geometry from the camera. A point cloud carries this geometry explicitly, whereas an image-space encoder has to infer it from appearance (Section~\ref{sec:related-visuomotor}). The depth image is back-projected through the calibrated intrinsics into camera-frame points. Points outside a fixed crop box around the workspace or outside the valid depth range are rejected, so the encoder sees the hand, the nut, and the bolt rather than the background. The remaining points are normalized by fixed workspace constants and carry the color of their pixel. The encoder takes a fixed set of 4096 points per frame, so that frames can be batched, while the number of valid points varies from frame to frame. Each pixel therefore receives a ranking score, and the 4096 highest-ranked pixels form the input set. Valid pixels always rank above rejected ones, so rejected pixels enter the set only when a frame has fewer than 4096 valid points. These fill-in points stay marked as invalid and are excluded from the pooling below, so they cannot dominate the visual feature. Among the valid pixels, the score decides which subset is kept. During training it is drawn uniformly at random, so every update sees a different subset of the scene and the encoder cannot rely on individual points. At evaluation each pixel carries a fixed score from a golden-ratio sequence over the pixel index, which makes the selection deterministic and spreads it across the image without the cost of farthest-point sampling. A compact PointNet with pointwise layers and global max pooling over the valid points yields the 64-D visual feature $\vec{v}_t$. The encoder has no pretrained backbone. It is initialized randomly and trained jointly with the flow head, so that its representation is shaped by the action objective alone.

The sensor observation alone does not tell the student where it is in the cyclic task. Every cycle repeats the same grasp, so the scenes of the first and the last cycle differ mainly in the small axial position of the nut. At the end of a rotation, near-identical scenes precede either further turning or the release. Two low-dimensional conditions, the global trajectory progress and the process phase, supply this context and are added to the 256-D condition through separate learned embeddings. The global trajectory progress $p_t\in[0,1]$ maps the first timestamp of each demonstration to 0 and the last to 1. At deployment it advances with elapsed time from the start of the trial, normalized by the median demonstration duration stored in the checkpoint, and is clamped to 1 afterwards. It is a monotonic, non-periodic clock for the complete behavior and encodes neither the teacher stage nor the cycle index. It tells the student how far into a nominally timed trial it is, which disambiguates, for instance, the first and the last approach of a trial that otherwise look alike. The process-phase encoding $\vec{e}^{m}_t$ is a three-way one-hot over the policy-controlled phases, the release waypoints, and the return. During training it is derived from the recorded phase labels, and during evaluation it is published by the cyclic coordinator, in simulation from the physical events of Section~\ref{sec:hybrid} and on the robot from the release proxy and return gate, so that a cycle that takes longer than nominal cannot select the wrong phase. The phase encoding synchronizes the student with the cycle. The phase only selects the behavior and not the network. One network with one action contract serves all phases, and the student predicts the transition actions itself.

The velocity field $\vfield$ is parameterized by a non-causal trajectory Transformer. The noisy chunk $\Uc^{\lambda}$ is split into $H=8$ action tokens of dimension 9. A condition token embeds $\cond_t$, and a time token embeds the flow time $\lambda$ through a sinusoidal encoding. Learned positional embeddings mark the token slots and carry no absolute time, which enters only through the progress condition. The Transformer applies full self-attention over all tokens, so that every step may attend to the whole noisy chunk when predicting its velocity, and a linear output layer maps each action token to its 9-D velocity. The attention is non-causal because flow matching transports the whole chunk at once, so no step of the chunk is generated before another. One token per step lets the field model the correlation between the steps of the proposed trajectory directly, instead of flattening the chunk into a single vector. The model keeps an exponential moving average of its parameters, and inference uses the averaged weights, which fluctuate less than the weights after the last gradient step. Table~\ref{tab:flow} in the appendix lists the network sizes and the training hyperparameters.

The student predicts actions in chunks of $H=8$ steps, which cover 0.53\,s at the 15\,Hz control rate. Chunks keep consecutive actions within one demonstrated behavior and reduce the number of independent decisions over the long horizon (Section~\ref{sec:chunking-background}). The first action of a chunk is the command that acts on the observed state. Executing a whole chunk open loop would leave the student without feedback for 0.53\,s while the contact between fingers and nut changes. At deployment the student therefore replans at every control step, and the command sent to the controller is a temporal ensemble of all still-valid proposals for the current tick with weights $0.5^{k}$ for a proposal of age $k$. Replanning keeps the feedback at the control rate, and the ensemble keeps the continuity of the earlier proposals instead of discarding them at every replan. A decay of zero recovers strict first-action execution. The ensemble operates in action space and is distinct from the parameter-space \acs{EMA}. The executed 9-D action is clipped to $[-1,1]$, decoded to the filtered Cartesian target, and passed to the controller of Section~\ref{sec:action-contract}. Because the label scale is fixed by the action contract of Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}, no action statistics are learned, and the student output is decoded independently of the dataset it was trained on.

Distillation is offline rather than by \ac{DAgger}. \ac{DAgger} compares the actions of teacher and student at the same simulation step, whereas the flow head learns a distribution over action chunks and needs a fixed dataset of observations and their demonstrated chunks. The impulse recoveries of Section~\ref{sec:hybrid} take the place of the on-policy states that \ac{DAgger} would otherwise visit, because they show the student how the teacher returns from states off the nominal trajectory (Section~\ref{sec:il-background}). Algorithm~\ref{alg:offline} summarizes offline distillation. The dataset consists of the simulated hybrid-teacher rollouts, windows of $H$ consecutive commands are built from each episode without crossing episode boundaries, and the velocity field is trained with \eqref{eq:student-objective}.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]%
    \caption{Offline flow distillation}\label{alg:offline}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require hybrid teacher $\piHT$, horizon $H$
        \State Collect simulated rollouts $\Dsim$ with $\piHT$ under random arm impulses and visual domain randomization
        \State Form $H$-step chunks from $\Dset=\Dsim$, masking resets and padding terminal chunks
        \Repeat
            \State Sample $(\obs_t,\Uc^{D}_t)\sim\Dset$, $\Uc^{0}_t\sim\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\mat{I})$, $\lambda\sim U[0,1]$
            \State Encode the point cloud and fuse it with proprioception, progress, and phase to obtain $\cond_t$
            \State Update $\theta$ by one gradient step on \eqref{eq:student-objective}
            \State Update \acs{EMA} parameters $\bar{\theta}\leftarrow\alpha\bar{\theta}+(1-\alpha)\theta$
        \Until{convergence}
        \State \Return student velocity field $\vec{v}_{\bar{\theta}}$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}%
