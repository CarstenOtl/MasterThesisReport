\chapter{State of the Art}\label{ch:sota}%

This chapter provides the background that the method of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} builds on and places the thesis within related work. Section~\ref{sec:theory} introduces the theoretical concepts in the order in which the method uses them: the geometry of threaded joints that the thread model reproduces, reinforcement learning with privileged critics for the teacher, imitation learning and its compounding-error problem for the distillation, generative action heads and visual representations for the student, and operational-space control for the action path that teacher, student, and robot share. \section{Theoretical Background}\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Threaded Joints and Thread Geometry}\label{sec:thread-background}

Screws are divided into fastening screws and motion screws, and a nut on a bolt is a fastening joint~\cite{Niemann2019}. Threaded fasteners make a joint nonpermanent, so that it can be released again for field assembly, disassembly, maintenance, and adjustment~\cite{Budynas2020}. Their threads are standardized, for example in the ISO metric M series~\cite{Budynas2020}, whose geometry Figure~\ref{fig:iso-thread} shows. The thread converts a large circumferential motion into a small axial motion, and a small circumferential force into a large axial force~\cite{Niemann2019}. Assembling a threaded joint and securing it are treated as separate design topics~\cite{Niemann2019}. This thesis addresses the assembly motion, turning the nut along the thread, and not the securing of the joint.

The quantity that links rotation and axial travel is the pitch $\rho$, the distance between adjacent threads~\cite{Budynas2020}. For a single-start thread, adjacent threads are successive turns of one helix. A nut that turns by an angle $\Delta\theta$ about the bolt axis therefore advances along the axis by $\rho\,\Delta\theta/(2\pi)$, that is, by one pitch per revolution.

Because a partial turn advances the nut by only a fraction of a pitch, the axial position at which a hand must grasp the nut changes with every turn. The simplified thread model of Section~\ref{sec:thread-model} keeps exactly this relation: it replaces the flank contacts by a kinematic coupling that enforces one pitch of advance per revolution and represents the resistance of the thread to turning by a friction torque.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning with Privileged Information}\label{sec:rl-background}

Reinforcement learning formalizes sequential decision making as a \Ac{MDP} $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma)$ with state space $\mathcal{X}$, action space $\mathcal{A}$, transition kernel $P(\vec{x}_{t+1}\mid \vec{x}_t,\vec{a}_t)$, reward $r(\vec{x}_t,\vec{a}_t)$, and discount factor $\gamma\in[0,1)$~\cite{Kober2013}. A policy $\pi(\vec{a}\mid\vec{x})$ is sought that maximizes the expected discounted return $J(\pi)=\E_\pi[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t]$. In robot manipulation the full state is not observable at deployment. The problem is then a \ac{POMDP}, in which the policy acts on observations $\vec{z}_t\sim O(\cdot\mid\vec{x}_t)$ instead of states.

\Ac{PPO}~\cite{Schulman2017} is the on-policy actor--critic algorithm used throughout this thesis. The actor $\pi_\theta$ is updated by maximizing the clipped surrogate objective
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppo}
  L^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta) = \E_t\!\left[\min\!\left(\eta_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\; \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\eta_t(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)\hat{A}_t\right)\right],
  \qquad \eta_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(\vec{a}_t\mid\vec{z}_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\vec{a}_t\mid\vec{z}_t)},
\end{equation}
where $\eta_t(\theta)$ is the probability ratio between the new and the old policy, $\epsilon$ is the clipping parameter, and $\hat{A}_t$ is an advantage estimate computed from the critic's value prediction. The clipping bounds the policy change per update and makes \ac{PPO} stable on the thousands of parallel environments that GPU simulators provide.

In an asymmetric actor--critic the actor and the critic receive different inputs~\cite{Pinto2017}. The critic estimates the value function from the complete simulator state, including quantities that no sensor measures. The actor observes only a noisy subset that can later be reproduced on hardware or distilled into a sensor-based policy. Because the critic is discarded after training, the privileged information shapes the gradients without entering the deployed policy. When the actor observation is itself a noisy or delayed function of the state, a recurrent actor with a \ac{LSTM} cell~\cite{Hochreiter1997} integrates the observation history into a hidden state $\vec{h}_t$ and thereby recovers part of the information lost to partial observability. This recipe, together with domain randomization of physical and controller parameters, underlies the sim-to-real results in dexterous manipulation reported in~\cite{Handa2024,Petrenko2023,Lum2024,Singh2025}, and it is the recipe of the privileged teacher in Section~\ref{sec:teacher}.

\subsection{Imitation Learning and Compounding Error}\label{sec:il-background}

\Ac{IL} trains a policy from demonstrations rather than from a reward. Given a dataset $\Dset=\{(\obs_t,\act^{*}_t)\}$ of observations and expert actions, \ac{BC} fits a policy by supervised regression,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bc}
  \theta^{*}=\argmin_\theta \E_{(\obs,\act^{*})\sim\Dset}\!\left[\ell\!\left(\piS(\obs),\act^{*}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
with a per-sample loss $\ell$ such as the squared error. \ac{BC} is simple and data-efficient, but it is trained on the state distribution induced by the expert and evaluated on the state distribution induced by the student. Small prediction errors move the student into states the expert never visited, where its predictions are unreliable, and the error grows with the horizon. Ross et al.~\cite{Ross2011} show that the expected cost of a \ac{BC} policy with per-step error $\varepsilon$ can grow as $O(T^2\varepsilon)$ over a horizon $T$, because an error at step $t$ affects all subsequent steps. For a cyclic task that spans many cycles this quadratic growth is the central obstacle. A policy that reproduces each phase well in isolation can still fail after a few cycles once small deviations in grasp height or nut angle accumulate.

\Ac{DAgger}~\cite{Ross2011} addresses the distribution mismatch by querying the expert on the states that the student itself visits. In iteration $i$ the environment is driven by a mixture policy that executes the expert's action with probability $\beta_i$ and the student's action otherwise. The expert's action is recorded as the label for every visited state, the labeled states are added to the aggregated dataset, and the student is retrained. Under a no-regret learner, the resulting policy has expected cost $O(T\varepsilon)$, linear rather than quadratic in the horizon. The guarantee holds because the aggregated dataset converges to the state distribution of the learned policy itself, so that the supervised error measured on that dataset is the error the policy will actually incur. Behavior cloning, in contrast, measures its error on the expert's distribution, where the policy is never evaluated. The price is the $O(T)$ expert queries per iteration and the requirement that the expert be queryable at every visited state. The schedule $\beta_i$ is typically decayed from one toward zero, so that early iterations stay close to the expert's distribution and later iterations expose the student to its own mistakes. SafeDAgger~\cite{Zhang2016} reduces the number of expert queries by invoking the expert only when a learned safety classifier, or a threshold on the disagreement between expert and student actions, predicts that the student is about to fail. The predecessor of this thesis applied \ac{DAgger} and SafeDAgger to distill a privileged grasping teacher into an RGB student and found that the safety-gated variant produced both the best grasp success and the fewest unsafe episodes~\cite{Oertel2026}.

\ac{DAgger} therefore requires an expert that can be queried at arbitrary student-visited states. A privileged simulation policy satisfies this requirement. A recorded demonstration does not. The hybrid teacher of this thesis is such a queryable expert, and the method of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} uses this property in two ways: offline, by disturbing the teacher during collection so that it labels its own recoveries from off-nominal states, and online, through a \ac{DAgger} variant with a flow-matching head that serves as a comparison.

\subsection{Generative Action Heads: Diffusion and Flow Matching}\label{sec:generative-background}

A deterministic regression head predicts one action for each observation. Demonstrations of manipulation tasks are, however, often multimodal. Two demonstrators, or one teacher recovering from two different disturbances, may approach the same nut from different sides, and averaging their actions produces an action that neither of them would take. Generative action heads represent the full conditional distribution $p(\act\mid\obs)$ and sample from it at run time. Diffusion Policy~\cite{Chi2024} models the distribution of action sequences by iterative denoising. Gaussian noise is added to demonstrated action chunks in a forward process, a network is trained to predict the noise, and actions are generated by reversing the process over tens to hundreds of denoising steps. Diffusion policies handle multimodality and predict action chunks, which improves temporal consistency, but their sampling cost is high for control loops with tight latency budgets.

Flow matching~\cite{Lipman2023,Lipman2024} reaches the same goal with a simpler training target and a cheaper sampler. Instead of a stochastic denoising process it learns a time-dependent velocity field $\vec{v}_\theta(\vec{x},\lambda)$ that transports samples of a simple source distribution $p_0$, typically a standard Gaussian, to the data distribution $p_1$ along the \ac{ODE} $\dot{\vec{x}}=\vec{v}_\theta(\vec{x},\lambda)$ for $\lambda\in[0,1]$. The conditional flow-matching objective regresses the velocity field onto the velocity of a prescribed conditional probability path between a source sample and a data sample. With the linear interpolant $\vec{x}^{\lambda}=(1-\lambda)\vec{x}^{0}+\lambda\vec{x}^{1}$, whose velocity is $\vec{x}^{1}-\vec{x}^{0}$, the objective is a plain regression, and the learned \ac{ODE} can be integrated with a handful of Euler steps. Section~\ref{sec:flow-matching} derives this objective and states it for conditional action chunks.

Diffusion and flow matching are closely related. The forward noising process of a diffusion model defines a Gaussian probability path between data and noise, and its reverse process can equally be written as a deterministic probability-flow \ac{ODE} driven by the learned score. Flow matching with Gaussian conditional paths contains this diffusion path as a special case, and with the optimal-transport path it replaces the curved diffusion trajectories by straight lines that are cheaper to integrate~\cite{Lipman2023}. The practical consequences are the absence of a noise schedule, a regression target that is a simple difference of two samples, and samplers with few steps. Flow matching is the action head of vision-language-action models such as $\pi_0$~\cite{Black2026}, which generate 50-step action chunks with ten integration steps, and it is the action head of the student in this thesis. RQ2 asks whether this generative head distills the hybrid-teacher demonstrations better than deterministic regression with recurrent or Transformer backbones.

\subsection{Visual Representations for Manipulation}\label{sec:vision-background}

A visuomotor student must extract the nut pose relative to the hand from camera data. Three families of encoders are relevant here. Image-space encoders apply a convolutional backbone such as ResNet-18~\cite{He2016} to the RGB image, possibly with depth as an additional channel, and pool the feature map into a vector. They inherit ImageNet pretraining but have to learn metric geometry from appearance, and they must be made robust to texture and lighting with visual domain randomization~\cite{Tobin2017}. Point-cloud encoders back-project the depth image through the calibrated camera model into a set of 3D points, optionally attach the pixel color, and encode the set with a small shared point network followed by global max pooling in the spirit of PointNet~\cite{Qi2017}. The \ac{DP3} encoder~\cite{Ze2024} is such a compact point encoder. \ac{DP3} shows that it generalizes better across viewpoints and appearance than image encoders for diffusion-based visuomotor policies, because the metric geometry enters the representation directly. Adapt3R~\cite{Wilcox2025} combines the two families. Dense 2D features from a frozen pretrained backbone are localized in 3D by the depth map and expressed in the end-effector frame before attention pooling, which makes the representation invariant to the embodiment and robust to domain change. All three families are implemented in the code base of this thesis. Section~\ref{sec:student} explains why the \ac{DP3}-style encoder was retained, and RQ2 compares it with the image-space encoder.

\subsection{Operational-Space Control}\label{sec:osc-background}

Both the teacher and the student command a Cartesian end-effector target, and a task-space impedance controller converts this target to joint torques. Task-space motion control with torque inputs and impedance control are treated in standard references~\cite{Lynch2017,Chung2016,Villani2016}. This section states the law in the form that the thesis uses. Let $\vec{q}$ be the joint positions of the seven-DoF arm, $\mat{J}(\vec{q})$ the geometric Jacobian of the controlled frame, and $\vec{e}\in\R^6$ the position and axis-angle orientation error between target and current pose. The controller applies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:osc}
  \vec{\tau}=\mat{J}^{\mathrm{T}}\!\left(\mat{K}_p\vec{e}-\mat{K}_d\dot{\vec{x}}\right)+\mat{N}^{\mathrm{T}}\!\left(\mat{K}_{p,0}(\vec{q}_0-\vec{q})-\mat{K}_{d,0}\dot{\vec{q}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\dot{\vec{x}}$ is the end-effector twist, $\mat{K}_p$ and $\mat{K}_d=2\sqrt{\mat{K}_p}$ are diagonal stiffness and critical-damping gains, and the second term pulls the redundant seventh degree of freedom toward a rest posture $\vec{q}_0$ through a nullspace projector $\mat{N}$ with posture gains $\mat{K}_{p,0}$ and $\mat{K}_{d,0}$. The first term is a task-space spring and damper: the end effector behaves as if it were attached to the target by a virtual stiffness $\mat{K}_p$ and damping $\mat{K}_d$, which is the defining property of impedance control~\cite{Villani2016}.

The nullspace projector decides how strongly the posture term interferes with the task. It is dynamically consistent when $\mat{N}^{\mathrm{T}}=\mat{I}-\mat{J}^{\mathrm{T}}\bar{\mat{J}}^{\mathrm{T}}$ with the inertia-weighted pseudoinverse $\bar{\mat{J}}=\mat{M}^{-1}\mat{J}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mat{J}\mat{M}^{-1}\mat{J}^{\mathrm{T}})^{-1}$ and joint-space inertia $\mat{M}$~\cite{Dietrich2015,RoboticSystemsLab2019}. Torques in the nullspace then produce no acceleration of the controlled frame. A projector built from a pseudoinverse of the Jacobian without inertia weighting needs no inertia model, but it decouples the nullspace from the task only statically, so that posture torques can still disturb the task during motion~\cite{Dietrich2015}. The simulated controller uses the dynamically consistent projector, and the controller on the physical robot uses a damped kinematic one; Section~\ref{sec:real-setup} describes this difference. Equation~\eqref{eq:osc} is the Factory and FORGE controller~\cite{Narang2022,Noseworthy2025}, which this thesis inherits. Scaling the spring and damper term by the task-space inertia $\mat{\Lambda}=(\mat{J}\mat{M}^{-1}\mat{J}^{\mathrm{T}})^{-1}$ and compensating the nonlinear dynamics yields the full operational-space formulation, in which the closed loop becomes a decoupled unit-mass system in every task direction~\cite{Lynch2017}.

Impedance control is preferred over stiff position control for a contact-rich task because the compliant law yields on unexpected contact rather than driving through it~\cite{Villani2016}, and because the policy can shape the interaction by commanding pose offsets rather than torques. The compliance is also what allows the simplified thread model of Section~\ref{sec:thread-model} to tolerate small deviations of the hand from the helical path.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

The related work is ordered along the three difficulties of threading. Section~\ref{sec:related-assembly} reviews robotic assembly and threading, the application itself. Section~\ref{sec:related-dexterous} reviews learned dexterous manipulation (difficulty~(b)), Section~\ref{sec:related-sim2real} reviews sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich manipulation (difficulty~(a)), and Sections~\ref{sec:related-chaining} and~\ref{sec:related-distillation} review long-horizon tasks and the teacher--student distillation with which this thesis addresses them (difficulty~(c)). Section~\ref{sec:related-gap} states the gap.

\subsection{Robotic Assembly and Threading}\label{sec:related-assembly}

Assembly tasks such as peg-in-hole insertion, gear meshing, connector mating, screwdriving, and nut--bolt fastening demand precise motion and closed-loop contact under geometric and dynamic uncertainty~\cite{Siciliano2016}. In industrial practice, threaded joints are assembled with dedicated tools such as nutrunners and screwdriving spindles, and fixtures present the parts in a known pose.
% TODO(cite): industrial practice of threaded-joint assembly (nutrunners, screwdriving spindles, fixtures, parts in a fixed pose).
Learning-based assembly in simulation aims to relax these requirements. Factory~\cite{Narang2022} made nut-and-bolt assembly learnable by combining signed-distance-field collision detection, contact reduction, and a Gauss--Seidel solver, so that a thousand parallel assemblies run in real time on one GPU. It also splits the task into pick, place, and screw subpolicies. FORGE~\cite{Noseworthy2025} adds force-guided exploration under pose uncertainty and demonstrates a multistage planetary-gear assembly that includes nut threading, and IndustReal~\cite{Tang2023industreal} transfers simulation-trained assembly policies to a physical arm. All three use a parallel-jaw gripper.

Screwdriving with hand tools has been modeled analytically and learned with physics-informed models~\cite{Tang2023,Tang2024screwdriving,Manyar2023}. With multi-fingered hands, unscrewing a bottle lid, a threaded joint released by turning, has been learned with two hands by sim-to-real \ac{RL}~\cite{Lin2024} and with one hand from teleoperated demonstrations with a visuomotor diffusion policy~\cite{Koczy2025}. Play2Perfect~\cite{Lum2026} pretrains a goal-conditioned manipulation policy through play and fine-tunes it for precise assembly, including screwing a rod-like furniture leg. This gives goal-directed screwing in a structured setting, but not for everyday threaded parts such as nuts. The closest prior work is DexScrew~\cite{Hsieh2026}, which uses a hand--arm setup comparable to the one here and replaces the thread geometry by a revolute-joint proxy to learn a rotational finger gait. The gait assists teleoperation and supports the collection of real demonstrations for tactile behavior cloning. Its policy spins the fastener without a target angle and without learned termination. Goal-directed threading over many grasp-and-release cycles, which this thesis addresses for several nut sizes, is left open by these works.

\subsection{Dexterous Manipulation with Learned Policies}\label{sec:related-dexterous}

A multi-fingered hand removes the need for task-specific tooling, but it brings high-dimensional control and complex contact dynamics with it~\cite{BicchiKumar2000,Weinberg2024}. Analytical models of motion and contact have rarely coped with the uncertainty of real environments, and the field has therefore turned to learning~\cite{Weinberg2024}. Model-free \ac{RL} in simulation learns in-hand reorientation of more than two thousand objects~\cite{Chen2021}. DeXtreme~\cite{Handa2024} transfers agile in-hand reorientation to a low-cost hand with a vision-based pose estimator, and DexPBT~\cite{Petrenko2023} learns regrasping and grasp-and-throw for hand--arm systems with population-based training. DextrAH-G~\cite{Lum2024} and DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025} grasp a wide range of objects with a hand--arm system from depth and RGB images, and SimToolReal~\cite{Kedia2026} manipulates tool-like objects to random goal poses and generalizes zero-shot to real tools. Without learning, finger gaits that pivot an object under contact-force control have been demonstrated~\cite{Xue2023}.

These works name the high-dimensional action space of the hand as a central challenge~\cite{Lum2024,Chen2023}. Most of them solve one skill per episode: a grasp, a goal pose of a tool, or a target orientation. In-hand reorientation chains goals and counts consecutive successes~\cite{Handa2024}, but the object stays in the hand. Threading additionally requires the hand to release the part and acquire it again while the bolt constrains it, and the grasp must hold under the turning load in between. This thesis narrows the solution space to one grasp strategy, a thumb--index pinch, and lets the learned policy control this pinch together with the arm (Section~\ref{sec:system}).

\subsection{Sim-to-Real Transfer for Contact-Rich Manipulation}\label{sec:related-sim2real}

Policies trained in simulation meet discrepancies in perception, dynamics, contact, and control when they are deployed on hardware, and the discrepancies grow with the dimensionality of the control problem and the amount of contact involved. Thread contact is an extreme case, because engaged threads produce thousands of simultaneous contacts~\cite{Narang2022}. The most common response is to make the policy robust enough for zero-shot transfer, by randomizing visual and physical parameters during training~\cite{Tobin2017,Handa2024}, by randomizing dynamics together with a force threshold~\cite{Noseworthy2025}, or by making the visual representation itself transferable~\cite{Wilcox2025}. A second family adapts a simulation-trained policy with real-world data, for instance by tuning the simulator to a few minutes of real data~\cite{Lin2025}, by learning a residual policy from on-robot corrections~\cite{Jiang2025transic}, or by continuing training on the robot~\cite{Luo2025,Huang2025}.

This thesis follows the first family, and RQ3 asks whether it suffices for threading. The student is trained on simulated data only, and transfer rests on four properties of the training setup. The camera model is calibrated against the physical cell and reproduced in simulation. The visual representation is a metric point cloud in the spirit of \ac{DP3}~\cite{Ze2024}, which is invariant to appearance up to depth noise. The rendered appearance is randomized in the style of DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025}. The Cartesian action is executed on the robot by a controller that reproduces the simulated control law. The approach requires no real-world data collection and no training on the robot.

\subsection{Long-Horizon Tasks and Policy Chaining}\label{sec:related-chaining}

A single policy for a long-horizon task faces two difficulties. Small control errors compound over the horizon~\cite{Ross2011}, and fine-tuning the policy on later subtasks can erase what it learned on earlier ones, the catastrophic forgetting problem~\cite{Kirkpatrick2017}. Larger networks, frozen or regularized parameters, and architectures with longer temporal memory such as recurrent networks and Transformers mitigate these effects but do not remove them. At large scale, a single policy can nevertheless be trained end to end. AlphaNIST~\cite{Zhang2026} trains policies from scratch with a sparse binary task reward and without demonstrations, progress shaping, or scripted primitives, and its policies complete ten assembly pairs in one simulated episode without a prescribed order.

The alternative is to decompose the task into skills with their own policies and to chain them. Each skill is then easier to learn, but the chain introduces a transition problem. The states in which one skill terminates may lie outside the initial-state distribution on which the next skill was trained, so the chain breaks at the boundary. Adversarial skill chaining~\cite{Lee2022} regularizes the terminal-state distribution of each skill toward the initial-state distribution of its successor. Sequential Dexterity~\cite{Chen2023} learns a transition-feasibility function with which adjacent dexterous policies are fine-tuned, switches between them autonomously, and recovers from failed transitions. A cyclic task sharpens the transition problem, because the same boundaries recur in every cycle while the task state advances, so every transition error has many chances to occur and its effect carries into the next cycle.

The method of this thesis decomposes only during data generation, where every phase has a simple specification, and unifies at deployment by distilling the chained demonstrations into one policy. The transitions are then part of what the student learns rather than a problem to be solved between separate policies. RQ1 tests whether this unified student sustains consecutive cycles better than a single-cycle policy that is invoked repeatedly.

\subsection{Teacher--Student and Visuomotor Distillation}\label{sec:related-distillation}

Learning by Cheating~\cite{Chen2020} established the route from a policy that is trained with privileged simulator state to a deployable policy that acts from onboard sensing. In dexterous manipulation, DextrAH-G~\cite{Lum2024} and DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025} distill a privileged \ac{RL} grasping teacher into depth- and RGB-based students with online \ac{DAgger}. UniGraspTransformer~\cite{Wang2025} consolidates many object-specific teachers into one student, and Lin et al.~\cite{Lin2025} learn separate single-task \ac{RL} policies on a humanoid and distill them into one generalist vision-based policy. The author's semester thesis~\cite{Oertel2026} applied this recipe to a 23-DoF hand--arm system and compared behavior cloning, \ac{DAgger}, and SafeDAgger.

For long-horizon behavior, two further ingredients matter. Predicting a sequence of future actions instead of a single action improves temporal consistency. \Ac{ACT}~\cite{Zhao2023} predicts chunks with a Transformer and blends overlapping chunks by temporal ensembling. Representing the action distribution generatively lets a policy keep several valid behaviors apart instead of averaging them. Diffusion Policy~\cite{Chi2024} models multimodal action sequences by iterative denoising, \ac{DP3}~\cite{Ze2024} conditions it on compact point-cloud features, and flow matching~\cite{Lipman2023} learns a continuous vector field that generates actions by integrating an \ac{ODE} in few steps, as the vision-language-action model $\pi_0$~\cite{Black2026} does. The student of this thesis combines a point-cloud encoder, action chunking with temporal ensembling, and a flow-matching head, because its training data mix nominal teacher actions with the teacher's recoveries from disturbances.

\subsection{Summary and Research Gap}\label{sec:related-gap}

The related work addresses each difficulty of threading separately. Simulation-based assembly learning makes thread contact tractable and transfers to hardware, but with a parallel-jaw gripper and a fastener that is already held~\cite{Narang2022,Noseworthy2025,Tang2023industreal}. Learned dexterous threading and screwing covers a spinning gait for an engaged fastener~\cite{Hsieh2026} and goal-conditioned screwing of a furniture leg~\cite{Lum2026}. Policy chaining reaches long horizons at the cost of transitions between separately deployed policies~\cite{Lee2022,Chen2023}, and teacher--student distillation yields deployable visuomotor policies for single skills~\cite{Lum2024,Singh2025}. No prior work threads a nut over many cycles with learned grasping and re-grasping on a dexterous hand. Chapter~\ref{ch:method} closes this gap by combining the concepts of Section~\ref{sec:theory}: a simplified thread model makes \ac{RL} in simulation feasible, a privileged teacher learns the pinch-grasp turn, scripted transitions chain it into multi-cycle demonstrations, and one flow-matching student distills these demonstrations for zero-shot deployment.
