\section{Theoretical Background}\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Threaded Joints and Thread Geometry}\label{sec:thread-background}

Screws are divided into fastening screws and motion screws, and a nut on a bolt is a fastening joint~\cite{Niemann2019}. Threaded fasteners make a joint nonpermanent, so that it can be released again for field assembly, disassembly, maintenance, and adjustment~\cite{Budynas2020}. Their threads are standardized, for example in the ISO metric M series~\cite{Budynas2020}, whose geometry Figure~\ref{fig:iso-thread} shows. The thread converts a large circumferential motion into a small axial motion, and a small circumferential force into a large axial force~\cite{Niemann2019}. Assembling a threaded joint and securing it are treated as separate design topics~\cite{Niemann2019}. This thesis addresses the assembly motion, turning the nut along the thread, and not the securing of the joint.

For a single-start thread, adjacent threads are successive turns of one helix. A nut that turns by an angle $\Delta\theta$ about the bolt axis therefore advances along the axis by $\rho\,\Delta\theta/(2\pi)$, that is, by one pitch per revolution.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning}\label{sec:rl-background}

In \ac{RL}, an agent learns a behavior from interaction and a scalar reward, not from demonstrations~\cite{Sutton2018,Kober2013}. At every step an agent observes its environment, chooses an action, and receives a reward that rates the outcome. The agent has to discover the actions that lead to high reward by trying them, and it has to attribute a reward to the earlier actions that made it possible.

The interaction is formalized as a \ac{MDP} $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma)$ with state space $\mathcal{X}$, action space $\mathcal{A}$, transition kernel $P(\vec{x}_{t+1}\mid \vec{x}_t,\vec{a}_t)$, reward $r(\vec{x}_t,\vec{a}_t)$, and discount factor $\gamma\in[0,1)$~\cite{Sutton2018}. The Markov property states that the next state depends only on the current state and action, not on the earlier history. A stochastic policy $\pi(\vec{a}\mid\vec{x})$ maps states to distributions over actions. Executing it generates a trajectory $\tau=(\vec{x}_0,\vec{a}_0,r_0,\vec{x}_1,\dots)$, and the goal is a policy that maximizes the expected discounted return
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rl-objective}
  J(\pi)=\E_{\tau\sim p_\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r_t\right].
\end{equation}
The discount factor sets how far ahead the agent plans. A reward that lies $t$ steps in the future is weighted by $\gamma^{t}$, so rewards beyond roughly $1/(1-\gamma)$ steps contribute little.

Value functions measure how good a state or an action is under a given policy. The state value $V^{\pi}(\vec{x})=\E_\pi[\sum_{l\geq0}\gamma^{l}r_{t+l}\mid\vec{x}_t=\vec{x}]$ is the expected return when the policy starts in $\vec{x}$. The action value $Q^{\pi}(\vec{x},\vec{a})$ is the expected return when the agent first takes $\vec{a}$ and then follows $\pi$. Their difference is the advantage
\begin{equation}\label{eq:advantage}
  A^{\pi}(\vec{x},\vec{a})=Q^{\pi}(\vec{x},\vec{a})-V^{\pi}(\vec{x}),
\end{equation}
which is positive for actions that are better than the policy's average behavior in that state and negative for worse ones. The state value satisfies the Bellman equation $V^{\pi}(\vec{x}_t)=\E_\pi[r_t+\gamma V^{\pi}(\vec{x}_{t+1})]$, which relates the value of a state to the reward of one step and the value of its successor~\cite{Sutton2018}. This recursion lets a value function be learned from single transitions instead of complete trajectories.

Value functions rate states and actions, but the policy itself can be improved directly by gradient ascent on the expected discounted return $J$ of \eqref{eq:rl-objective}. This requires a differentiable policy $\pi_\theta$, here a neural network with parameters $\theta$, and a gradient of $J$ that can be estimated from sampled trajectories. The likelihood-ratio form of the policy gradient,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:policy-gradient}
  \nabla_\theta J(\pi_\theta)=\E_{\tau\sim p_{\pi_\theta}}\!\left[\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(\vec{a}_t\mid\vec{x}_t)\,\Psi_t\right],
\end{equation}
estimates this gradient from sampled trajectories alone, without a model of the transition kernel~\cite{Williams1992,Sutton2018}. The weight $\Psi_t$ scores the action $\vec{a}_t$. With the return that follows step $t$ as $\Psi_t$, as in REINFORCE~\cite{Williams1992}, the estimate is unbiased but has high variance, because the return accumulates the randomness of every later action and transition.

Actor--critic methods reduce this variance by learning a value function alongside the policy. The actor is the policy $\pi_\theta$, and the critic is a value network $V_\phi$ that is regressed onto the observed returns. The critic replaces the return in $\Psi_t$ by an estimate of the advantage \eqref{eq:advantage}, that is, of how much better an action turned out than the critic expected. After one step, the critic compares its expectation $V_\phi(\vec{x}_t)$ with the observed reward plus its estimate of the next state. The difference is the temporal-difference residual $\delta_t=r_t+\gamma V_\phi(\vec{x}_{t+1})-V_\phi(\vec{x}_t)$, which is positive when the step went better than expected. \Ac{GAE}~\cite{Schulman2016} averages these residuals over the following steps with exponentially decaying weights,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gae}
  \hat{A}_t=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(\gamma\lambda_{\mathrm{GAE}})^{l}\,\delta_{t+l},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\mathrm{GAE}}\in[0,1]$ trades the bias of the critic against the variance of the observed returns.

The critic is needed only to compute $\hat{A}_t$ during training and is discarded afterwards. It may therefore receive information that the actor will not have at deployment. In an asymmetric actor--critic~\cite{Pinto2017}, the critic observes the full, noise-free simulator state, while the actor acts only on the observations available to it. The privileged critic gives more accurate value estimates and therefore better advantages for the actor, but the actor never depends on the privileged state.

\Acf{PPO}~\cite{Schulman2017} is an on-policy actor--critic algorithm. On-policy methods estimate the gradient from data collected by the current policy, so every batch becomes outdated as soon as the policy changes. \Ac{PPO} reuses each batch for several epochs of minibatch updates and keeps the updated policy close to the one that collected the data. It maximizes the clipped surrogate objective
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppo}
  L^{\mathrm{clip}}(\theta) = \E_t\!\left[\min\!\left(\eta_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\; \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\eta_t(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)\hat{A}_t\right)\right],
  \qquad \eta_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(\vec{a}_t\mid\vec{x}_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\vec{a}_t\mid\vec{x}_t)},
\end{equation}
where $\eta_t(\theta)$ is the probability ratio between the new and the old policy and $\epsilon$ is the clipping parameter. Once the ratio leaves the interval $[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$ in the direction that the advantage favors, the objective stops growing, and the gradient no longer pushes the policy further. The full loss adds a squared-error loss for the critic and an optional entropy bonus that keeps the policy stochastic,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppo-loss}
  L(\theta,\phi)=-L^{\mathrm{clip}}(\theta)+c_V\,\E_t\!\left[\big(V_\phi(\vec{x}_t)-\hat{R}_t\big)^{2}\right]-c_H\,\E_t\!\left[\mathcal{H}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid\vec{x}_t)\big)\right],
\end{equation}
with the return target $\hat{R}_t=\hat{A}_t+V_{\phi}(\vec{x}_t)$, the critic coefficient $c_V$, the entropy coefficient $c_H$, and the policy entropy $\mathcal{H}$~\cite{Schulman2017}. One training epoch collects a rollout of fixed length from every environment, computes the advantages \eqref{eq:gae}, and then runs several passes of minibatch gradient descent on \eqref{eq:ppo-loss}. Figure~\ref{fig:ppo-loop} summarizes this loop. On-policy learning needs many samples, and GPU-parallel simulators supply them by stepping thousands of environments at once. On such simulators, \ac{RL} has trained in-hand reorientation that transferred to hardware~\cite{Handa2024} and regrasping and throwing with simulated hand--arm systems~\cite{Petrenko2023}.

The reward is the only specification of the task that the agent receives, and its design largely decides whether learning succeeds~\cite{Kober2013}. A sparse reward that pays only on success is easy to specify, but in a high-dimensional action space random exploration almost never reaches success, so the agent receives no learning signal. Reward shaping adds dense terms that pay for intermediate progress, such as moving the hand toward the object or turning it toward the goal. Shaping can, however, change which policy is optimal. Ng et al.~\cite{Ng1999} show that a shaping term of the form $F(\vec{x}_t,\vec{x}_{t+1})=\gamma\Phi(\vec{x}_{t+1})-\Phi(\vec{x}_t)$, with an arbitrary potential function $\Phi$ over states, leaves the optimal policy unchanged, and that other shaping terms carry no such guarantee. A term that is not of this form may be exploited. The agent optimizes the reward as written rather than the task as intended. A term that pays for every reduction of a distance, for example, pays again each time the agent moves away and comes back, so a policy can collect it by oscillating. In simulation the agent can also exploit inaccuracies of the physics, as the interpenetration through the thread flanks of Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-sim2real} illustrates.

\ac{RL} thus produces a policy from a reward alone, at the cost of many simulated trials and a carefully shaped reward. When an expert policy or recorded demonstrations are available, a policy can instead be learned by supervised imitation, which the next subsection introduces.

\subsection{Imitation Learning}\label{sec:il-background}

\Ac{IL} is the problem of learning a policy from the demonstrations of an expert rather than from a reward. \Ac{IL} methods differ in how they use the demonstrations. The simplest method, \ac{BC}, reduces \ac{IL} to supervised learning~\cite{Ross2011}. It treats every demonstrated pair of observation and expert action as a labeled training example and fits the learned policy, called the student, to these labels. Given a dataset $\Dset=\{(\obs_t,\act^{*}_t)\}$, \ac{BC} solves the regression problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bc}
  \theta^{*}=\argmin_\theta \E_{(\obs,\act^{*})\sim\Dset}\!\left[\ell\!\left(\piS(\obs),\act^{*}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
with a per-sample loss $\ell$ such as the squared error. \ac{BC} is trained on the states that the expert visits but evaluated on the states that the student visits. Small prediction errors move the student into states that are not covered by the demonstrations, and the errors compound. Ross et al.~\cite{Ross2011} show that the expected cost of a \ac{BC} policy with per-step error $\varepsilon$ can grow as $O(T^2\varepsilon)$ over a horizon $T$. For a cyclic task, small deviations in grasp height or nut angle carry over from one cycle into the next.

\Ac{DAgger}~\cite{Ross2011} counters this by querying the expert on the states that the student itself visits. The environment is driven by a mixture that executes the expert's action with probability $\beta$ and the student's action otherwise, the expert labels every visited state, and the student is retrained on the aggregated dataset. The expected cost then grows only linearly in the horizon. \ac{DAgger} requires an expert that can be queried at arbitrary states. A policy in simulation satisfies this requirement, a recorded demonstration does not.

\subsection{Generative Action Heads: Diffusion and Flow Matching}\label{sec:generative-background}

A deterministic regression head predicts one action for each observation, in the same way that the policy network of Section~\ref{sec:rl-background} outputs one mean action. Trained by \ac{BC} with the squared error \eqref{eq:bc}, it converges to the mean of the demonstrated actions for a given observation. The mean is the right target as long as the expert acts the same way in the same situation. Demonstrations of manipulation tasks are, however, often multimodal: the same observation is followed by different, equally valid actions. In the Push-T task of Chi et al.~\cite{Chi2024}, for example, the end effector can pass the block on the left or on the right to push it into place. Averaging the two demonstrations yields a motion between them that neither demonstration contains.

A generative action head avoids this averaging. It learns the full conditional distribution $p(\act\mid\obs)$ and draws a sample from it at run time, so different samples can land in different modes, and each sample is an action that a demonstrator could have taken. Two families of generative heads are common in robot learning, diffusion and flow matching. Both start from Gaussian noise and transform it step by step into an action, but they learn this transformation differently.

Diffusion Policy~\cite{Chi2024} learns to remove noise. During training, Gaussian noise of a random magnitude is added to a demonstrated action, and a network that is conditioned on the observation predicts the added noise. At run time, sampling starts from pure noise and applies the network over a fixed number of denoising iterations, each of which removes part of the predicted noise. The number of iterations sets the inference cost. Chi et al.\ train with 100 iterations and sample with 10 using an accelerated sampler, which gives an inference latency of 0.1\,s on a desktop GPU~\cite{Chi2024}. Diffusion Policy also predicts a sequence of future actions, called an action chunk, instead of a single action, and executes several of them before it predicts again~\cite{Chi2024,Zhao2023}. All actions of a chunk come from the same sample. The policy therefore commits to one mode for the duration of the chunk instead of switching between modes from one step to the next, which would produce jittery motion.

Flow matching~\cite{Lipman2023,Lipman2024} learns the transformation from noise to action as a velocity field. Each noise sample can be pictured as a particle that moves during a flow time $\lambda$ from 0 to 1. A network $\vec{v}_\theta(\act,\lambda\mid\obs)$ predicts the velocity of the particle at position $\act$ and time $\lambda$, and integrating this velocity from a noise sample $\act^{0}$ at $\lambda=0$ yields an action $\act^{1}$ at $\lambda=1$. The velocity field that maps the noise distribution onto the demonstrated actions is unknown, but a training target can be built from pairs of a noise sample and a demonstrated action. The simplest construction connects the two by a straight line,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fm-line}
  \act^{\lambda}=(1-\lambda)\act^{0}+\lambda\act^{1},\qquad \act^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\mat{I}),
\end{equation}
along which the velocity is the constant $\act^{1}-\act^{0}$. Training regresses the network onto this velocity at random points of random lines,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fm-loss}
  L(\theta)=\E_{(\obs,\act^{1})\sim\Dset,\;\act^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\mat{I}),\;\lambda\sim U[0,1]}\left\|\vec{v}_\theta(\act^{\lambda},\lambda\mid\obs)-\left(\act^{1}-\act^{0}\right)\right\|_2^{2}.
\end{equation}
Many lines pass through the same point, so the network learns the average of their velocities. Lipman et al.~\cite{Lipman2023} show that this average is exactly the unknown velocity field, so the simple loss \eqref{eq:fm-loss} trains the correct model. At run time, an action is generated by drawing $\act^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\mat{I})$ and following the learned field with a few explicit Euler steps $\act^{\lambda+\Delta\lambda}=\act^{\lambda}+\Delta\lambda\,\vec{v}_\theta(\act^{\lambda},\lambda\mid\obs)$. Because every training path is straight, the generated paths are nearly straight as well, and few steps suffice~\cite{Lipman2023}.

Diffusion and flow matching are closely related. A diffusion model corresponds to flow matching with a particular Gaussian path between noise and data, whose trajectories are curved. The straight-line path of \eqref{eq:fm-line} replaces them by straighter trajectories that need fewer integration steps~\cite{Lipman2023}. Figure~\ref{fig:diffusion-vs-fm} contrasts the two. In practice, flow matching needs no noise schedule, its regression target is the difference of two samples, and it samples deterministically from a given noise sample. Flow matching is the action head of vision-language-action models such as $\pi_0$~\cite{Black2026}, which generate 50-step action chunks with ten integration steps.

\subsection{Visual Representations for Manipulation}\label{sec:vision-background}

A visuomotor student must extract the nut pose relative to the hand from camera data. Three families of encoders are relevant here. Image-space encoders apply a convolutional backbone such as ResNet-18~\cite{He2016} to the RGB image, possibly with depth as an additional channel, and pool the feature map into a vector. They inherit ImageNet pretraining but have to learn metric geometry from appearance, and they must be made robust to texture and lighting with visual domain randomization~\cite{Tobin2017}. Point-cloud encoders back-project the depth image through the calibrated camera model into a set of 3D points, optionally attach the pixel color, and encode the set with a small shared point network followed by global max pooling in the spirit of PointNet~\cite{Qi2017}. The \ac{DP3} encoder~\cite{Ze2024} is such a compact point encoder. \ac{DP3} shows that it generalizes better across viewpoints and appearance than image encoders for diffusion-based visuomotor policies, because the metric geometry enters the representation directly. Adapt3R~\cite{Wilcox2025} combines the two families. Dense 2D features from a frozen pretrained backbone are localized in 3D by the depth map and expressed in the end-effector frame before attention pooling, which makes the representation invariant to the embodiment and robust to domain change.

\subsection{Operational-Space Control}\label{sec:osc-background}

A learned policy can command a Cartesian end-effector target, which a task-space impedance controller converts to joint torques. Task-space motion control with torque inputs and impedance control are treated in standard references~\cite{Lynch2017,Chung2016,Villani2016}. This section states the law in the form that the thesis uses. Let $\vec{q}$ be the joint positions of the seven-DoF arm, $\mat{J}(\vec{q})$ the geometric Jacobian of the controlled frame, and $\vec{e}\in\R^6$ the position and axis-angle orientation error between target and current pose. The controller applies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:osc}
  \vec{\tau}=\mat{J}^{\mathrm{T}}\!\left(\mat{K}_p\vec{e}-\mat{K}_d\dot{\vec{x}}\right)+\mat{N}^{\mathrm{T}}\!\left(\mat{K}_{p,0}(\vec{q}_0-\vec{q})-\mat{K}_{d,0}\dot{\vec{q}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\dot{\vec{x}}$ is the end-effector twist, $\mat{K}_p$ and $\mat{K}_d=2\sqrt{\mat{K}_p}$ are diagonal stiffness and critical-damping gains, and the second term pulls the redundant seventh degree of freedom toward a rest posture $\vec{q}_0$ through a nullspace projector $\mat{N}$ with posture gains $\mat{K}_{p,0}$ and $\mat{K}_{d,0}$. The first term is a task-space spring and damper: the end effector behaves as if it were attached to the target by a virtual stiffness $\mat{K}_p$ and damping $\mat{K}_d$, which is the defining property of impedance control~\cite{Villani2016}.

The nullspace projector decides how strongly the posture term interferes with the task. It is dynamically consistent when $\mat{N}^{\mathrm{T}}=\mat{I}-\mat{J}^{\mathrm{T}}\bar{\mat{J}}^{\mathrm{T}}$ with the inertia-weighted pseudoinverse $\bar{\mat{J}}=\mat{M}^{-1}\mat{J}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mat{J}\mat{M}^{-1}\mat{J}^{\mathrm{T}})^{-1}$ and joint-space inertia $\mat{M}$~\cite{Dietrich2015,RoboticSystemsLab2019}. Torques in the nullspace then produce no acceleration of the controlled frame. A projector built from a pseudoinverse of the Jacobian without inertia weighting needs no inertia model, but it decouples the nullspace from the task only statically, so that posture torques can still disturb the task during motion~\cite{Dietrich2015}. The simulated controller uses the dynamically consistent projector, and the controller on the physical robot uses a damped kinematic one; Section~\ref{sec:real-setup} describes this difference. Equation~\eqref{eq:osc} is the Factory and FORGE controller~\cite{Narang2022,Noseworthy2025}. Scaling the spring and damper term by the task-space inertia $\mat{\Lambda}=(\mat{J}\mat{M}^{-1}\mat{J}^{\mathrm{T}})^{-1}$ and compensating the nonlinear dynamics yields the full operational-space formulation, in which the closed loop becomes a decoupled unit-mass system in every task direction~\cite{Lynch2017}.

Impedance control is preferred over stiff position control for a contact-rich task because the compliant law yields on unexpected contact rather than driving through it~\cite{Villani2016}, and because the policy can shape the interaction by commanding pose offsets rather than torques.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

The related work is ordered along the three difficulties of threading. Section~\ref{sec:related-assembly} reviews robotic assembly and threading, the application itself. Section~\ref{sec:related-dexterous} reviews learned dexterous manipulation (difficulty~(b)), Section~\ref{sec:related-sim2real} reviews sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich manipulation (difficulty~(a)), and Sections~\ref{sec:related-chaining} and~\ref{sec:related-distillation} review long-horizon tasks and the teacher--student distillation with which this thesis addresses them (difficulty~(c)). Section~\ref{sec:related-gap} states the gap.

\subsection{Robotic Assembly and Threading}\label{sec:related-assembly}

Assembly tasks such as peg-in-hole insertion, gear meshing, connector mating, screwdriving, and nut--bolt fastening demand precise motion and closed-loop contact under geometric and dynamic uncertainty~\cite{Siciliano2016}. In industrial practice, threaded joints are assembled with dedicated tools such as nutrunners and screwdriving spindles, and fixtures present the parts in a known pose.
% TODO(cite): industrial practice of threaded-joint assembly (nutrunners, screwdriving spindles, fixtures, parts in a fixed pose).
Learning-based assembly in simulation aims to relax these requirements. Factory~\cite{Narang2022} made nut-and-bolt assembly learnable by combining signed-distance-field collision detection, contact reduction, and a Gauss--Seidel solver, so that a thousand parallel assemblies run in real time on one GPU. It also splits the task into pick, place, and screw subpolicies. FORGE~\cite{Noseworthy2025} adds force-guided exploration under pose uncertainty and demonstrates a multistage planetary-gear assembly that includes nut threading, and IndustReal~\cite{Tang2023industreal} transfers simulation-trained assembly policies to a physical arm. All three use a parallel-jaw gripper.

Screwdriving with hand tools has been modeled analytically and learned with physics-informed models~\cite{Tang2023,Tang2024screwdriving,Manyar2023}. With multi-fingered hands, unscrewing a bottle lid, a threaded joint released by turning, has been learned with two hands by sim-to-real \ac{RL}~\cite{Lin2024} and with one hand from teleoperated demonstrations with a visuomotor diffusion policy~\cite{Koczy2025}. Play2Perfect~\cite{Lum2026} pretrains a goal-conditioned manipulation policy through play and fine-tunes it for precise assembly, including screwing a rod-like furniture leg. This gives goal-directed screwing in a structured setting, but not for everyday threaded parts such as nuts. The closest prior work is DexScrew~\cite{Hsieh2026}, which uses a hand--arm setup comparable to the one here and replaces the thread geometry by a revolute-joint proxy to learn a rotational finger gait. The gait assists teleoperation and supports the collection of real demonstrations for tactile behavior cloning. Its policy spins the fastener without a target angle and without learned termination. Goal-directed threading over many grasp-and-release cycles, which this thesis addresses for several nut sizes, is left open by these works.

\subsection{Dexterous Manipulation with Learned Policies}\label{sec:related-dexterous}

A multi-fingered hand removes the need for task-specific tooling, but it brings high-dimensional control and complex contact dynamics with it~\cite{BicchiKumar2000,Weinberg2024}. Analytical models of motion and contact have rarely coped with the uncertainty of real environments, and the field has therefore turned to learning~\cite{Weinberg2024}. Model-free \ac{RL} in simulation learns in-hand reorientation of more than two thousand objects~\cite{Chen2021}. DeXtreme~\cite{Handa2024} transfers agile in-hand reorientation to a low-cost hand with a vision-based pose estimator, and DexPBT~\cite{Petrenko2023} learns regrasping and grasp-and-throw for hand--arm systems with population-based training. DextrAH-G~\cite{Lum2024} and DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025} grasp a wide range of objects with a hand--arm system from depth and RGB images, and SimToolReal~\cite{Kedia2026} manipulates tool-like objects to random goal poses and generalizes zero-shot to real tools. Without learning, finger gaits that pivot an object under contact-force control have been demonstrated~\cite{Xue2023}.

These works name the high-dimensional action space of the hand as a central challenge~\cite{Lum2024,Chen2023}. Most of them solve one skill per episode: a grasp, a goal pose of a tool, or a target orientation. In-hand reorientation chains goals and counts consecutive successes~\cite{Handa2024}, but the object stays in the hand. Threading additionally requires the hand to release the part and acquire it again while the bolt constrains it, and the grasp must hold under the turning load in between. This thesis narrows the solution space to one grasp strategy, a thumb--index pinch, and lets the learned policy control this pinch together with the arm (Section~\ref{sec:system}).

\subsection{Sim-to-Real Transfer for Contact-Rich Manipulation}\label{sec:related-sim2real}

Policies trained in simulation meet discrepancies in perception, dynamics, contact, and control when they are deployed on hardware, and the discrepancies grow with the dimensionality of the control problem and the amount of contact involved. Thread contact is an extreme case, because engaged threads produce thousands of simultaneous contacts~\cite{Narang2022}. The most common response is to make the policy robust enough for zero-shot transfer, by randomizing visual and physical parameters during training~\cite{Tobin2017,Handa2024}, by randomizing dynamics together with a force threshold~\cite{Noseworthy2025}, or by making the visual representation itself transferable~\cite{Wilcox2025}. A second family adapts a simulation-trained policy with real-world data, for instance by tuning the simulator to a few minutes of real data~\cite{Lin2025}, by learning a residual policy from on-robot corrections~\cite{Jiang2025transic}, or by continuing training on the robot~\cite{Luo2025,Huang2025}.

This thesis follows the first family, and RQ3 asks whether it suffices for threading. The student is trained on simulated data only, and transfer rests on four properties of the training setup. The camera model is calibrated against the physical cell and reproduced in simulation. The visual representation is a metric point cloud in the spirit of \ac{DP3}~\cite{Ze2024}, which is invariant to appearance up to depth noise. The rendered appearance is randomized in the style of DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025}. The Cartesian action is executed on the robot by a controller that reproduces the simulated control law. The approach requires no real-world data collection and no training on the robot.

\subsection{Long-Horizon Tasks and Policy Chaining}\label{sec:related-chaining}

A single policy for a long-horizon task faces two difficulties. Small control errors compound over the horizon~\cite{Ross2011}, and fine-tuning the policy on later subtasks can erase what it learned on earlier ones, the catastrophic forgetting problem~\cite{Kirkpatrick2017}. Larger networks, frozen or regularized parameters, and architectures with longer temporal memory such as recurrent networks and Transformers mitigate these effects but do not remove them. At large scale, a single policy can nevertheless be trained end to end. AlphaNIST~\cite{Zhang2026} trains policies from scratch with a sparse binary task reward and without demonstrations, progress shaping, or scripted primitives, and its policies complete ten assembly pairs in one simulated episode without a prescribed order.

The alternative is to decompose the task into skills with their own policies and to chain them. Each skill is then easier to learn, but the chain introduces a transition problem. The states in which one skill terminates may lie outside the initial-state distribution on which the next skill was trained, so the chain breaks at the boundary. Adversarial skill chaining~\cite{Lee2022} regularizes the terminal-state distribution of each skill toward the initial-state distribution of its successor. Sequential Dexterity~\cite{Chen2023} learns a transition-feasibility function with which adjacent dexterous policies are fine-tuned, switches between them autonomously, and recovers from failed transitions. A cyclic task sharpens the transition problem, because the same boundaries recur in every cycle while the task state advances, so every transition error has many chances to occur and its effect carries into the next cycle.

The method of this thesis decomposes only during data generation, where every phase has a simple specification, and unifies at deployment by distilling the chained demonstrations into one policy. The transitions are then part of what the student learns rather than a problem to be solved between separate policies. RQ1 tests whether this unified student sustains consecutive cycles better than a single-cycle policy that is invoked repeatedly.

\subsection{Teacher--Student and Visuomotor Distillation}\label{sec:related-distillation}

Learning by Cheating~\cite{Chen2020} established the route from a policy that is trained with privileged simulator state to a deployable policy that acts from onboard sensing. In dexterous manipulation, DextrAH-G~\cite{Lum2024} and DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025} distill a privileged \ac{RL} grasping teacher into depth- and RGB-based students with online \ac{DAgger}. UniGraspTransformer~\cite{Wang2025} consolidates many object-specific teachers into one student, and Lin et al.~\cite{Lin2025} learn separate single-task \ac{RL} policies on a humanoid and distill them into one generalist vision-based policy. The author's semester thesis~\cite{Oertel2026} applied this recipe to a 23-DoF hand--arm system and compared behavior cloning, \ac{DAgger}, and SafeDAgger.

For long-horizon behavior, two further ingredients matter. Predicting a sequence of future actions instead of a single action improves temporal consistency. \Ac{ACT}~\cite{Zhao2023} predicts chunks with a Transformer and blends overlapping chunks by temporal ensembling. Representing the action distribution generatively lets a policy keep several valid behaviors apart instead of averaging them. Diffusion Policy~\cite{Chi2024} models multimodal action sequences by iterative denoising, \ac{DP3}~\cite{Ze2024} conditions it on compact point-cloud features, and flow matching~\cite{Lipman2023} learns a continuous vector field that generates actions by integrating an \ac{ODE} in few steps, as the vision-language-action model $\pi_0$~\cite{Black2026} does. The student of this thesis combines a point-cloud encoder, action chunking with temporal ensembling, and a flow-matching head, because its training data mix nominal teacher actions with the teacher's recoveries from disturbances.

\subsection{Summary and Research Gap}\label{sec:related-gap}

The related work addresses each difficulty of threading separately. Simulation-based assembly learning makes thread contact tractable and transfers to hardware, but with a parallel-jaw gripper and a fastener that is already held~\cite{Narang2022,Noseworthy2025,Tang2023industreal}. Learned dexterous threading and screwing covers a spinning gait for an engaged fastener~\cite{Hsieh2026} and goal-conditioned screwing of a furniture leg~\cite{Lum2026}. Policy chaining reaches long horizons at the cost of transitions between separately deployed policies~\cite{Lee2022,Chen2023}, and teacher--student distillation yields deployable visuomotor policies for single skills~\cite{Lum2024,Singh2025}. No prior work threads a nut over many cycles with learned grasping and re-grasping on a dexterous hand. Chapter~\ref{ch:method} closes this gap by combining the concepts of Section~\ref{sec:theory}: a simplified thread model makes \ac{RL} in simulation feasible, a privileged teacher learns the pinch-grasp turn, scripted transitions chain it into multi-cycle demonstrations, and one flow-matching student distills these demonstrations for zero-shot deployment.
