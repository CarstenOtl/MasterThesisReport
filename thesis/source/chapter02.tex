\section{Theoretical Background}\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Simulation of Threaded Contact}\label{sec:thread-background}

A nut on a bolt is a fastening joint whose threads are standardized, for example in the ISO metric M series~\cite{Budynas2020,Niemann2019}. The thread converts a large circumferential motion into a small axial motion, and a small circumferential force into a large axial force~\cite{Niemann2019}. The quantity that links rotation and axial travel is the pitch $\rho$, the distance between adjacent threads~\cite{Budynas2020}. For a single-start thread, adjacent threads are successive turns of one helix. A nut that turns by an angle $\Delta\theta$ about the bolt axis therefore advances along the axis by $\rho\,\Delta\theta/(2\pi)$, that is, by one pitch per revolution.
% TODO(cite): lead equals pitch for a single-start thread (Shigley Ch. 8 thread definitions); verify the passage before citing Budynas2020 for it.
Because a partial turn advances the nut by only a fraction of a pitch, the axial position at which a hand must grasp the nut changes with every turn. This thesis addresses turning the nut along the thread, including tightening and securing the joint. It does not address the thread start, in which the first turns of the nut and the bolt meet and lock onto the helical path. Every episode begins with the nut already engaged on the bolt.

Figure~\ref{fig:thread-profile}(a) shows the basic profile of an ISO metric thread in an axial section. The flanks of nut and bolt enclose an angle of $60^{\circ}$ and lie on a fundamental triangle of height $H=\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\rho$, whose tips are truncated by $H/8$ and $H/4$ at the crests and roots. The profile defines the major, pitch, and minor diameters $d$, $d_2$, and $d_1$ of the bolt and the corresponding diameters $D$, $D_2$, and $D_1$ of the nut~\cite{Budynas2020,Niemann2019}. Real parts deviate from the basic profile within tolerance zones. For the common fit 6H/6g in Figure~\ref{fig:thread-profile}(b), the nut zone starts at the basic profile and the bolt zone lies below it by the fundamental deviation $|es|$, so that a mating pair always has a small clearance between its flanks.

The most faithful way to simulate a thread is to model nut and bolt as independent rigid bodies, each with its own mass, inertia, material, and friction parameters, and to let the physics engine resolve the contact between their thread flanks. Real threads have a clearance between their flanks, so the nut is not confined to a pure helical motion. Only a model that simulates the collisions between the threads reproduces the phases of a real fastening, such as initial mating and rundown, and its failures, such as cross-threading and jamming~\cite{Narang2022}. An engaged nut touches the bolt along the flanks of every engaged thread turn. A rigid-body simulator detects such contacts between collision geometries that approximate the true shapes of the bodies, and the choice of approximation trades fidelity against computational cost~\cite{Narang2022}. A single convex hull is the cheapest geometry, but it fills every concavity, so the thread grooves of a nut and a bolt disappear. A convex decomposition approximates the shape by several convex hulls and can represent concavities. On complex geometry, however, it can introduce spatial artifacts, generate contact normals that point in the wrong direction and let parts snag, and the number of collision pairs to test grows quadratically with the number of hulls~\cite{Narang2022}. A triangle mesh represents the geometry almost exactly and gives the highest fidelity, but it is also the most computationally expensive. A detailed nut-and-bolt mesh consists of 10\,000 to 50\,000 triangles, the number of collision pairs grows quadratically with the number of triangles, and penetrations are hard to resolve because triangles have no volume~\cite{Narang2022}. For any of these geometries, the simulator represents the contact by a set of contact points, and the helical flanks of a nut and a bolt produce thousands of them at once~\cite{Narang2022}. Resolving these contacts in every physics step is the cost that makes thread simulation expensive. Factory~\cite{Narang2022} reduces it with signed-distance-field collision detection, contact reduction, and a Gauss--Seidel solver, and simulates 1000 nut-and-bolt assemblies in real time on one GPU. Even so, the cost is paid in every parallel environment, and the fidelity of the contact depends on how the engine resolves it.

GPU-parallel engines such as PhysX and MuJoCo resolve contact by tolerating a small overlap between bodies and pushing them apart in the following steps, with a separating velocity that grows with the overlap and is capped.
% TODO(cite): penetration-based contact resolution in PhysX and MuJoCo (soft or penalty-like contact, depenetration velocity, contact offsets).
Contact is therefore never strictly rigid. The overlap is harmless for large bodies, but the flanks and crests of a thread with a pitch of a few millimeters are of the same order as the tolerated overlap. A nut that is pushed along the bolt can then sink through a flank and be pushed into the next groove, which advances it by one pitch without a turn. A learning policy that is rewarded for axial progress finds this shortcut.

Learning-based methods need many simulated samples, and resolving the thread contact in every one of them is expensive. Many learning-based works on fastening therefore do not simulate the thread contact and replace it by a cheaper joint between the two parts~\cite{Hsieh2026}. DexScrew~\cite{Hsieh2026} attaches the nut to a fixed base through a revolute joint, so the policy learns to spin the nut in place. The nut never advances along the bolt in simulation, and the downward motion that the thread demands enters only through real-world demonstrations. Twisting Lids~\cite{Lin2024} connects lid and bottle by a continuous revolute joint and generates the static friction of a closed lid with a brake link that presses against the lid. For screwdriving, the screw has been simulated as a body that rotates about its axis against a stiffness that raises the resisting torque, with its other five degrees of freedom fixed~\cite{Tang2024screwdriving}. Such proxies are cheap and stable, and their joint friction or stiffness can be randomized like any other simulation parameter. They drop the axial travel of the fastener, however, and with it the helical constraint, which is the essence of a thread. A policy trained on them only learns to rotate the fastener. On a real thread, the same rotation advances the nut along the bolt, so the hand has to follow a helical path that couples rotation and axial travel. A behavior that does not follow this path is of little use for fastening. Play2Perfect~\cite{Lum2026}, in contrast, keeps the thread geometry and limits the cost by using SDF collision only on the mating regions. Between these extremes lies a model that keeps the helix but drops the thread contact. A revolute and a prismatic joint are coupled through the pitch, so that the nut advances by one pitch per revolution and axial progress can only be earned by turning. Section~\ref{sec:thread-model} shows the shortcut in detail and uses such a coupling, while the finger--nut contacts remain simulated.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for the Privileged Teacher Policy}\label{sec:rl-background}

In \ac{RL}, an agent learns a behavior from interaction and a scalar reward~\cite{Sutton2018,Kober2013}. The interaction is formalized as a \ac{MDP} $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma)$ with state space $\mathcal{X}$, action space $\mathcal{A}$, transition kernel $P(\vec{x}_{t+1}\mid \vec{x}_t,\vec{a}_t)$, reward $r(\vec{x}_t,\vec{a}_t)$, and discount factor $\gamma\in[0,1)$~\cite{Sutton2018}. A stochastic policy $\pi_\theta(\vec{a}\mid\vec{x})$, here a neural network with parameters $\theta$ that outputs a Gaussian distribution over actions, is trained to maximize the expected discounted return
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rl-objective}
  J(\pi_\theta)=\E_{\tau\sim p_{\pi_\theta}}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r_t\right]
\end{equation}
over trajectories $\tau=(\vec{x}_0,\vec{a}_0,r_0,\vec{x}_1,\dots)$. A reward that lies $t$ steps in the future is weighted by $\gamma^{t}$, so rewards beyond roughly $1/(1-\gamma)$ steps contribute little.

Policy-gradient methods ascend $J$ with gradients estimated from sampled trajectories~\cite{Williams1992,Sutton2018}. Actor--critic methods reduce the variance of this estimate with a learned value network $V_\phi$, the critic, that predicts the expected return from a state. The critic turns each sampled action into an advantage estimate $\hat{A}_t$, which is positive when the action turned out better than expected, here computed with \ac{GAE}~\cite{Schulman2016}. \Acf{PPO}~\cite{Schulman2017} is the actor--critic algorithm used in this thesis. It reuses each batch of on-policy data for several epochs of minibatch updates and keeps the updated policy close to the policy $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ that collected the data by maximizing the clipped surrogate objective
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppo}
  L^{\mathrm{clip}}(\theta) = \E_t\!\left[\min\!\left(\eta_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\; \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\eta_t(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)\hat{A}_t\right)\right],
  \qquad \eta_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(\vec{a}_t\mid\vec{x}_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\vec{a}_t\mid\vec{x}_t)},
\end{equation}
with the clipping parameter $\epsilon$. Once the probability ratio $\eta_t$ leaves $[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$ in the direction that the advantage favors, the objective stops growing. On-policy learning needs many samples, and GPU-parallel simulators supply them by stepping thousands of environments at once. On such simulators, \ac{RL} has trained in-hand reorientation that transferred to hardware~\cite{Handa2024} and re-grasping and throwing with simulated hand--arm systems~\cite{Petrenko2023}.

The critic is needed only during training, so it may receive information that the actor will not have at deployment. In an asymmetric actor--critic~\cite{Pinto2017}, the critic observes the full, noise-free simulator state, while the actor acts only on the observations available to it. The teacher of this thesis goes one step further: its actor also observes the nut pose from the simulator, which no sensor on the physical system provides. Such a privileged policy is not deployable, but it is a well-informed expert, and Section~\ref{sec:il-background} explains how its behavior is transferred to a policy that acts on sensor data.

The reward is the only specification of the task that the agent receives, and its design largely decides whether learning succeeds~\cite{Kober2013}. A sparse reward that pays only on success rarely produces a learning signal in a high-dimensional action space, so dense shaping terms pay for intermediate progress. Only shaping terms of the potential-based form $\gamma\Phi(\vec{x}_{t+1})-\Phi(\vec{x}_t)$ are guaranteed to leave the optimal policy unchanged~\cite{Ng1999}. Other terms may be exploited, because the agent optimizes the reward as written rather than the task as intended. A term that pays for every reduction of a distance, for example, pays again each time the agent moves away and comes back. In simulation the agent can also exploit inaccuracies of the physics, as the interpenetration through the thread flanks of Section~\ref{sec:thread-background} illustrates.

\ac{RL} thus produces a policy from a reward alone, at the cost of many simulated trials and a carefully shaped reward. When an expert policy is available, a policy can instead be learned by supervised imitation, which the next subsection introduces.

\subsection{Imitation Learning and Policy Distillation}\label{sec:il-background}

The privileged teacher of Section~\ref{sec:rl-background} cannot be deployed, because it observes state that no sensor provides. A deployable policy must act on sensor observations, and training it with \ac{RL} directly from these observations would repeat the costly exploration on a much harder input. Teacher--student distillation instead splits the problem into learning to act, which \ac{RL} solves on the privileged state, and learning to perceive, which is posed as supervised imitation of the teacher~\cite{Chen2020,Lum2024}. A student that acts on sensor observations is trained to reproduce the teacher's actions and thereby inherits a behavior that was found on the privileged state without ever observing that state. Distillation changes what the policy observes and leaves its action unchanged. The student imitates the teacher's action in the teacher's action space, and the same controller executes it. Policy distillation is thus \ac{IL} with a learned policy as the expert.

\Ac{IL} is the problem of learning a policy from the demonstrations of an expert rather than from a reward. \Ac{IL} methods differ in how they use the demonstrations. The simplest method, \ac{BC}, reduces \ac{IL} to supervised learning~\cite{Ross2011}. It treats every demonstrated pair of observation and expert action as a labeled training example and fits the learned policy, called the student, to these labels. Given a dataset $\Dset=\{(\obs_t,\act^{*}_t)\}$, \ac{BC} solves the regression problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bc}
  \theta^{*}=\argmin_\theta \E_{(\obs,\act^{*})\sim\Dset}\!\left[\ell\!\left(\piS(\obs),\act^{*}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
with a per-sample loss $\ell$ such as the squared error. \ac{BC} is trained on the states that the expert visits but evaluated on the states that the student visits. Small prediction errors move the student into states that are not covered by the demonstrations, and the errors compound. Ross et al.~\cite{Ross2011} show that the expected cost of a \ac{BC} policy with per-step error $\varepsilon$ can grow as $O(T^2\varepsilon)$ over a horizon $T$. For a cyclic task, small deviations in grasp height or nut angle carry over from one cycle into the next.

\Ac{DAgger}~\cite{Ross2011} counters this by querying the expert on the states that the student itself visits. The environment is driven by a mixture that executes the expert's action with probability $\beta$ and the student's action otherwise, the expert labels every visited state, and the student is retrained on the aggregated dataset. The training data thereby come to cover the states that the student itself reaches, including the states it drifts into after its own mistakes, and the expert's labels teach it how to return from them. The training error is then measured on the student's own state distribution and no longer degrades when the student leaves the demonstrations. If the expert can recover from a single deviation within a few steps, the expected cost of the student grows only linearly in the horizon, $O(T\varepsilon)$, instead of quadratically~\cite{Ross2011}. \ac{DAgger} requires an interactive expert that can label the states the student visits, which are not known in advance. A policy in simulation satisfies this requirement, whereas a fixed set of recorded demonstrations does not.

The teacher can be distilled offline, by \ac{BC} on a fixed dataset of its rollouts, or online, by \ac{DAgger} with the teacher labeling the states that the student visits. The offline variant trains like any supervised model and needs no further simulation, but it inherits the compounding error of \ac{BC}. The variation in the recorded rollouts, for example recoveries from disturbances applied during collection, decides how far the student's states stay inside the demonstrated distribution. This thesis distills offline (Section~\ref{sec:distillation}).

In the formulation above, the student is a regression policy. A network maps the current observation to a single action and is queried at every control step. This formulation has two shortcomings. First, demonstrations often contain several valid actions for the same observation, for example a nominal motion in one demonstration and a recovery from a disturbance in another. The squared-error loss of \eqref{eq:bc} then fits the average of these actions, and the average is often not a useful action at all. If the expert sometimes passes an obstacle on the left and sometimes on the right, a regression policy learns to drive straight into it~\cite{Chi2024}. Second, the student makes one decision per control step. Over a task of $T$ steps it makes $T$ decisions, each of which can add to the compounding error above, and consecutive decisions are made independently, so the student can switch between demonstrated behaviors from one step to the next~\cite{Zhao2023,Chi2024}. Both shortcomings concern what the student outputs rather than what it observes. They motivate the generative action heads and the action chunking of the next subsection.

\subsection{Conditional Flow Matching}\label{sec:generative-background}

Generative models learn a probability distribution from a set of samples and produce new samples from it. They were developed for the synthesis of data such as images, where diffusion models reach a high sample quality~\cite{Ho2020,Rombach2022}, and robot learning adopted them as action heads to overcome the shortcomings of regression policies~\cite{Chi2024}. A generative action head learns the conditional distribution $p(\act\mid\obs)$ of the demonstrated actions given the observation and draws an action from it at run time. When the demonstrations contain several valid actions for the same observation, a sample from the generative head takes one of them, whereas a regression head returns their average~\cite{Chi2024}. The Gaussian policy of Section~\ref{sec:rl-background} also represents only a single mode.

Generative heads are usually combined with action chunking~\cite{Zhao2023,Chi2024}. An action-chunking policy predicts a sequence of $H$ future actions, the action chunk $[\act_t,\dots,\act_{t+H-1}]$, from the observation $\obs_t$, and executes the first $H_{\mathrm{e}}\leq H$ of them before it predicts again~\cite{Zhao2023}.\label{sec:chunking-background} The policy then makes only $T/H_{\mathrm{e}}$ decisions, so the effective horizon of the task shrinks by the factor $H_{\mathrm{e}}$ and fewer errors can compound~\cite{Zhao2023}. A chunk also covers behavior that a single observation does not disambiguate, such as a pause in the demonstration and the motion that follows it, and it is sampled as a whole, so consecutive actions follow one behavior rather than switching between several~\cite{Zhao2023,Chi2024}. The cost of chunking is reactivity. While the actions of a chunk execute, the policy does not observe their effect and cannot respond to a disturbance before its next query. Temporal ensembling restores feedback: the policy is queried at every step and executes an exponentially weighted average of all predictions for the current step from the chunks that are still valid~\cite{Zhao2023}.

Two families of generative action heads are common in robot learning, diffusion and flow matching. Both start from Gaussian noise and transform it step by step into an action, but they learn this transformation differently.

Diffusion models~\cite{Ho2020} learn to remove noise, and Diffusion Policy~\cite{Chi2024} applies them to action chunks. During training, Gaussian noise is added to a demonstrated action, and a network that is conditioned on the observation predicts the added noise. The magnitude of the noise depends on a randomly drawn iteration index through the noise schedule, a hand-designed sequence of noise variances that fixes how much noise each iteration adds or removes. At run time, sampling starts from pure noise and applies the network over a fixed number of denoising iterations, each of which removes part of the predicted noise. The number of iterations sets the inference cost. Diffusion Policy predicts action chunks and executes several actions of a chunk before it predicts again, and \ac{DP3}~\cite{Ze2024} conditions the same head on compact point-cloud features and the robot pose.

Flow matching~\cite{Lipman2023,Lipman2024} learns the transformation from noise to action as a velocity field. Each noise sample can be pictured as a particle that moves during a flow time $\lambda$ from 0 to 1. A network $\vec{v}_\theta(\act,\lambda\mid\obs)$ predicts the velocity of the particle at position $\act$ and time $\lambda$, and integrating this velocity from a noise sample $\act^{0}$ at $\lambda=0$ yields an action $\act^{1}$ at $\lambda=1$. The velocity field that maps the noise distribution onto the demonstrated actions is unknown, but a training target can be built from pairs of a noise sample and a demonstrated action. The simplest construction connects the two by a straight line,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fm-line}
  \act^{\lambda}=(1-\lambda)\act^{0}+\lambda\act^{1},\qquad \act^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\mat{I}),
\end{equation}
along which the velocity is the constant $\act^{1}-\act^{0}$. Training regresses the network onto this velocity at random points of random lines,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fm-loss}
  L(\theta)=\E_{(\obs,\act^{1})\sim\Dset,\;\act^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\mat{I}),\;\lambda\sim U[0,1]}\left\|\vec{v}_\theta(\act^{\lambda},\lambda\mid\obs)-\left(\act^{1}-\act^{0}\right)\right\|_2^{2}.
\end{equation}
Many lines pass through the same point, so the network learns the average of their velocities. Lipman et al.~\cite{Lipman2023} show that this average is exactly the unknown velocity field, so the simple loss \eqref{eq:fm-loss} trains the correct model. At run time, an action is generated by drawing $\act^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\vec{0},\mat{I})$ and following the learned field with a few explicit Euler steps $\act^{\lambda+\Delta\lambda}=\act^{\lambda}+\Delta\lambda\,\vec{v}_\theta(\act^{\lambda},\lambda\mid\obs)$.

Diffusion and flow matching are closely related. A diffusion model corresponds to flow matching with a particular Gaussian path between noise and data, whose trajectories are curved~\cite{Lipman2023}. Figure~\ref{fig:diffusion-vs-fm} contrasts the two. The straight-line path of \eqref{eq:fm-line} gives flow matching three advantages over a diffusion policy. Training is simpler, because the regression target is the difference of two samples and the path fixes how noise and data are mixed over the flow time, so no noise schedule has to be designed. Sampling is faster, because a straight path can be followed with a few large integration steps, whereas a curved path needs many small ones. On image generation, flow matching reaches the sample quality of diffusion with fewer network evaluations~\cite{Lipman2023}. Sampling is also the deterministic integration of an \ac{ODE} from a given noise sample, so the number of integration steps can be chosen at run time, which trades accuracy against inference time without retraining. Fast sampling matters for robot control, because a new action chunk must be ready within the control period. The vision-language-action model $\pi_0$, for example, uses flow matching to generate action chunks for control at up to 50\,Hz~\cite{Black2026}.

In a policy, the flow-matching action head sits on top of a backbone. The backbone encodes the observation, for example camera images and the robot state, into a condition vector $\cond$, which takes the place of $\obs$ in \eqref{eq:fm-loss}. The head is the velocity network $\vfield(\act^{\lambda},\lambda\mid\cond)$. It receives the noisy action or action chunk $\act^{\lambda}$, the flow time $\lambda$, and the condition, and it outputs a velocity of the same dimension as the action. The flow time is a scalar and is usually embedded with sinusoidal features before it enters the network~\cite{Black2026}. The velocity network is typically an \ac{MLP} or a Transformer. The condition enters it by concatenation with the input, by a feature-wise scaling and shifting of the hidden layers~\cite{Chi2024}, or as additional tokens that the action tokens attend to~\cite{Black2026}. The backbone is evaluated once per generated action chunk, the head once per integration step. Each additional step therefore costs only one evaluation of the head. Section~\ref{sec:distillation} describes the backbone and the head of the student in this thesis.

Section~\ref{sec:related-dexterous} reviews dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered hands (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}). Sections~\ref{sec:related-chaining} and~\ref{sec:related-visuomotor} review policy chaining and the distillation of privileged teachers into visuomotor policies, the two routes to the long-horizon difficulty (Section~\ref{sec:difficulty-horizon}), and Section~\ref{sec:related-visuomotor} also reviews the visual representations from which such policies act. Section~\ref{sec:related-gap} states the gap.

\subsection{Learning-Based Robotic Assembly and Fastening}\label{sec:related-assembly}

Robotic assembly, for example peg-in-hole insertion, gear meshing, connector mating, and nut--bolt fastening, requires contact-rich interaction with high precision and accuracy~\cite{Tang2023industreal}. In mass production, dedicated fixtures position the parts precisely and thereby reduce the uncertainty of the hole pose~\cite{Manyar2023}. Learning-based methods train assembly policies in simulation. Factory~\cite{Narang2022} makes nut-and-bolt contact tractable in simulation (Section~\ref{sec:thread-background}) and learns nut-and-bolt assembly with \ac{RL} through separate pick, place, and screw subpolicies. FORGE~\cite{Noseworthy2025} trains force-guided exploration under pose uncertainty and demonstrates on a real robot a multistage planetary-gear assembly that includes nut threading, and IndustReal~\cite{Tang2023industreal} transfers simulation-trained assembly policies to a physical arm. All three use a parallel-jaw gripper.

Screwdriving with a manual screwdriver has been addressed with analytical models and force control, first with the tool rigidly attached to a robot arm~\cite{Tang2023} and then with the tool held by a Shadow Hand~\cite{Tang2024screwdriving}. For an industrial screwdriving tool on an impedance-controlled arm, a physics-informed learned model describes the motion of the screw tip~\cite{Manyar2023}. With multi-fingered hands, twisting the lids off bottle-like objects has been learned with two hands by sim-to-real \ac{RL}~\cite{Lin2024}, and unscrewing a bottle lid with one hand has been learned from teleoperated demonstrations with a visuomotor diffusion policy~\cite{Koczy2025}. Play2Perfect~\cite{Lum2026} pretrains a goal-conditioned dexterous manipulation policy through play and fine-tunes it for precise assembly, including screwing a furniture leg into a table fixture. It thus covers goal-directed screwing of a furniture leg, but not of small fasteners such as nuts. The only reviewed work that fastens nuts with a dexterous hand is DexScrew~\cite{Hsieh2026}. It mounts a 12-DoF hand on a robot arm and replaces the thread geometry by a revolute-joint proxy to learn a rotational finger gait with \ac{RL}. The gait assists teleoperation and supports the collection of real demonstrations for tactile behavior cloning. The \ac{RL} policy is rewarded for the angular velocity of the fastener, its episodes end on failure or after a fixed time limit, and the nut is already installed on the bolt. Neither the reward nor the termination refers to a target angle. Goal-directed threading over many grasp-and-release cycles, which this thesis addresses, is left open by these works.

\subsection{Dexterous Manipulation with Multi-Fingered Hands}\label{sec:related-dexterous}

A multi-fingered hand is a general-purpose alternative to task-specific tooling, but it brings high-dimensional control and complex contact dynamics with it~\cite{BicchiKumar2000,Weinberg2024}. Weinberg et al.~\cite{Weinberg2024} conclude in their survey that analytical models of motion and contact have failed to provide practical solutions for in-hand manipulation, because they cannot cope with complex environments and uncertainty, and that research has therefore shifted to learning. Model-free \ac{RL} in simulation learns in-hand reorientation of more than two thousand objects~\cite{Chen2021}. DeXtreme~\cite{Handa2024} transfers agile in-hand reorientation from simulation to a comparatively affordable Allegro hand with a vision-based pose estimator, and DexPBT~\cite{Petrenko2023} learns re-grasping and grasp-and-throw for simulated hand--arm systems with population-based training. DextrAH-G~\cite{Lum2024} and DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025} grasp a wide range of objects with a hand--arm system from depth and RGB images, respectively, and SimToolReal~\cite{Kedia2026} manipulates tool-like objects to random goal poses and generalizes zero-shot to real tools. Without learning, an arm-driven hand has executed a finger gait on a tool whose tip pivots on a supporting plane, using hybrid position/impedance control with reference finger forces~\cite{Xue2023}.

Learning-based works on dexterous manipulation name the high-dimensional action space of the hand or of the hand--arm system as a main difficulty~\cite{Chen2021,Lum2024,Chen2023}. Most of the works above learn one skill, such as grasping, reorienting an object, or moving a tool to goal poses. In-hand reorientation chains goals and counts consecutive successes~\cite{Handa2024}, but the object stays in the hand. Threading additionally requires the hand to release the part and acquire it again while the bolt constrains it, and the grasp must hold under the turning load in between. This thesis narrows the solution space to one grasp strategy, a thumb--index pinch, and lets the learned policy control this pinch together with the arm (Section~\ref{sec:teacher}).

\subsection{Policy Chaining in Long-Horizon Tasks}\label{sec:related-chaining}

A long-horizon task can be learned by one policy over the whole horizon or decomposed into skills, and each route has its own difficulties. One policy trained with \ac{RL} needs a reward that covers every phase, and it has to assign the outcome of a late phase to decisions made many steps earlier. AlphaNIST~\cite{Zhang2026} shows in simulation that a sparse binary task reward can suffice. It trains one policy from scratch on the 20 assembly tasks of the NIST Assembly Task Board~\#1, which require precise, contact-rich control of small, tight-fitting parts, and reaches 98.2\,\% overall training success without demonstrations, progress shaping, or scripted primitives. The same framework trains policies that complete ten assembly pairs in one episode without a prescribed order, rewarded only when all ten pairs are complete. Imitation learning avoids designing a reward for the whole horizon, because it learns from expert demonstrations instead. A single imitation policy for the whole task, however, requires demonstrations of the whole task, and teleoperated demonstrations of forceful dexterous manipulation are hard to collect~\cite{Kedia2026}.

Decomposing the task into skills shortens the horizon that each skill covers, but it raises the problems of forgetting and of transitions between skills. If one network learns the skills one after another, fine-tuning it on later skills can erase what it learned on earlier ones. This catastrophic forgetting affects supervised and reinforcement learning alike~\cite{Kirkpatrick2017}. Separate policies per skill avoid forgetting, but chaining them introduces a transition problem. The states in which one skill terminates may lie outside the initial-state distribution on which the next skill was trained, and the next skill can then fail~\cite{Lee2022}. Adversarial skill chaining~\cite{Lee2022} regularizes the terminal-state distribution of each skill toward the initial-state distribution of its successor. Sequential Dexterity~\cite{Chen2023} learns a transition-feasibility function that fine-tunes each sub-policy toward states from which its successors succeed, and it uses the same function to switch between sub-policies autonomously, which lets it recover from failures and skip redundant stages. A cyclic task sharpens the transition problem. The same boundaries recur in every cycle while the task state advances, so a task of many cycles crosses each boundary many times, and an error at one crossing carries into the following cycle.

Generative action heads make it practical for one imitation policy to cover a multi-stage task. Such a head samples one of several demonstrated actions for similar observations instead of their average (Section~\ref{sec:generative-background}). The flow-matching model $\pi_0$~\cite{Black2026} is trained on data from several dexterous robot platforms and applied to multi-stage tasks such as laundry folding, table cleaning, and box assembly. Its demonstrations are collected by teleoperation~\cite{Black2026}, which is the kind of data that is hard to obtain for forceful dexterous manipulation.

The method of this thesis decomposes the task into separately controlled phases only during data generation. The privileged \ac{RL} teacher executes the pinch-grasp turn, scripted controllers execute release and return, and together they generate complete multi-cycle demonstrations in simulation without teleoperation (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}). One visuomotor student with a flow-matching head is distilled from these demonstrations and deployed. It learns all phases in one training run, so no phase is fine-tuned after another and catastrophic forgetting does not arise. The phase enters the student as a conditioning signal derived from physical events, and the student predicts the transition actions itself (Section~\ref{sec:distillation}). The student therefore learns the transitions together with the phases. RQ1 tests whether this unified student sustains consecutive cycles better than a single-cycle policy that is invoked repeatedly.

\subsection{Visuomotor Policies and Privileged-Teacher Distillation}\label{sec:related-visuomotor}

A visuomotor policy maps raw sensor observations, such as camera images, depth images, or point clouds, together with the proprioceptive state of the robot directly to motor commands. It does not rely on a separate state-estimation stage that first recovers the object poses. A policy trained in simulation can instead act on privileged information, such as exact object poses and contact forces, that the simulator provides but that is not measured on the real robot. A common way to obtain a deployable visuomotor policy is therefore to train a privileged teacher first and then distill it into a student that acts from sensor observations alone (Section~\ref{sec:il-background}). This subsection reviews such distillation and the visual representations from which the student can act.

Learning by Cheating~\cite{Chen2020} trains a privileged agent on ground-truth simulator state by imitating an expert and then uses it to supervise a sensorimotor agent that acts from a forward-facing camera image, the vehicle speed, and a high-level command. In dexterous manipulation, DextrAH-G~\cite{Lum2024} and DextrAH-RGB~\cite{Singh2025} distill a privileged \ac{RL} grasping teacher into a depth-based and an RGB-based student, respectively, with online \ac{DAgger}. UniGraspTransformer~\cite{Wang2025} distills 3200 object-specific \ac{RL} teachers into one Transformer student, and Lin et al.~\cite{Lin2025} train separate per-object \ac{RL} specialists on a humanoid and distill their successful rollouts offline into one generalist policy per task that acts from egocentric depth, the object position, and the joint states. All of these distill single skills whose episodes end when the object is grasped, reoriented, or handed over.

A visuomotor policy must extract the task-relevant geometry from camera data, in threading the pose of the nut relative to the hand. Two families of encoders, and combinations of them, are used for this. Image-space encoders apply a convolutional backbone such as ResNet-18~\cite{He2016} to the RGB image, possibly with depth or other per-pixel channels added, and pool the feature map into a vector. They can start from ImageNet-pretrained weights, but from RGB alone they have to infer metric geometry from appearance, and when they are trained on rendered images they must be made robust to texture and lighting, for example by visual domain randomization~\cite{Tobin2017}. Point-cloud encoders back-project the depth image through the calibrated camera model into a set of 3D points, optionally attach the pixel color, and encode the set with a shared point network followed by global max pooling in the spirit of PointNet~\cite{Qi2017}. The \ac{DP3} encoder~\cite{Ze2024} is such a compact encoder, a three-layer \ac{MLP} with max pooling and a projection head, and it uses the point coordinates without color. In real-robot tests with one trial per condition, \ac{DP3} generalizes to unseen object colors and slightly changed camera views on which image- and depth-image-based Diffusion Policy~\cite{Chi2024} fail. The authors attribute the robustness to appearance to the omission of color and the spatial generalization to the 3D representation~\cite{Ze2024}. Adapt3R~\cite{Wilcox2025} combines the two families. Dense 2D features from a pretrained backbone are localized in 3D by the depth map and expressed in the end-effector frame before attention pooling. In simulation, this lets a policy transfer zero-shot to unseen robot arms and camera viewpoints, although its success rate on unseen arms is lower than on the training arm.

\subsection{Research Gap}\label{sec:related-gap}

The related work addresses each difficulty of threading separately. Simulation-based assembly learning makes thread contact tractable~\cite{Narang2022} and transfers insertion and nut threading to hardware~\cite{Tang2023industreal,Noseworthy2025}, but with a parallel-jaw gripper, a part that is already held when contact begins, and no re-grasping during threading~\cite{Narang2022,Noseworthy2025}. Learned dexterous screwing covers a spinning gait for an installed nut~\cite{Hsieh2026} and goal-conditioned screwing of a furniture leg~\cite{Lum2026}. Policy chaining reaches long horizons at the cost of transitions between separately trained policies~\cite{Lee2022,Chen2023}, and teacher--student distillation yields deployable visuomotor policies for single skills~\cite{Lum2024,Singh2025}. None of the reviewed works threads a nut over many grasp-and-release cycles with a dexterous hand, and none deploys such a policy trained on simulated data alone. Chapter~\ref{ch:method} addresses this gap by combining the concepts of Section~\ref{sec:theory}: a simplified thread model makes \ac{RL} in simulation feasible, a privileged teacher learns the pinch-grasp turn, scripted transitions chain it into multi-cycle demonstrations, and one flow-matching student distills these demonstrations for zero-shot deployment.
